## 4.1 The classical Malthusian structure: a world where food binds population

In arguments about low fertility and stagnant growth in the advanced economies, two explanations turn up as standard: a change in values, and a failure of policy. People came to prize careers and free time and to regard children as a cost. Or governments run weak family policy, with inadequate childcare, housing and education. Both accounts are partly right. But there is a question I cannot get past. Are values and policy really causes? Or do they sit on the results side, determined by a deeper macro structure?

In the world Malthus analysed the constraint variable was unambiguous. It was food production. Where land and agricultural technique have a ceiling, a rising population means falling grain per head, and famine, epidemic, war, later marriage and contraception push the population back down again. Population is permanently tethered to the neighbourhood of the food constraint. That is the classical Malthusian trap. Nitrogen fixation by the Haber-Bosch process in the early twentieth century changed that structure dramatically. The spread of artificial fertiliser lifted grain yields enormously, and in the advanced countries a shortage of total calories ceased, at the macro level at least, to be the binding constraint.

What matters here is that Malthus's schema was not itself wrong. What was wrong was the premise that agriculture would go on being the bottleneck forever. The structure, that population is held down by the dominant physical constraint, holds. The bottleneck simply moved out of agriculture into another domain.

## 4.2 The modern "food" is the household's economic capacity, and it has Baumol's disease

Nobody in an advanced country today gives "there is nothing to eat" as the reason for not having children. And yet fertility sits pinned at historic lows. What I want to put forward here is the following hypothesis.

> **The modern "food" is, in substance, the household's economic capacity. But the household's economic capacity and its constraint are themselves derived variables, fixed by the macro energy structure and the level of technology.**

Households judge whether a life is possible from the level of wages and from the price system. The point is that the price system is itself a function of the energy constraint and of technological structure. The price of a good or a service is set, roughly, by energy cost, manufacturing cost (including the extraction, processing and transport of materials), transport cost, computing cost, and the labour cost that has not been automated away. Energy is the base cost common to almost all production, transport and computation, and labour cost is heavily affected by energy and by the level of technology, which is to say the degree of automation. Hence,

<p class="equation">Price vector of goods and services p = f(energy price P_E, degree of automation A)</p>
The "labour cost that has not been automated away" has a proper name in economics. It is Baumol's cost disease.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Wage rises in the sector where productivity grows, manufacturing, spill over into the labour-intensive sectors where productivity structurally cannot grow: education, healthcare, childcare, elder care. The relative price of the latter is therefore fated to keep rising. Helland and Tabarrok's work showed that the main cause of the surge in American education and healthcare prices is exactly this Baumol mechanism.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

And here is the decisive part. The cost items that make up raising one child are almost all Baumol sectors plus housing. Childcare, education, healthcare, and a house. In the famous decomposition of American consumer prices, since 2000 hospital services have risen more than 200 percent, college tuition around 170 percent, and childcare more than 100 percent, while the prices of televisions, toys and software have fallen by anything from tens of percent to more than 90 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> The goods automation reached collapsed in price; the person-to-person services it did not reach soared. Raising a child is the act of buying, twenty years at a time, the least automated basket in the economy. On the Brookings Institution's update of a US Department of Agriculture estimate, raising one child born in 2015 to the age of seventeen costs about 310,000 dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> In Korea, spending on private tutoring alone hit a record 29.1919 trillion won in 2024. Seoul's ratio of house prices to income is among the worst in the world.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

What the household faces is the budget constraint composed of that price vector and its wages. As we have seen through Chapter 3, in the advanced importing countries after the 1970s households carried the cost of energy conversion over a long period, living costs rose faster than real wages, disposable income was pared back, and births were suppressed to that extent. What comes into view is this: the modern population-suppressing structure that deserves the name "the household Malthus" is not a matter of subjective household judgment or of values. It is a secondary constraint derived mechanically from energy, technology and the price system.

<p class="equation">Expected number of children n* ≈ g(household disposable income Y_d ÷ expected cost per child C_child)</p>
> Here C_child is a composite of the effective prices of housing, education, childcare and healthcare, and each of those terms, being a Baumol sector, rises faster than anything else inside p.

## 4.3 Evidence: economics is arriving at the same place

This hypothesis is consistent with where the economics of fertility has got to. The genealogy is worth setting out. Gary Becker formalised childbearing as an economic choice of the household, and Becker and Lewis showed the quantity-quality trade-off, the mechanism by which rising income raises investment per child, the quality, rather than the number. And the modern summing-up, the survey by Doepke and colleagues, reports a decisive fact.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> The negative income gradient that once held, richer countries and richer households having fewer children, has flattened inside the advanced world and in places has begun to invert. What now separates fertility rates is not income itself but the cost of combining career and family: opportunity cost and effective prices. The frontier of economics is quietly shifting its centre of gravity from cultural determinism to a theory of cost structure.

The individual evidence is worth lining up too. Housing: an American panel analysis showed that a rise in house prices slightly increases births among owner-occupiers while clearly reducing them among renters.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> A wealth effect for those with assets, a price effect for those without. For the young, house prices are the entrance fee for having a child. Employment: Adserà's international comparison showed that high unemployment and unstable employment push fertility down systematically.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> Japan is the decisive instance. The employment ice age, with youth unemployment spiking and the share of non-regular employment doubling, covered the late 1990s and the 2000s, and marriage and births collapsed behind it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> Japanese fertility fell into hell not because values changed first. It fell because the income outlook of the young collapsed first.

For Korea, let me record the scale of the spending and nothing else. Cumulative spending on low-fertility measures since 2006 has reached roughly 380 trillion won, and the TFR arrived at after those twenty years is 0.72 (2023), and 0.55 in Seoul.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> Twenty years of handing out a sum equivalent to several percent of GDP, and the price structure itself did not move.

Pull back to the world and this is not a local phenomenon. On the United Nations' World Population Prospects 2024, world TFR has fallen to 2.25 and two thirds of the world's population live in countries below replacement. The GBD projection published in The Lancet has world TFR heading for 1.83 in 2050 and 1.59 in 2100. Thailand's TFR is 0.86, already below Japan's.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> The human species has entered the gravitational field of price.

### 4.3.1 The largest case: China

The site where this mechanism is running at the largest scale in the world is China. I will not go into the merits of the political system here, only the arithmetic of population and economic structure.

**The cliff.** In 2025 China's annual births fell to 7.92 million. That is a drop of 17 percent in a single year from the previous year's 9.54 million, less than half the 2016 figure, and the lowest since the founding of the republic. The population fell by 3.39 million to 1.405 billion, a fourth consecutive year of decline, and the rate of decline more than doubled against the previous year. Marriage is the leading indicator, and marriage registrations in 2024 numbered 6.10 million, down two tenths on the year, the largest fall since the statistics began. The 2013 peak was 13.47 million.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup>

Why this will not reverse is already settled once you look at the cohorts. The female population aged 20 to 34, which carries 85 percent of births, goes from 105 million in 2025 to 58 million in 2050, close to halving, and that is fixed by the number of people who have already been born. This thesis's own estimate lays the observed trends in marriage rates and marital fertility over that cohort structure and stacks up annual births from them, and the implication is simple. Births, which ran at around 20 million a year in the 1990s (20.67 million in 1996), fall below 6 million around 2032 and flatten out at 5 to 6.5 million a year from the late 2030s into the 2040s.

Let me set out the skeleton of the estimate. Births in a given year are the female population of reproductive age in that year multiplied by age-specific fertility rates and summed. China's mean age at first birth was 28.6 in 2024, and mean age at childbearing is 29.2 to 29.3 on the United Nations' estimates;<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> the distribution of births spreads from roughly 18 to 40, centred on that mean with a slight tail to the right. The decisive point is that every woman who will give birth in 2050 has been born by 2032 at the latest. The population base is already fixed. The only degree of freedom left is the level of age-specific fertility, which is to say the TFR. The TFR of 0.93 used in the figures below is a private estimate, and sources give a range.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup>

The median birth year of the mothers of the 7.92 million children born in 2025, backed out from the mean age at childbearing, falls around 1996. Births in 1996 were 20.67 million. By 2003 they had fallen to about 16 million.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> Mechanically from there, births in 2032 shrink to around 6 million. Actually applying the age-specific fertility distribution and summing gives about 6.55 million if TFR is held at its 2025 level of 0.93, and about 5.97 million if TFR falls to 0.75.

The number of mothers born between 2003 and 2010 is flat, and between 2011 and 2017 births jumped temporarily. That structure gives the estimated line three phases. Births from about 2033 to 2040 stabilise at 5 to 6.5 million (they enter the labour market between 2055 and 2062). The years from about 2041 to 2047 are the last opportunity to raise the number of births (labour market entry between 2063 and 2069). And from 2047 the line enters a phase of halving as the years pass (2070 onward). The estimate for 2060 is 3.12 million even with TFR held, and 2.32 million at a TFR of 0.75.

This reading is still an optimistic one, and I should say so. The held-constant scenario does not price in any further fall from the current TFR of 0.93. Look at the present state of things, with young people in Shanghai, Hangzhou and Shenzhen worn down by 996 and by involution, moving out to Chengdu and elsewhere under the banner of "run", and lying flat, and there is nothing unreasonable about assuming a TFR that falls to somewhere between 0.7 and 0.8. In that case births in the late 2030s come to 5 million a year, which is about the American level. A country of 1.4 billion people and a country of 340 million would be producing the same number of children.

That is the site where the household Malthus mechanism runs at the largest scale in the world. Persistently high youth unemployment reproduces at scale, and without alteration, the suppression of births through a collapsed income outlook seen in section 4.3. The behaviour of not moving when money is handed out, and moving only in the years when the income outlook moved, is the same as Korea's.

What becomes of China as an economy, and as a cluster, is not the subject of this chapter. The chain of oversupply and vanishing demand, the shrinking of the talent pool, and selection between regions are handled in Chapter 8. What this chapter has shown is one thing only. Population is the variable that is fixed before any other, and the one that moves last.

## 4.4 Values are a tertiary variable

Once this structure is in view, the standing of the "change in values" account changes. Wanting to put a career first, not wanting to give up free time, seeing children as a burden. In most cases these values appear at the final stage of a chain that runs like this.

> Energy and technology structure → the price system and the wage structure → the household's budget constraint and its anxiety about the future → weighting toward career and toward fewer children as a "rational" principle of action → that principle later justifying itself as a "value"

In this sense values should be treated not as a primary cause but as a tertiary variable thrown up by a layered structure. People are not choosing values freely in a vacuum. They run a calculation of gains and losses inside the macro structure they are given, and the principle of action they adopt as a result is simply being put into words as a "value".

Let me take up the strongest counter-evidence against this hypothesis. Kearney and colleagues regressed the fall in American fertility since 2007 on state-level economic variables, unemployment, house prices, childcare costs and student debt,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> and showed that no economic variable explains more than a small part of the decline. Their conclusion is a cross-cohort shift in priorities: a change in how younger generations design their lives. That looks like a serious challenge to the price account.

My response has two stages. First, a shift in priorities is not the end point of an explanation. It is the thing to be explained. When the priorities of a generation move all at once, that is not a change in taste occurring in a vacuum; it is a cohort-level adaptation to the price system that generation observed while growing up, and to the struggle it watched its parents' generation put up. To detect the movement of a tertiary variable and call it a primary cause is close to calling the movement of a thermometer needle the cause of heat. Second, their analysis measures elasticities in short-run, cross-state variation. What this thesis claims operates at the long-run, structural level, and a price structure for housing, education and childcare that took twenty years to form does not show up in a few years of variation between states. Even so, their work deserves credit for burying the naive version of the price account, the version that says people will have children if you hand out subsidies. That result in fact points the same way this thesis does. Cheques do not move it. Only structure moves it.

Let me be precise about the reach of the claim this thesis is making. What Kearney and colleagues measure is a marginal effect inside the present regime: how much of the variation in births is explained by variation in unemployment and house prices from state to state. What this thesis is talking about is the regime itself: what happens to births in a state where the effective price of energy falls by an order of magnitude, a productivity revolution reaches the Baumol sectors, and the wealth so created is distributed to households. These are not the same question.

An elasticity observed under the constraint cannot be extrapolated to a world with the constraint removed. And the converse holds too. Data from inside the regime does not refute this thesis's claim, but neither does it support it. This part of the thesis is not observation but conditional prediction. It describes a state humanity has never once been placed in, so direct evidence cannot exist in principle. What is left to lean on is only the record of periods when the regime moved. Britain during the industrial revolution, the postwar United States, the Gulf during the oil boom. Those are the three cases given in Chapter 1, and every one of them is a case of movement toward the loosened side of the constraint. The falsification table in the final chapter is written so that this claim can be handled as a conditional prediction.

Let me record here one hypothesis I raised and then discarded. It was that there might be a relationship between fertility and entrepreneurship.

As intuition it hung together. Starting a family is structurally similar to starting a company. You take on a period you cannot see the end of on your own judgment, you price in the possibility of failure, and you begin anyway. If that is right, then when the readiness for it thins out across a society, the number of new companies and the number of children should move in the same direction. In a society where advancing along a prepared track has become the most rational strategy, both should fall. That was the thought.

I checked, and it was not supported. On World Bank and ILO data for 2023, the correlation between the self-employment share and the total fertility rate is a strong +0.76 across 172 countries. But that is the effect of income. Control for GDP per capita and it drops to +0.13; take only the high-income countries and it is +0.02, and controlling for income, −0.09, which is to say the relationship disappears. Poor countries have thick informal sectors and high fertility. That was all it was.

The individual counterexamples are more decisive still. Korea's self-employment share is 23.2 percent, high by advanced-country standards, and its fertility rate is the lowest in the world at 0.72. Greece, Italy and Poland show the same combination. Conversely the American self-employment share is 6.1 percent, the second lowest in the OECD, and yet its fertility rate of 1.60 is on the high side among the large advanced economies. And in the United States, over the same period from 2019 to 2025 in which new business applications rose six tenths, from 3.5 million to 5.67 million, the fertility rate set a fresh record low.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup> Company formation went up and children went down.

At the individual level a slight relationship is reported. Self-employed married men have 0.1 to 0.2 more children. But the mechanism is not risk appetite; it is business succession. And the direction of the relationship runs the other way in most studies, which mainly detect the path in which having a child leads to choosing self-employment.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup>

Why write this down. To show the method of this thesis. Faced with a residual the price explanation does not reach, there may still be a primary variable worth trying before fleeing to the tertiary variable of values. That stance does not change. But it does not mean that the primary variable one happens to think of survives. This hypothesis was discarded in two hours. The other claims in this thesis deserve the same treatment.

## 4.5 Breaking degrowth: population is the anchor

One misreading to kill before I finish. This thesis is not arguing that population ought to be increased.

The collapse of population is neither an objective nor an enemy. It is one among several symptoms produced by a single structural constraint. Stagnation, rising prices and the failure of distribution come out of the same constraint. The prescription is therefore not one per symptom but one in total. Take the constraint off and every one of the symptoms moves toward resolution, for the simple reason that they were only what they were under a larger structural constraint.

Population occupies a large place in this thesis not because it is the objective. It is because it is the slowest-moving symptom. The slow thing sets the timetable for everything else.

That said, the degrowth cast of mind has to be broken: the idea of accepting a falling population in order to hold down the consumption of resources. There is no ground for suppressing population. Population is a result, and pressure is being applied to it now only because there is a constraint on the supply of energy. If energy were inexhaustibly abundant and supplied cheaply and in volume, the rate of economic growth would properly explode, and with redistribution working, our population would be permitted to rise. As we saw in Chapter 1, societies of abundance have in fact reproduced their populations. In Britain during the industrial revolution, in the postwar United States, in the Gulf during the oil boom.

Elon Musk's dream of becoming a multiplanetary species: is that not an object to be unlocked rather than a mission? Abolish the energy constraint on Spaceship Earth and our civilization turns necessarily toward expansion, and expansion in the direction of space follows as a matter of course. There is no need to make it a mission. Better to put everything into releasing the constraint.

Falling fertility is not a cultural phenomenon but a price phenomenon. Only a collapse in the cost of energy and robotics, therefore, can reverse the demographics. That is the end of the diagnosis. Three chapters have traced the path by which the constraint descends to the household and from the household to population. From the next chapter I take up what happens when that constraint comes off. And the geographical question of where it comes off first is held over to Chapter 8.

## Objections and Limits
### The edge of the framework

Two edges of the framework, and only two.

**The Nordics.** In the countries with the most generous family policy in the world, the TFR has collapsed since the 2010s.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> Family policy transfers childcare and leave, but it has not been able to move housing, the largest term in C_child, or the expected value of the opportunity cost of a career. On top of that, the decision to have a child rests not on current prices but on **expected lifetime prices**. A generation that grew up in an era of low growth discounts the sustainability of policy however generous that policy is. An expected price system is also a price. Even so, I accept that this case marks the edge of this thesis's explanatory power. The gravity of price speaks to necessary conditions; it does not speak to sufficient ones.

**Israel.** The TFR is around 2.9, and this is not a phenomenon confined to the ultra-Orthodox: the secular population holds at around 2.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> And this in a high-price society. I accept that religion, community and a national ethos can overwrite price signals. But the fact that the counterexample is a single country is itself the outline of the law.

### Korean fertility has recovered in recent years (4.3)

Korea's TFR bottomed at 0.72 in 2023 and has recovered for two consecutive years, to 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025. Seoul has risen from 0.55 to 0.63 (birth statistics from Statistics Korea and the National Data Office). **This can be read as an achievement of policy.** This thesis does not read it that way because an explanation in terms of rebound and of people bringing decisions forward holds equally well, and not enough time has passed to separate the two. **What this thesis uses as evidence is only the spending side; it does not use the reversal side.** If that judgment turns out to be wrong, the central proposition of Chapter 4 is itself weakened. The second row of the falsification table in the final chapter corresponds to this.

### The Chinese estimate depends on TFR, a quantity that is not published (4.3.1)

China's National Bureau of Statistics publishes births but does not publish a total fertility rate. **The 0.93 this thesis uses is a private estimate; the *China Population and Employment Statistics Yearbook* gives 1.14 for 2024 and the United Nations estimate is about 1.01, so the sources diverge widely.** What is robust in this thesis's estimate is the population base, the part that says every woman who gives birth in 2050 has been born by 2032 at the latest, not the part that depends on the level of the TFR. **The absolute number of births should therefore be read with a range around it, and what this thesis is claiming is not the level but the shape.**

### On the residual the price account cannot explain (4.4)

The study by Kearney and colleagues treated in the body shows that economic variables explain only a small part of the fall in American fertility. This thesis's response is as written in the body, but let me also write down that the response is not complete. **The objection that a shift in priorities is a thing to be explained is only finished once it shows what explains that shift. This thesis has not yet shown it.** The first and fourth items of the research agenda in the final chapter correspond to this gap.

And there is a price to be paid for an argument that separates the regime from the marginal. **The distinction protects this thesis's claim from data inside the regime, but at the same time it forfeits any support from data inside the regime.** No record exists of a state with the constraint removed, so what is left is only the three cases of periods when the constraint loosened. **Three cases are too few to establish a law.** That is why this thesis writes this part as a conditional prediction rather than as a law.
