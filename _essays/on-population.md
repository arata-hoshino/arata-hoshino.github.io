## 4.1 The classical Malthusian structure

In the debate over falling fertility in the advanced economies, a change in values and a failure of policy are the standard explanations. People came to weight career and free time, and to see children as a cost. Or the government's family policy is weak, and childcare, housing and education are inadequate. Both are partly right. What this chapter asks is whether they are causes.

In the world Malthus analyzed the constraint variable was unambiguous. It was food production. Where land and agricultural technique have a ceiling, a rising population means falling grain per head, and famine, epidemic, war, later marriage and contraception push the population back down again. "Population is permanently tethered to the neighborhood of the food constraint." That is the classical Malthusian trap. Nitrogen fixation by the Haber-Bosch process in the early twentieth century changed that structure dramatically. The spread of artificial fertilizer lifted grain yields enormously, and in the advanced countries a shortage of total calories ceased, at the macro level at least, to be the binding constraint.

Malthus's schema was not itself wrong. What was wrong was the premise that agriculture would go on being the bottleneck forever. The structure, that population is held down by the dominant physical constraint, holds. The bottleneck simply moved out of agriculture into another domain.
