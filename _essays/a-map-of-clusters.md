## 8.1 The unit is the stack

The seven chapters to here have been about when things happen. This last one takes up where.

Let me say at the outset what this chapter is not. It is not geopolitics. The contest of power between states, alliances, deterrence and the succession of hegemony are a subject that requires a book of its own, and this thesis does not treat them. What this chapter treats is a far more prosaic question: where on earth the variables identified so far have gathered.

And the moment the question is put, the country breaks down as a unit. Recall the function derived in Chapter 1.

<p class="equation">Civilizational competitiveness C ≈ Institutions I × Effective energy abundance E × Labour input L</p>
None of these three terms exists as a national average. The number called the industrial electricity price of the United States is the result of averaging Texas and California, and it is of no use in either siting decision. Whether land can be assembled is settled at the level of the county and the municipality. Institutions acquire meaning only once you descend to state law and municipal permitting. What attracts a genius is not a nationality but a density: the density in which, if you get up in the morning and walk to a cafe, you meet a founder and an investor you have never met.

But lining clusters up does not make a map. Before lining them up, one has to say what binds clusters to one another. The answer is the stack.

Computation runs only when five layers are present at once. Borrowing Jensen Huang's arrangement: energy, chips, infrastructure, models, applications (Chapter 2). In Chapter 2 I wrote that this carving of the layers is itself secondary when seen from the side of civilization. I do not revise that judgement now. Seen from the endgoal, two layers are enough. But in this chapter, which asks where what is, the five-layer carving becomes decisive. The resolution required to discuss the purpose of a civilization and the resolution required to discuss where an industry sits are simply different.

And if even one of these five layers is missing, the remaining four do not move. What is decisive is the fact that not one cluster on earth holds all five for itself. Taipei has leading-edge logic but no foundation models. Palo Alto has foundation models but neither lithography tools nor materials. Abu Dhabi has capital and energy but has neither of those.

What settles a cluster's standing is therefore not what that cluster holds but which stack it is connected to. This chapter calls the bundle of those connections a techno-bloc. The three variables of Chapter 1 and the five variables of Chapter 2 were talking about the inside of a cluster. The stack is talking about the outside, which is to say the connections. A map is a diagram of those connections.

## 8.2 Two techno-blocs, and the structure of a division of labour

There are at present only two stacks in the world in any substantive sense.

**The American techno-bloc.** Its essence is not that one country holds every layer. It is that a division of labour holds. Map it onto the five layers of 8.1. America carries models and applications, and the core of infrastructure is in America too, but the two layers below sit outside. In the chip layer, Taiwan takes leading-edge logic, Korea takes high-speed memory, and Japan takes production equipment and materials. In the energy layer, Texas and the Gulf supply connectable gigawatts. On top of that, the Gulf supplies capital and a regulatory sandbox, and England supplies the people and the norms of basic research.

This chain does not function if any one link is pulled out. Put the other way, whether a cluster is built into this division of labour is that cluster's standing.

Let me be more concrete about what Japan, Korea and Taiwan carry. Japan carries chemicals and materials, machinery, industrial robots, semiconductor production equipment, automobiles, and the advanced industries that hang off them. Korea carries semiconductors and electronics, and shipbuilding. Taiwan carries the AI semiconductor itself. Only the three together constitute the physical-side supply chain of physical AI. The role of Japan, Korea and Taiwan is not the brain but the body. This is not a demotion. When physical AI comes down into the physical world, the parts on the physical side pass almost entirely through these three, which is to say they hold the very capacity to implement "direction 1" seen in Chapter 2. But stated precisely in this chapter's framework, they do not hold the upper layers of the stack. Their fate is therefore settled not by their own demand but by the demand of whatever they are connected to.

The Gulf's position, too, becomes exact only in this framework. The Gulf's strength is capital, energy and location, which is another way of saying that it is the one place where the three conditions of Chapter 1 are assembled in the present era. Barakah's 5.6 gigawatts, solar at 1.35 cents per kilowatt-hour which was a world record at the time, and Abu Dhabi's 5-gigawatt-class AI campus (Chapter 1). What the Gulf carries is not a layer but the flow of capital between layers. A node that belongs to every pole while trading with all of them is the exact description of its function.

About the Gulf, as someone who lives there, I have to write one thing honestly. That capital, energy and location are assembled does not mean that talent comes.

What tells in the production function of an innovation corridor is not the population as a whole but a particular stratum: frontier researchers, zero-to-one founders, deep engineers. The net inflow of that stratum into the Gulf, tax advantage notwithstanding, is still thin. The reason is simple. What this stratum uses to choose where to move is not a tax rate but a density. The probability of running into somebody smarter than you every week by accident. The confidence that if you fail, the next job is within walking distance. The number of peers you can argue with at midnight. This is why the Bay Area does not let go of the top tier of talent despite high taxes and a high cost of living, and it is equally why the talent flowing into the Gulf keeps repeating the pattern of coming to earn and leaving in a few years.

There are two touchstones: the average number of years of stay, and whether people raise their children there. The Gulf's foreign population is still, in essence, a revolving door. In the language of 1.2, the Gulf has succeeded better than anywhere in the world at making E, while on the talent-attracting half of I it has not yet finished making the coefficient. And the asymmetry of time constants tells here as well. E was made in ten years; institutions take generations. Whether Abu Dhabi can truly become a pole is settled not by whether the 5GW campus gets built but by whether the children of the people working there graduate from local schools.

To be fair, let me write the other side. The UAE recognises this weakness and is acting on it. The golden visa introduced in 2019 detaches residency from the employer and grants investors, researchers and professionals a self-contained ten-year right of stay; it is an institutional change aimed at the revolving-door structure itself.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> My claim is not that this attempt will fail. It is that the answer is not yet in. A few years from enactment, neither the median length of stay nor whether a second generation stays on can yet be observed. Abu Dhabi succeeded in making E in ten years. The coefficient on the talent side cannot be judged until twenty years after enactment. The implication for an investor is simple. Whether an allocation to the Gulf is framed as a story about energy and capital or as a story about the agglomeration of talent, the time horizon required differs by a factor of two.

England sits on the innermost rim of the first pole. What London and the Oxbridge corridor supply is the intelligence of basic research, and England is also the party to which America grants top-level access first. But that intelligence is inclined to leak. The emblem of it is the fact that the most important AI research organisation this corridor produced ended up under American capital. The place that produces intelligence and the place that holds the equity in that intelligence do not have to be the same. This is the same structure as the capital flight argued in Chapter 6, and it corroborates from the side of talent this thesis's claim that the siting of clusters is decisive. But as seen in Chapter 1, industrial electricity is the highest of 28 compared countries, and high-speed rail costs roughly 396 million pounds per mile. It is also the type case of a cluster that holds only the upper layers: the research comes out, the implementation does not.

**The China techno-bloc.** This one is not a division of labour but vertical integration. A single political unit tries to assemble energy, materials, manufacturing, computation, models and applications for itself. The advantage is speed of decision and resilience against a cut in supply; the defect is that if any one of the five layers is structurally low, that layer becomes the rate limit on the whole. And in fact the first layer, leading-edge semiconductors, is two to three generations behind because of sanctions. However excellent the layers above, there is a ceiling on computing capacity.

Which of the two is faster? This thesis's framework reduces the answer to a single comparison: where the rate-limiting step sits. What is rate-limiting in the implementation of abundance is neither models nor capital but the speed at which gigawatts are connected and buildings are put up. These two are hard to divide up. Neither electricity nor land can be exported. If the division-of-labour structure wins, therefore, it wins only in the case where computation and capital can move to the clusters that are strong on the physical side. If vertical integration wins, it wins only in the case where it holds every layer inside, and then its greatest weakness becomes demand.

### Scoring against the equation

Having talked about the stack, let me return to the equation of Chapter 1. C ≈ I × E × L. A map is a table with these three terms filled in region by region. What follows is that scoring.

**Japan and Korea.** What is missing is E alone. Both hold the upstream of L. Materials, production equipment, industrial robots, high-speed memory. They stand closest to the supply chain of automation, and that is also a bargaining card. As parties for the American stack to hand computing resources or orbital infrastructure to, these two are at the top of the list. I, on the side that calls in capital, is high enough as well. There is ownership, there is investor protection, there is predictability of contract. What is weak is the second half of I, the social inclusiveness on the side that calls in people. And E is structurally low. No resources, dependence on imports, a tight grid. For both countries, therefore, the implementation of next-generation energy, fusion among it, is not an option but a precondition. Fill that alone and the three terms are complete. Leave it unfilled and they remain countries that hold the world's best L and cannot run it at home.

**Europe.** E can be filled; L cannot. If fusion is implemented, the energy side may be solved. But on the automation side there is no technological asset equivalent to Japan's or Korea's. And there is a problem with I as well. Regulatory density is part of institutions, and it tells directly on both the preservation of returns and the speed of permitting. High industrial electricity, slow construction and heavy regulation are not separate problems. They are three faces of one term. The research comes out. The implementation does not.

**Texas and the Gulf.** The three terms are most nearly complete here. E is at the world's highest level and, moreover, was made by decision. As for L, the technology is not homegrown, but it arrives through alliance and capital. And I is extremely strong on the side that calls in capital. That these two regions take the highest values in this thesis's framework is not an accident. They are filling from the side of physics. The remaining question is as written in the first half of this section: the second half of I, whether talent settles.

**China.** What is missing is I. Of the three terms, E it made at the largest scale in the world. L, the first layer aside, is thick as well. But I does not turn. Precisely stated, it is not the I on the side that calls in capital; it is the side that distributes the wealth produced to the population that is not working. The consequence is examined in detail in the next section, but the mechanism can be written in one line. Wealth is not distributed, so households do not become wealthy, people are in surplus, they tire of competition, youth unemployment stays high, births collapse, demand shrinks, and the oversupply deepens further. The choice between a wealthy China and a strong China is what has stopped this gear train. And as seen in 2.7, even when the supply equation turns, without distribution the rotation does not reach demand. China is becoming the first large-scale instance of that.

**India and Africa.** The entrance itself is closing. The twentieth-century entrance of gathering cheap labour and attracting manufacturing no longer opens, because the content of labour input has been replaced by automation. As seen in Chapter 2, the human-wave approach ended in China in the 2010s. Before anyone can catch up, automation goes further away. Their individual circumstances are taken in turn below.

This scoring table settles the composition of the rest of the chapter. What needs going into deeply are only the two that hold two or more of the three terms for themselves and are trying to stand up a stack of their own. That is, China and America. What there is to say about the other regions is close to exhausted by the lines above.

## 8.3 Inside China's vertical integration

In the scoring table I wrote that what China is missing is I. Here I look at what that consists of.

To avoid misunderstanding, let me set this down. That China's I is weak is not a statement about ownership or contract. The institutions on the side that calls in capital did in fact call in the fastest investment in the world over these forty years. What is not moving is the circuit that distributes the wealth produced to the side of the population. And if supply alone keeps being reinforced while that circuit stays shut, the sequence seen in 2.7 is cut partway. Supply turns. But it does not reach demand.

What follows is a record of the site where that severance is actually happening.

Treat China as a monolith and this map becomes unreadable.

Before that, a word about a larger geography. Over the past several decades, population and influence alike have kept moving from the interior of the Eurasian landmass to the coast. Russia's one-sided retreat and Europe's relative retreat can both be read as part of that movement. And the centre of gravity of the destination is the Indo-Pacific, which covers roughly eighty percent of the ocean's surface. The reason is the physics of trade. More than eighty percent of world merchandise trade by volume moves by sea. More sharply: air cargo carries less than 1 percent of world trade by weight but close to three tenths by value.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> Heavy things go by sea; light and expensive things go by air. And the physical substrate this thesis treats, meaning transformers, turbines, reactors and building materials, is all on the heavy side. Distance from a port is, directly, distance to construction.

On this measure the Atlantic is very nearly an inland sea. And South America and sub-Saharan Africa, while settling the world's future in population, are placed outside the connective relations of the stack. As Figure 4-6 in Chapter 4 shows, a little over three tenths of the world's children are now born in sub-Saharan Africa. Being outside the connections and being unimportant are entirely different things. When I write in the fifth item of 8.8 that investment should go into designs that reach there, that means refusing to leave this asymmetry alone, and at the same time it is a note that this is the largest blank on the current map.

Apply the same measure to China and China is not a country but a continent. And the body of it is the coast. What actually drives the Chinese economy is two clusters. The Yangtze River Delta has a population of about 240 million and a GDP of about 4.65 trillion dollars, already larger than Japan as a whole. The Pearl River Delta has a population of about 86 million and a GDP of about 2.1 trillion dollars, the equivalent of Spain. These two regions alone account for about 38 percent of China's GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Beijing does not produce a pole; it exists by administering one.

And over the next ten years China will experience regional divergence on a ferocious scale. The interior will become countryside and will pass through population decline on a scale human history has not yet seen. This is not a story about a country. It is a story about movement to the coast inside a continent. The figures for the three northeastern provinces below are no more than the first instance of it.

Descend to the level of cities and the resolution rises further. Shenzhen is the hardware of physical AI, Hangzhou is the AI ecosystem, and Shanghai is capital and finance. China's computation, manufacture and capital are converging on these three points.

And Beijing does not join that list. Beijing has no water. The water constraint of the North China Plain structurally forbids Beijing from becoming a more concentrated population region than it already is. There is land, but water sets the ceiling. What is happening in China is a selection: people vanish from the countryside, gather in the cities, and in the process winners and losers are settled fairly plainly region by region. Talk about China at the level of the country and this selection becomes invisible.

The two characters are fundamentally different. The Yangtze Delta is finance and software, inward-facing, oriented to the domestic market. The Pearl River Delta is hardware and manufacture, outward-facing, oriented to the global market. Shanghainese and Cantonese are mutually unintelligible, and this is not the difference between New York and Los Angeles. The fault line between a maritime, commercial southern China and an agricultural, bureaucratic northern China has run for a thousand years, and Beijing has placed a political weight on top of that fault line.

From here comes the observation most important to this thesis's framework. The objective functions of the Pearl River Delta and the Yangtze Delta do not coincide with Beijing's objective function. What a Shenzhen engineer wants to use is the world's toolchain, and what Shanghai finance wants to face is global expansion. Being told to develop inside a closed stack is political compulsion, not an engineer's natural choice. In the language of Chapter 2, they want to lower the convergence cost of direction 2, and their connection has been cut.

Let me correct a common misunderstanding here. China's constraint is not in the model layer. The framing of the six companies once called the "six little AI tigers" no longer captures the reality. As of 2026, the top of the Chinese providers has Xiaomi and Alibaba's Qwen side by side, with DeepSeek following, and MiniMax, Zhipu and StepFun, the survivors among the six tigers, in between. In domestic use, ByteDance's Doubao has weekly users on the order of 155 million. And the share of Chinese providers in OpenRouter's weekly tokens moved from under 2 percent a year earlier to over 45 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> In other words the third layer is not weak. What is weak is the first layer. The constraint is in semiconductors, not in models. Get this distinction wrong and you misread both China's fragility and its strength.

What is certain at present is the movement in the opposite direction. China has an independent AI stack, and decoupling with respect to the AI supply chain will proceed further from here. Completing a domestic stack inside a closed ecosystem is probably possible in itself. The problem is that it will be a completion achieved on top of a severed connection to the world market.

The very-long-run branch can therefore be written this way. If a phase arrives in which the integrative force of the Chinese vessel weakens, these two clusters will move toward exit from the China stack. And since the world's stacks are effectively a choice of two, exit means almost automatically a joining with the American stack. Northeast Asia is already inside that sphere. There is a historical precedent as well. When Qing sovereignty weakened, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Yokohama, Kobe and Busan formed, as a treaty-port network, what was in effect a single economic zone.

But whether or not that branch occurs, there is something else about China this thesis can say with confidence. Physical AI solves the supply side. It does not solve the contraction of demand.

As seen in Chapter 4, annual births fell to 7.92 million in 2025, and the population of women aged 20 to 34, who account for 85 percent of births, will roughly halve by 2050, which is already fixed by the number of people already born. From here on it is not a question of demography but the arithmetic of an economy.

**How the trap closes.** An oversupplied economy whose property bubble has burst has only three roads left: expand domestic demand, expand exports, or cut supply back. Japan took thirty years over that adjustment, and the exit came not from policy but from the side of population structure, in the form of a labour shortage. China is attempting to pass through these thirty years on a far lower GDP per capita than Japan's and on a far thinner accumulation. And labour, too, is in oversupply.

What happens if, at this point, one raises slogans such as "new quality productive forces" and pushes productivity higher still? Supply is reinforced further and the situation grows harsher still. As derived in 2.7, what is scarce in a world where supply is automated is not the capacity to supply but demand, and productive capacity nobody can be sold to is not an asset but a liability. And demand is a function of population. Weighting toward supply becomes a self-reinforcing trap. Oversupply produces unemployment, unemployment kills births, falling births kill future demand, and vanished demand produces further oversupply.

The precedent is already inside the country. If you want to see China's future you do not need to wait twenty years. Look at the three northeastern provinces.

Liaoning's births in 2024 were 180,000, a birth rate of 4.32 per thousand. That is far below even China's national birth rate of 5.63 per thousand in 2025. Jilin's births were 97,000. Heilongjiang has not published the disaggregated birth-count item in its statistical communiqué for two years running. Heilongjiang's age structure has those aged 0 to 14 at 8.4 percent of the total population and those aged 65 and over at 19.4 percent. The resident population of the three provinces fell by roughly 12.5 million in total over the ten years from 2014. And part of that is not death but outflow. In 2024 alone, net outflow was 108,000 from Jilin and 49,000 from Liaoning.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup>

The order in which this happened matters. Industry sank, the young left, the remaining population aged, births collapsed, housing stopped carrying a price, and public finance stopped turning. Hegang, in Heilongjiang, was the first provincial city in China to enter a state equivalent to fiscal reconstruction.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> To the question of what becomes of a society whose population has fallen, the three northeastern provinces have already given an answer.

And what is decisive for this chapter's subject is the next point. The population that disappeared from the three northeastern provinces did not disappear from China. It moved. The destinations are the Yangtze Delta and the Greater Bay Area. Seen at the level of the country it is population decline; seen at the level of the cluster it is relocation. Inside China, winners and losers are already being settled. As stated in 8.1, a national average is of no use in a siting decision. The cruellest instance of that proposition is inside China.

**Talent, the second cliff.** As seen in Chapter 2, talent is not an input factor of production but an input factor of direction. What must be asked, then, is not the total but the thickness of the pool from which selection is made. Researchers selected from among some 20 million young people a year and researchers selected once that pool has become 5 million a year are not the same. Even the AI and physical AI sectors are, if not labour-intensive, talent-intensive. Involution occurred in China for one reason only, which is that millions of people are supplied every year from excellent universities. Every time a spotlight falls on a new topic, excellent young people stake their careers and swarm into that industry. This is what the economy of the stampede actually is, and its effect, too, thins along with the pool.

The alternative route is thin as well. The United States has evaded this problem by gathering geniuses from all over the world. As seen in 8.2, what attracts talent is not a tax regime but a cluster, and the premise of a cluster is institutions and freedom. A fortress is not an architecture well suited to gathering geniuses.

But there is an exit. Here the point of this thesis tells. If energy is abundant, automation advances, and the wealth produced explodes, and if that wealth comes to be distributed naturally, then individuals become wealthy and the fertility rate reverses. China is now at the brink of deciding which it will make real first: the strengthening of supply capacity, or the wealth of the individual. On my own observation, China looks to be giving up on a wealthy China and moving to a strong China. If not repeating the century of humiliation is something close to a national creed, then where this ends is a fortified state with extremely strong supply capacity, and a society cut off from the world in its access to computing resources. If that is so, I judge the probability of failure to be high. It is precisely because personal wealth becomes abundant that people become able to have children.

What becomes of a society whose population has collapsed has a precedent. Demand vanishes. The provinces are folded up. Investment flees the country. And as the least visible loss of all, the resource called youth, the population on the side that starts change, disappears from the society as a whole. China is attempting to pass through the process Japan experienced, in a poorer state than Japan, faster than Japan, at ten times Japan's scale. For the world economy this is the announcement of the largest demand shock of the century. And as stated in 5.7, the trap China now faces is one the world as a whole will experience once. The difference is that China experiences it under the constraint, while the world experiences it with the constraint off.

### Can a country that built itself in too deeply turn?

There is a point often made as a weakness inherent in the structure of vertical integration: that overinvestment comes back as the cost of pivoting. The speed and volume with which China built renewable generating capacity from the 2010s into the 2020s cannot be explained by market logic alone. The sight of Xinjiang covered end to end in solar panels and wind turbines looks less like the result of a return calculation than the result of a national order. If so, will it not become a legacy asset with maintenance costs attached when the next technological generation arrives?

I thought so myself at first. But when I went to the numbers, this reading is not supported. I am rewriting it.

China's wind curtailment rate peaked at about 17.6 percent in 2016. In Gansu 43 percent and in Xinjiang 38 percent was being thrown away. At the time it was indeed waste. But over the subsequent process in which installed capacity roughly quadrupled, the curtailment rate fell to 3 percent in 2020 and 2.7 percent in 2023. National utilisation as of 2025 is 94.0 percent for wind and 95.0 percent for solar.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> Had the building genuinely been uneconomic, the proportion thrown away should have kept rising as capacity rose. It moved the other way. Ultra-high-voltage transmission, inter-provincial trading and dispatch reform outran the speed of building.

The cost side points the same way. In IRENA's 2024 compilation, China's levelised cost of onshore wind is 0.029 dollars per kilowatt-hour, the cheapest in the world, and utility-scale solar is 0.033 dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> The low capacity factor is already priced into that number. Because the equipment is cheap, it works even at a low capacity factor.

Further, for 2025 to 2027 China's policy authorities deliberately lowered the utilisation target for new energy from 95 percent to 90 percent or above.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> The reason has been published as well: paying 0.5 yuan of storage cost in order not to throw away 0.3 yuan of electricity per kilowatt-hour is not economically rational. A degree of curtailment is optimal, and aiming at zero curtailment is the more wasteful course. Reading a rise in the curtailment rate straight off as evidence of overinvestment is an error that China's own energy planning authorities identified and corrected.

Where, then, should the criticism fall? In four places. All of them are duller than the story above, and exactly to that extent more solid.

First, the reported utilisation rate counts only curtailment for reasons on the grid. The 2025 published figure carries a note that it is "calculated using only restricted electricity from grid causes." Economic curtailment caused by falling market prices sits outside this number. The actual economic surplus is larger than the published figure.

Second, statistical transparency has gone backwards. In mid-2024 the National Energy Administration stopped publishing the provincial and monthly utilisation series. It did so in a period when they were falling.

Third, the capacity factor is about half the American level. China's wind capacity factor is roughly 22 percent and solar roughly 13 percent, well below the American figures of 34 and 24 percent (utility-scale) respectively.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> China holds roughly 4.7 times America's wind and solar capacity while generation stops at about 2.7 times. Most of the difference is not curtailment but resource conditions, siting, and the adoption rate of tracking mounts. That the same gigawatt does not mean the same electricity is the point most often missed when national strength is compared by installed capacity.

Fourth, and as the most fundamental change, the premise of revenue changed in 2025. Under document No. 136 of the National Development and Reform Commission and the National Energy Administration, wind and solar entering operation from June 2025 were moved from a fixed price linked to the coal benchmark price to a contract for difference set by auction. Shandong's auction in September of the same year cleared at 0.319 yuan per kilowatt-hour for wind and 0.225 yuan for solar.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> There is a view that this level for solar is below the level required to finance new development. The stranding risk sits not in the existing fleet but in the marginal projects still to be built.

And there remains something as a more general proposition. A capital stock built in deeply to a particular technological generation raises the cost of turning to the next. This is not a property peculiar to China. It is asset specificity, a problem economics has handled for a long time. This thesis itself, in 5.6, records the same concern about the AI capital expenditure proceeding in the middle of a market economy. Three tenths of fund managers running 440 billion dollars of assets regarded hyperscaler capital expenditure as the epicentre of a systemic credit event. Overinvestment does not occur because a state gives an order. When conviction concentrates capital, it occurs regardless of the system.

The conclusion of this section is therefore more cautious than my initial intuition. China's renewable fleet is not, for now, a stranded asset. If China suffers in the next change of technological mode, the reason will not be the generating capacity it built too much of but the population seen in the first half of 8.3 and the constraint on the second layer of the stack seen in 8.2. And those two are far harder to move.

## 8.4 Can India become a pole

India has the conditions of a pole assembled. The magnetism of three thousand years of civilizational accumulation. A population that will shortly be the world's largest. The ease of integration into Western supply chains that comes with being English-speaking. Substantive competitiveness in IT services, pharmaceuticals and space. And the legitimacy of democracy. The diplomatic doctrine of strategic autonomy is likewise a declaration of intent not to be wholly subordinate to any pole.

But the condition of population itself needs to be doubted. India's demographic dividend is correct as a statement about the country as a whole and is already incorrect as a statement about clusters.

According to the Sample Registration System of the Registrar General of India, the total fertility rate of the five southern states is 1.3 in Kerala, 1.3 in Tamil Nadu, 1.4 in Andhra Pradesh, 1.5 in Karnataka and 1.5 in Telangana. All India is 1.9. Bihar, by contrast, is 2.8 and Uttar Pradesh 2.6.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> The demographic dividend is in the north, and it is no longer in the south, which is the economic centre.

Line the numbers up and the strangeness of the situation becomes plain. Norway's total fertility rate in 2024 was 1.44, and all five southern states are below it. Yet income per head is roughly 4,700 dollars in Karnataka, 4,500 in Tamil Nadu and 3,800 in Kerala. Norway's GDP per capita is 94,594 dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> The same fertility rate, achieved on a twentieth of the income. The proposition set out in the next section, that fertility decline arrives before wealth, is already complete in southern India.

What does this mean for a cluster account of India? Bangalore and Hyderabad are both in the south. The pool that has supplied them with talent is now the fastest-shrinking pool there is. The population of the north is large in number, but it does not necessarily carry the same quality or the same institutional environment as talent supplied to the southern clusters. India's story of "the world's largest young country" is correct at the level of the country and needs to be read at a discount at the level of the cluster.

Even so, this thesis's reading is that India will not become an independent pole. The reasons narrow to two: brain drain, and the absence of a full-stack semiconductor industry. The first, in the language of Chapter 2, is that the pool for selection is thick while its top layer keeps being drawn off to other clusters. The second is that stacking the upper layers without holding the first layer of the stack leaves the ceiling set from outside.

There are therefore two branches: a road on which India aims at an AI stack of its own, and a road on which it settles inside the American stack. This thesis's reading leans to the latter. An independent stack demands the first layer, and the first layer demands decades of accumulation, enormous capital, and above all the settling of talent. India converges not on an independent pole but on a regional great power with a sphere of influence inside the American techno-bloc. The time frame is the 2040s to the 2050s. This is also where the ranking could move most: if the feasibility of energy and land improves, this reading is rewritten.

## 8.5 The end of the development model itself

Up to here the talk has been of clusters already connected. This section is about the side that is not connected. And this is the longest-range point in the chapter.

The twentieth-century development model was a single sequence. There is cheap labour; manufacturing agglomerates there; a middle class is born; a domestic consumption market forms; industry moves upmarket. Japan, Korea, Taiwan and China all climbed that staircase.

This sequence is breaking from two sides at once.

First, fertility decline arrives before wealth. Vietnam's total fertility rate has already fallen below 2, and urban areas are below 1.5. GDP per capita at that point is about 5,100 dollars. Korea and Taiwan met the same level of fertility at a stage past 10,000 dollars per head, and Vietnam is entering the same problem at roughly half that level. Indonesia too is at about 5,100 dollars per head, and urban Java is falling sharply.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup>

Why this happens can be explained directly in the framework of Chapter 4. It used to be that wealth came first and the price structure throttled births afterwards. Now only the price structure is imported first. Urbanisation pushes up housing and education costs, and the smartphone carries in the lifestyle of the advanced countries and the sense of opportunity cost at the same time. Income has not yet arrived, but the denominator of household Malthus arrives ahead of it.

Second, manufacturing does not come in the first place. The entrance to the twentieth-century sequence was cheap labour. But in a world where the robot is cheaper, cheap labour does not call in investment. As seen in Chapter 2, the automation of direction 1 has not stopped once in two hundred years. The largest reason Bangladesh or Ethiopia cannot become the next Vietnam is neither politics nor geography but that the labour-intensiveness of manufacturing is itself being lost.

Overlay these two and an ironic map appears. The demographic dividend lasts longest in sub-Saharan Africa, which can enjoy it from the 2030s into the 2060s. But it does not have the institutions and infrastructure to make use of it. Where the institutions are, fertility decline has already begun.

Africa's individual circumstances need only be recorded briefly as case notes on this structure. Nigeria was talked about for thirty years on the figure of a population above 200 million, and then in 2024 a currency devaluation shrank dollar GDP by about 47 percent and it lost its position as Africa's largest economy. A country of 237 million has spent decades in a state where its generating capacity is below that of small countries, and the product of the three terms of Chapter 1 is structurally low.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> As for East Africa, the fact that the discourse of potential has itself run for twenty years is a warning signal in its own right. The gap with Vietnam appears not as a difference of political system or of population but as a difference in state execution capacity, which is to say the capacity to connect gigawatts and put up buildings.

This thesis's conclusion is this. The model of becoming wealthy by making use of a demographic dividend has a high probability of ending with Vietnam and Indonesia as the last generation, and even they are short of the trap. This is not a story about the failure of one region. It is the end of the twentieth-century development model itself. And as stated in Chapter 5, if the cost of energy and automation really does collapse, the first and largest beneficiary ought to be not the frontier but the societies now made to pay the highest effective prices. The ethical meaning of the cascade hangs on this section.

## 8.6 How to use space

First, what this thesis is not claiming. This is not a story about humanity living off Earth. For the twenty-first century at least, habitation is economically inefficient. Life support is the use that demands energy, land and labour all at the highest unit price.

What is claimed is that the use of space will proceed at overwhelming speed. The order is visible too. It begins with data centres in orbit, generation follows, the automation and acceleration of transport follows, and production sites go up last.

The reason is that in orbit the conditions of energy differ from those on the ground in two respects. Sunlight arrives unobstructed, and there is no queue for grid connection. The reliability of supply, however, is if anything harsher than on the ground. As stated in 8.8, if the rate limit is lead time, then a place with no queue is structurally strong. But it means accepting, in exchange for the absence of a queue, a different constraint: the waste heat discussed below.

That said, I will not praise it unreservedly. The largest constraint in orbit is not energy but waste heat.

A vacuum has no medium to move heat into. Waste heat can only be radiated as infrared, and the quantity radiated is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature under the Stefan-Boltzmann law. The numbers in this section are not taken at second hand from industry material; I calculated them myself from the law.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup>

**Ideal values.** Radiation from a surface at 20 degrees Celsius is 419 watts per square metre as a black body, 838 watts over two sides. Put the emissivity of the radiator's coating at 0.85 and it is 712 watts over two sides. For one megawatt of computation, roughly 1,400 square metres. Close to a single ice hockey rink.

**Measured values.** The International Space Station's ammonia radiators give 70 kilowatts over 422 square metres, which is 166 watts per square metre. That is less than a fifth of the ideal value. The difference comes not from the emissivity of the coating but from the radiators being operated at low temperature, from the finite thermal conduction of the fins, and from incident infrared radiation from Earth and incident sunlight. Back 166 watts out as one-sided black-body radiation and the effective radiating surface temperature corresponds to minus 41 degrees Celsius. Radiation in orbit has to be designed not at the textbook value but at a fifth of it.

The order of magnitude therefore carries a range. To put one gigawatt of computation in orbit, the radiating area required is about 1.4 square kilometres at the ideal value of 20 degrees Celsius and emissivity 0.85, and about 6.0 square kilometres at the ISS's measured level. This range is not measurement error; it is operating temperature and the quality of the design themselves. Sources differ by an order of magnitude on the area required at gigawatt scale because they do not write down which premises they used.

Mass is a heavier constraint than area. Extrapolate an ISS-class design and the radiator comes to about 100 tonnes per megawatt, an order of magnitude above the roughly 10 tonnes on the side of a leading-edge compute rack. For a gigawatt that is a hundred thousand tonnes. Put the single-trip capacity of current large rockets to low Earth orbit at the hundred-tonne class and the radiators alone amount to a thousand launches. A plan to put gigawatt-scale computation in orbit on current designs is a business of launching radiators, not computers.

Where, then, is the breakthrough? In two places. Temperature, and the form of the radiator.

**Temperature.** Radiation tells as the fourth power of absolute temperature, so if operation at high temperature is possible the area required shrinks rapidly. Computed for a two-sided radiator at emissivity 0.85: 712 watts at 20 degrees Celsius, 1,499 watts at 80 degrees, 2,303 watts at 120 degrees. Raising it from 20 to 120 degrees alone cuts the area to less than a third. The focus of the technology is therefore not on unfolding large panels but on two-phase loops that can dump heat at high temperature and on computers that operate at high temperature. The rate limit on an orbital data centre is not the performance of the semiconductor but how hot the semiconductor can get and still run.

**Form.** In the concept of a liquid-droplet radiator, which replaces the solid panel with droplets of liquid, a weight reduction of up to sevenfold against the conventional approach and a value of 450 watts per kilogram are reported. If that value is real, radiating one gigawatt takes about 2,200 tonnes. Against the hundred thousand tonnes extrapolated for solid radiators, that is roughly one forty-fifth. Whether gigawatt-scale computation in orbit holds up depends almost entirely on this one point. In launches it is the difference between a thousand and twenty, and that is not a difference of cost but a difference between possible and impossible. Europe's ASCEND sets a time frame of a 50-kilowatt demonstration in 2031 and one gigawatt in 2050.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> The 50 kilowatts of 2031 is a scale at which the heat-rejection problem can be evaded; the true test is on the 2050 side.

This thesis's position is as follows. Computation in orbit is not physically closed off. But what opens it is not the side of area; it is the side of temperature. And the time frame is not fast enough to make today's energy investment on the ground a stranded asset immediately.

Even so, an asymmetry remains that should be noted. The more heavily a cluster has invested ahead of others in ground-based energy infrastructure, the greater its downside when this move begins to tell. And at present the only holder of cheap access to orbit is the American techno-bloc. This is the question of which technology Wright's law of Chapter 5 fires on first, and depending on the answer, the map's centre of gravity moves.

## 8.7 What comes after victory

Extend the readings so far straightforwardly and the first half of the twenty-first century is a phase in which the relative advantage of the American techno-bloc accelerates. China contracts relatively under population decline and a closed ecosystem, India converges, and Europe lacks technological autonomy.

But what history shows repeatedly is that at the moment hegemony is completed, the enemy moves from outside to inside. Rome entered civil war after defeating Carthage; the Mongols, having unified Eurasia, came apart in succession struggles; Britain wore out at the point where the cost of maintenance exceeded the benefit. America itself has deepened its internal division from immediately after winning the Cold War.

In this thesis's framework, the enemy that moves inside decomposes into four.

First, distribution. As argued in Chapter 6, task-based distribution breaks not where the external enemy is but from where AI enters most deeply first. That is, it breaks from the centre of the American techno-bloc. A cluster that leaves its conduit shut is stopped from inside by politics.

Second, competition inside the bloc. When the common enemy vanishes, a division of labour is reinterpreted not as an alliance but as a conflict of interest. Between brain and body, between capital and technology, the negotiation over who takes the profit of which layer comes into the open.

Third, the separation of the state from technological power. Space infrastructure, intelligence infrastructure and computing infrastructure are all monopolised by companies rather than states. Whether the American state wins and whether American-origin technological capital wins are not the same question.

Fourth, the question of purpose. The Cold War produced the space programme and military competition produced semiconductors. Once the competitor is gone, the question of what progress is for remains. This lands in the same place as the subject of Chapter 7, which is what measures value once price, the measuring instrument, has been retired.

## 8.8 What, then, should the world invest in

Down from the map to practice. If the readings so far are correct, the allocation of resources from here takes the following order.

**One. Energy R&D.** This has been this thesis's top priority throughout, and it does not change in this chapter's framework either. The rate limit is neither models nor capital but the speed at which gigawatts are connected. And the lead time for a new grid connection is now the most powerful instrument of industrial policy in the world. A policy that shortens the queue moves the ranking of clusters faster than a policy that lowers the price. This claim, as the next part shows, can be measured.

**Two. Implementation design for the physical world.** Palo Alto makes AI's brain and Northeast Asia makes its body. But the people who design what, concretely, that does in a factory, a hospital, a city, logistics and agriculture are decisively short. This is not an engineer's question but a design question. In the language of Chapter 2, it is the work of deciding where to apply the lever, once direction 2 has become the lever that collapses the development cost of direction 1. This will be the thinnest-supplied profession of the coming decade.

**Three. Translation between capital and technology.** The Gulf is on the side that puts up capital and America is on the side that makes the technology. Between the two there is a serious shortage of translators. Translation here does not mean interpreting. It means being able to compose, in both languages, which layer capital goes into, on what horizon, and on what terms. Only capital that can read the function of Chapter 1 gets placed in the right place.

**Four. Institutions of distribution.** The birth-endowment capital accounts, the citizen-share sovereign fund and the universal share in automation capital argued in Chapter 6. This is not an item of ethics but an item of engineering that settles whether a cluster is still functioning ten years from now. The cluster that implements it seriously first buys the social stability of the 2040s.

Five. Basic investment in the side that is not connected. As seen in the previous section, the twentieth-century development model will not be replayed. Even so, a substantial part of the world's population has reached neither stable electricity nor water and sewerage nor basic education. On the same planet where we are talking about fusion and humanoids. If the cascade is real, then as goods whose marginal cost has approached zero, fresh water and electricity and education reach there too. If we do not invest in the design that gets them there, this thesis's argument ends as an in-house conversation on the frontier.

### The queue is policy, not physics

To measure the effective abundance of energy, one has to look at the lead time for a new grid connection in addition to price and reliability. The third is the newest and the most overlooked, so let me set down the numbers.

**The United States.** On the compilation by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, the generation and storage capacity waiting for grid connection at the end of 2025 was 2,061 gigawatts across 8,244 applications.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup> And for projects that entered operation in 2025, the median period from connection application to entry into operation was 61 months. In 2015 it was 36 months; in 2008, 22. It has stretched by almost threefold in seventeen years.

There is a heavier number. Of the projects applied for between 2000 and 2020, only 13 percent by capacity had actually reached operation by the end of 2025. Seventy-five percent were withdrawn. The length of the queue does not represent the size of demand. It represents the size of the graveyard of applications that did not get through.

The load side is more extreme still. ERCOT had roughly 410 gigawatts of large-load connection applications as of April 2026, about 87 percent of them data centres. The same ERCOT reports large load actually in operation of under 4 gigawatts.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> The ratio of application to operation is about a hundred to one. In Virginia, Dominion Energy states that connecting a large load above 100 megawatts takes four to seven years. The company's contract pipeline stood at 53.8 gigawatts in July 2026, but confirmed service contracts were 12.0 gigawatts, a little over two tenths of the total.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> Eight tenths of the pipeline is still only an option.

**The United Kingdom.** NESO's connection queue was above 800 gigawatts as of October 2025, more than four times the capacity needed for the 2030 clean power target. Connection dates offered run into the 2040s, and the gap between requested and offered dates had been widening by roughly 10 months a year. And the demand-side queue tripled in seven months, from 41 gigawatts in November 2024 to 125 gigawatts in June 2025. In the Netherlands, across a wide part of North Holland, no new capacity is expected to be available for the next ten years.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup>

Here there is a large difference inside the same continent. On the same LBNL compilation, ERCOT processes connection applications significantly faster than other grid operators, averaging 24 months. Its rate of entry into operation for projects applied for between 2000 and 2020 is also the highest in the country at 24 percent by capacity, nearly twice the national average of 13 percent. In 2024, connection agreements were signed for more than 25 gigawatts of large-scale generation. On the same North American continent, in the same industry, a national median of 61 months and an average of 24 months coexist. The physics is the same. What differs is the procedure.

The mechanism is plain. In ERCOT, generators do not bear the cost of grid reinforcement. Congestion is handled not by building transmission in advance but by redispatch and curtailment after the fact. Because the transmission network does not have to be finished before connection, there is no waiting.

But this is not a before-and-after comparison of a reform. ERCOT lies outside federal regulatory jurisdiction and was designed this way from the start. What it evidences is therefore not that slow procedures can be made fast but that the design of the procedure settles the time required. I keep the claim within that range.

And speed has its price. Since grid reinforcement is not done in advance, the curtailment risk is borne by the developer. ERCOT's design did not erase the wait for connection; it moved the risk the grid had been carrying in the form of waiting time onto the developer in the form of curtailment. For an investor this is not an abstraction. On a grid where connection is fast, you take on curtailment risk in place of queue risk. Only the object of what you take on changes. The risk does not vanish. Which of the two is preferable has a different answer depending on the project's financing structure.

Let me set down the opposite case as well.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-18">18</a></sup> With the move to a "ready-in-order" cycle process approved in November 2022, PJM stopped accepting new applications for roughly four years, from 2022 to April 2026. The first cycle reopened in April 2026 has drawn 811 applications and 220 gigawatts. PJM itself states a post-transition processing period of one to two years, but this is a self-reported outlook referring only to the study stage, not a measured record of the period from application to entry into operation. In fact LBNL's compilation contains almost no observations for PJM from 2023 to 2025 on the period from application to connection agreement. It was frozen. And the standard the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission set for cluster studies in Order 2023 is 150 days, with penalties of 1,000 to 2,500 dollars per business day for delay. One to two years is two and a half to five times that standard. A queue looking short and a queue being fast are different things.

A queue is not a physical constant. It is the output of institutions. This is the most concrete instance available of the proposition that the effective abundance of energy is an output of institutions. Neither endowment nor technology: the design of the procedure and the allocation of who bears the cost are what settle what can be built on that land. And the regulator has already declared what the number ought to be: 150 days. The present 61 months can be read as the degree of attainment against that target.

There is, however, a floor that institutions cannot shorten. The delivery time for large power transformers has stretched from under a year before the pandemic to a standard of three years now and a maximum of five. On the industry series it went from 50 weeks in 2021 to 128 weeks in the second quarter of 2025, with generator step-up transformers at 144 weeks.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-19">19</a></sup> However fast the procedure is made, two and a half to three years does not move. And this constraint is common to the whole world.

For that very reason there is something to be careful about. For China and the Gulf, no comparable published statistics on connection times exist. The speed of construction can be inferred from various reports, but nobody publishes a figure that measures the period from application to energisation. Being said to be fast and being measured as fast are different things. And since transformer delivery times are common to the whole world, the floor should be the same unless one holds a dedicated procurement network. This thesis places strong expectations on the Gulf, but on this one point it states explicitly that there is no number.

The implication for an investor comes down to the choice of indicator. Ranking countries directly by speed of connection cannot at present be done from published data. The United States publishes times required, the United Kingdom offers connection dates, the Netherlands publishes geographical exhaustion, and China and the Gulf publish nothing. One therefore works with proxies. Not the capacity in the queue but the proportion of the queue that actually reached energisation. Not the gross pipeline but the share held by confirmed contracts. And whether the grid operator has the will to offer a connection date. The last is probably the most eloquent. An operator that can offer a connection date knows its own queue.

## Objections and Limits
### Figures 8-1 and 8-2 are not measured values

Both are original schematics to this thesis. The thickness of the ribbons in Figure 8-1 is the author's ordinal judgement about how much of a layer is carried, not a measured share. The positions in Figure 8-2 are likewise ordinal judgements from the coefficients described in this chapter. **The two figures do not claim quantities. They claim an ordering.** Where the ranking could change places, I have said so at each point in the body.

### The blank on the Gulf (8.8)

**The absence of information most damaging to this thesis's claim is that for China and the Gulf no published statistics exist that measure the period from application to energisation.** The United States publishes times required, the United Kingdom offers connection dates and the Netherlands publishes geographical exhaustion, but the two regions on which this thesis places its strongest expectations are the only ones publishing nothing. The speed of construction can be inferred from various reports, but **being said to be fast and being measured as fast are different things.** And since transformer delivery times are common to the whole world, the floor should be the same unless one holds a dedicated procurement network. Until this blank is filled, this thesis's assessment of the Gulf has not been tested on the same ground as the other regions.

### Do not generalise from ERCOT (8.8)

ERCOT's average of 24 months is not a case of slow procedures being made fast. **That grid lies outside federal regulatory jurisdiction and was designed this way from the start.** What it shows is therefore that the design of the procedure settles the time required, not that changing institutions can shorten it. And speed has its price. Since grid reinforcement is not done in advance, the curtailment risk is borne by the developer. **Queue risk has not vanished; it has been moved across into curtailment risk.** Which of the two is preferable has a different answer depending on the project's financing structure.

### This thesis has withdrawn its initial reading on China's renewables (8.3)

The reading that overinvestment comes back as stranded assets was not supported when I went to the numbers. **Over the process in which installed capacity quadrupled, the curtailment rate in fact fell, and the levelised cost of generation is the cheapest in the world.** The criticism this thesis retains is on four points, all of them duller than the original story. **This withdrawal is not, however, a claim that there is nothing wrong with China's renewable investment.** The stranding risk sits not in the existing fleet but in the marginal projects built after the change to the revenue regime in 2025. And the general problem of asset specificity works regardless of the system. The concern recorded in 5.6 about the AI capital expenditure proceeding in the middle of a market economy is of the same form.

### This chapter does not treat geopolitics

The contest of power between states, alliances, deterrence and the succession of hegemony are not treated. **The map of this chapter can therefore be overwritten by changes in power.** I wrote "what comes after victory" in 8.7 in order to show where that overwriting could occur, from the side of the variables rather than the side of power. **This thesis deliberately leaves blank a subject that requires an independent book of its own.**
