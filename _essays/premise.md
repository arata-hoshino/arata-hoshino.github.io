You can see the future first in San Francisco, wrote Leopold Aschenbrenner.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Half of that is right. The future of computation can indeed be seen from San Francisco. But the future of civilization, which is to say the future of energy, population, and distribution, is visible first from the United Arab Emirates, where I live.

This thesis is that report. There is only one claim in it. Economic stagnation, collapsing birth rates, and wealth that no longer reaches people are not separate problems. Put them in the order they happened and they are one sequence. It begins with energy, which stopped getting cheaper. Then automation, which never reached the things a household has to buy, its housing and childcare and schooling and medicine. Then institutions, which were never built to pass what energy and automation do produce through to the household. So the advanced economies lost the capacity to build at scale, households could no longer afford children, and the wealth that was created stopped reaching people. Energy, automation, institutions. All three are moving at once now, for the first time in half a century. What happens when they line up, and where to be standing when they do, is what follows.

## Where the given and the chosen change places

The first thing living in the Gulf does to you is break your sense of what can be moved and what cannot. What the advanced economies treat as fixed, and what they treat as theirs to design, are swapped here.

First, energy. In the advanced economies energy is a cost, and supply is planned against demand. Here the order runs the other way. Neither Barakah's 5.6 gigawatts of nuclear nor Al Dhafra's 2 gigawatts of solar was built by waiting for demand to accumulate.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> You lay the power down first and decide afterwards what to build on it. That changes the question people ask. Not what a kilowatt-hour costs, but how many gigawatts you can use, for how many years, without having to count the cost. The two questions look alike and are not alike, and most of this thesis stands on the difference.

Second, fertility. Fertility among UAE nationals and among all residents differs by nearly a factor of three.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Same city, same streets, same shops, same summer. What differs is who is staying. One group is settled, and inside the arrangements that share out the country's wealth. The other holds a permit tied to an employer and goes home when it ends, and people who come to earn for a few years do not have children where they are earning. What explains that gap is not culture. It is whether people are staying, and how the wealth is shared.

Third, talent. Energy came first, then capital, then infrastructure. All three arrive if you decide they will. The fourth does not: the person who makes one out of zero. They choose where to move by density rather than by tax rate, by the chance of running into someone smarter than themselves every week.

Put energy, fertility and talent side by side and the inversion is plain. Energy and population, which the advanced economies hold fixed, are variables here. Talent, which they treat as a variable that money sets, is the most fixed of the three. That inversion is where this thesis began. If energy is chosen rather than given, then the stagnation stacked on top of it, and the falling births, and the wealth that stops short of people, were chosen too, and can be unchosen.

## Two puzzles

The future is unreadable if you watch what people say they want. It becomes readable if you watch what they want it for. Two puzzles, a century and a half apart, make the point.

At the start of the industrial revolution, if you had asked people what they wanted, they would have said a faster carriage. What arrived was the railway, and then the automobile. Something nobody ordered delivered what everybody wanted.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

The other is the humanoid robot that appeared in almost every account of the future written in the 1980s. Humanoids are arriving now, but not for the shopping errand. The value the shopping robot was supposed to deliver, which is having what you want appear at your door, came instead from Amazon, from rearranging warehousing, logistics, and the act of ordering. The imagined form never arrived. The value did.

The two puzzles are one puzzle. In both, the value stayed and the thing that had been delivering it was swapped out. What people wanted was not a carriage but to go farther, faster. Not a robot, but to have what they wanted appear at the door. A person's goal always sits on the side of the value. It never sits on the side of the form. And the form is the part that gets replaced. Hold value and form apart and most investment questions settle themselves, because what you can see, and price, and buy is always the form. Follow the same separation all the way down and it runs in one line from the causes of falling fertility through the structural limits of renewables, the trajectory of the Chinese economy, the next form of capitalism, and the end of money. This thesis is an attempt to draw that whole line.

## The energy question is older than AI

Energy abundance is not an AI story. It is a two-century story, and AI is a newcomer to it.

In the discourse of 2026 the phrase circulates almost entirely inside the AI context: how to supply data centers with power, how to thicken the bottom layer of what Jensen Huang calls the five-layer cake. That the bottom layer is energy is a point I would make too.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> The data center is only the newest thing to run into the wall of energy.

What this thesis treats is the wall itself. The abundance of energy has governed the skeleton of civilization since the industrial revolution. What can be built from nothing, whether population grows, how wealth is distributed, what value is measured by. AI is the current name of that two-century trend.

## Physics runs ahead of institutions

You do not win the argument about institutions. You replace the premises the argument is standing on. Two cases: Britain, and my own family.

In 1798 Malthus wrote that there is a ceiling on the number of people this island can feed. For half a century afterward Britain fought over that ceiling. The Corn Laws, the Poor Laws, rent and wages. The argument reached a political settlement in 1846, but what moved the height of the ceiling itself was not the argument. It was coal. To get, from firewood cut at a rate the woods could sustain, the heat that England was digging out of the ground in 1850 would have required 48.1 million acres. One and a half times the surface of the country.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>

Malthus's logic was correct about the world he was describing. In an economy running on nothing but the sunlight that arrived that year, output is bound to land. What broke was not the logic. It was the premise. Coal is the savings of past sunlight, and drawing on it removed the constraint of land on which the entire argument had been resting.

The same replacement happened in Japan, and my own family was inside it. In the last years of the Tokugawa shogunate, while the argument over expelling the foreigners or opening the country was still running, my great-great-grandfather's generation gave practical priority to installing the most advanced systems of the day, which were the steam engine and the telegraph. What they learned was that once the machines are in, the argument itself changes under you. That dispute did not get resolved. It disappeared as a question, somewhere past the point where the foreign steamships' engines had been taken apart and copied, the telegraph lines strung, and the railways laid.

That order is the method running through this thesis, and it is what is happening now in the Gulf.

## On the three variables

Falling fertility is treated as culture, AI as technology, inequality as politics. Seen through three separate windows, the prescriptions contradict each other. Raising the birth rate calls for fiscal spending, AI policy calls for attracting investment, and inequality calls for taxing capital. Do all three at once and the capital that dislikes being taxed leaves the country, and none of the three works. That is what is happening.

What is needed is not a fourth prescription but putting fertility, AI and inequality on one board. Most of what can be observed about a civilization is explained by three variables and no more: institutions, the effective abundance of energy, and labor input. Effective abundance is not the same thing as endowment. The same reserves become almost free energy in one society and stay in the ground in another.

Placed on one board, two things change. The order of cause and symptom inverts, and fertility becomes not a cause but the slowest-moving symptom. And the order of action follows. Rather than lining up one remedy per symptom, you fill in whichever of the three variables is missing.

Cutting it to three variables is crude. But a way of laying things out is never judged right or wrong, only by what it explains and what it predicts. The chapters that follow are that test. For an investor the frame comes down to one question, asked before any judgment about a particular security or a particular technology: which of the three is moving now?

## On a hypothesis that cannot be proved

Consider a society in which energy is supplied so cheaply that its price need not be counted, in which all processes are automated, and in which the way the wealth gets shared has been designed as well. The constraint that has bound what can be made into a business, and where large capital can be put to work, comes off. The wealth that is created is distributed to everyone as a stake held from birth, and by the age of eighteen a person is a capitalist without ever having worked.

At that point what a person does with their time changes. Productivity stops being the aim, because machines supply it. What is left in its place is the thing productivity was only ever a stand-in for, which is applying one's own competence for the benefit of others. That also becomes where income comes from, because by then the fact that someone received a benefit can be recorded directly, without a price standing in as the proxy.

And the problems governments now treat as intractable come from that same constraint: the economy that stopped growing, the social insurance bill that keeps swelling, the gap that keeps widening, the collapse of population. They begin to resolve when it comes off. Increasing population is not the objective. People stopped having children under a larger structural constraint. That is all it was, and when the constraint comes off, births return. Humanity goes farther, production and transport move off the planet, and the world of bits, refined to its limit, becomes nearly indistinguishable from magic.

This is not a proof. It is a hypothesis, and I cannot prove it. Statements about the future are like that in principle.

But being unprovable and being groundless are different things. Having analyzed and diagnosed civilization, one can say that it looks as though it will go this way. Sharpening that "as though" until it is precise enough to commit capital to is the work of this thesis. That is why it sets out eleven predictions and, for each, what would falsify it.

So we begin with the analysis of civilization.
