## 9.1 What to invest in

Which technologies and which institutions should capital go into, starting tomorrow, for the society on the far side of the constraint to arrive? The places map onto the terms of the equation in Chapter 1. Because it is a product, capital does most where the lowest term is. A second test: as section 5.7 showed, the only returns that can be relied on are the ones that do not depend on head-count, and businesses whose returns are proportional to the volume of demand sit in a structural headwind for the next half-century.

### Raising the effective abundance of energy

- **Fusion and SMRs.** Fusion at the top; conventional reactors and SMRs, the modular baseload, govern the transition. Renewables ride on top of firm power as a supplement and do not fill this term on their own. What sets the price is not discovery but volume. The cost curve follows Wright's law, and the contest turns on how many copies of one design get built. So what is worth buying is less the demonstration reactor itself than the production line and the supply chain that repeat one design after it. The effect on price arrives in the 2040s, and only capital that can wait that long belongs here.
- **Grid infrastructure and heavy electrical equipment.** However much generation is added, nothing moves unless a new load can be connected to the grid. Three things are buyable: manufacturing capacity for transformers and switchgear, construction capacity for transmission lines and substations, and land already connected to the grid. The median period from application to entry into operation, for projects entering operation in the United States in 2025, was 61 months, and of the projects applied for between 2000 and 2020 only 13 percent by capacity reached operation at all.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> A new interconnection right is no longer bought with time; it is bought with probability. The delivery time for large transformers went from under a year before the pandemic to a standard three years, with generator step-up transformers at 144 weeks.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> And since neither electricity nor land can be exported, this is the one term that cannot be divided among partners, which makes the choice of site itself the investment decision.

### Raising labor input

- **Physical AI.** Humanoids and automated plant. If mass production begins between the end of the 2020s and the early 2030s, the price of energy and the degree of automation move at the same time. Unit cost is already starting to ride Wright's law while the demonstration of quality lags, and the two advance separately. None of it can be planned on software's clock. Factories, parts, regulation, accidents and repairs all move in the time of the physical world.
- **The leading-edge physical supply chain.** Materials, semiconductor production equipment, lithography, leading-edge logic, high-speed memory, industrial robots, precision motors. When humanoids enter volume production, what runs out first is not the finished machine but the capacity to make this layer. The only region where every layer sits together is Japan-Korea-Taiwan, and Japan-Korea-Taiwan is structurally short of energy. So investment in this layer means nothing unless it sits inside the same design as fusion and the grid.
- **Implementation design for the Baumol sectors.** Housing, education, childcare and healthcare. Physical AI tells on population only when it reaches those four. The expected cost per child is made of them. Building the machines and designing where in a hospital, a school, a nursery or the production of housing to apply them are two different investments. The second is the thinner of the two.

### Building institutions

- **The institutions of distribution.** Capital accounts at birth, citizen-stake sovereign funds, and universal stakes in automation capital. This is not an ethical term but an engineering one: it decides whether a cluster is still working twenty years from now. Institutions can be made in law, so a decision moves them within a few years. Their effect, though, arrives twenty years later, which means institutionalizing them in the 2030s is buying the demand, the challengers and the tax base of the 2050s now.
- **The speed of connection and permitting.** Institutions are observable as lead times.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Britain in the 1840s held two things at once: incorporation by registration alone, and a statute that standardized the procedure for compulsory purchase. What sits in that position now is the interconnection queue, environmental assessment and building permits. The procedure can be shortened; the transformer's delivery time cannot.
- **The infrastructure of measuring use value.** When price stops being the device that rations scarcity, the job that remains is measurement. What is buyable is the technology that records the fact that a beneficiary felt a benefit, and the contractual machinery that turns that record into payment. Without it, neither distribution nor co-creation has a unit to settle in. As Chapter 7 showed, this is the harder of the two, harder than energy.

### Outside the equation

- **Infrastructure outside the stack.** For the half-century from 2035, the only part of the world where head-count is rising is the part now placed outside the stack. The twentieth-century sequence in which cheap labor draws in manufacturing will not be replayed, so that head-count does not turn into purchasing power on its own. The constraint there is neither capability nor will but the cost of capital, the grid and the procedures. Investing to build the ability to buy and investing to sell are two different things: selling into a market where income has not yet arrived turns the sale into a loan.
- **Orbit.** Computation in orbit beats the ground for two reasons: sunlight arrives unobstructed, and there is no connection queue. What stands in the way is not a law of physics but the engineering of getting rid of heat. So what is worth buying is semiconductors that run hot and a form of radiator to replace the solid panel. This is the rival to fusion and the grid, and at the same time the insurance on them.

One caution runs through all ten. Most of the returns named here exist because the constraint has not yet come off. Grid-connected land is dear because the procedure is slow, and faster permitting and connection thin it. Ground-based energy infrastructure is dear because orbit has not yet opened, and once orbit does open, whoever invested earliest and heaviest loses most.

## 9.2 How to invest

What I am proposing is a resource integrator that connects the terms held separately by actors in the United States, the UAE and Japan-Korea-Taiwan: a platform for co-creation that sits between a fund, a studio and a consortium.

As a venture fund it raises capital and puts it to work. As a studio it names, program by program, the theme to be taken on, and integrates the resources of the actors who gather under that theme to co-create concepts that do not yet exist. As a consortium it brings the energy, compute and regulatory parties into one contract. When one body does all three at once, what results can be called an ecosystem.

The studio works like this. It sets out one vision of a size no one reaches alone, such as building one fully automated manufacturing plant all the way through. The actors holding the component technologies that vision requires gather of their own accord to create value, lay their ideas over one another, conceive a range of products, build them, run them and test them. The faces change fluidly with the vision. The practice of putting people from different disciplines in one room and having them design together is not itself new.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> What changes here is the exit: it does not stop at a proposed concept but attaches gigawatts, compute and a site and carries the work through to the real thing. It is an ideathon run to completion.

The capital starts with the sovereign funds of the UAE, which are working to build domestic industry. It goes into the bits side, the foundation models and AI basic research of the San Francisco Bay Area, Texas and BosWash, and into the physical side, the actors around the leading-edge physical supply chain and the next generation of nuclear reactors in Japan-Korea-Taiwan. The counterparties are startups, large companies holding a technology, individuals holding the IP, and laboratories in universities and firms. Those invested in then choose, as the ground for their physical experiments, the UAE and Texas, where energy is cheap, a site can be had, permitting is fast and a regulatory sandbox is in place. This is how the UAE and Texas become the places on earth where implementation goes furthest.

For my own part I take the role on: as a strategic designer, who sets the vision, gathers the actors, prepares the conditions and designs the integration itself, and as a venture designer, who shapes each undertaking that stands up out of it.

## 9.3 The predictions

1. **Energy.** By the middle of the 2030s, in at least one major economic bloc, there will be a year in which new capital investment in nuclear fission exceeds new capital investment in renewables. Fusion will achieve its first commercial delivery of power to the grid in the 2030s, but the main theater of price impact is the diffusion phase in which the learning curve operates, which is to say the 2040s. The start-up of the first commercial reactor will be reported as the news that energy has become free, and that report will be wrong.
2. **The grid.** What settles the siting of gigawatt-scale projects shifts from the price of power to the lead time on connection. By the first half of the 2030s, investment flows will visibly separate between grid operators who can name a connection date and grid operators who cannot. Land already connected to the grid will be traded as an asset class of its own.
3. **Physical AI.** Mass production of humanoids and automated plant begins between the end of the 2020s and the early 2030s. Unit cost rides Wright's law, and the demonstration of quality lags behind it. What runs short first is the capacity to make industrial robots and precision motors.
4. **Population.** China's annual births will not come off their structurally declining path, whatever official support is put in place, because the population of potential mothers is already fixed. The first statistically significant reversal in fertility will be observed not in a country with generous family policy but in a country or a city that has structurally lowered the effective prices of energy and housing. The correlation between the size of a subsidy and the birth rate will weaken further over the coming decade.
5. **Labor.** The relative fall in youth employment in the occupations most exposed to AI will keep widening through the second half of the 2020s, and in at least one major economic bloc youth unemployment will become the central issue in politics. The first wave of solutions offered will be retraining, and it will not work, because tasks do not come back.
6. **Politics.** In the 2030s, in at least two major advanced economies, a wealth tax or a top marginal rate at the 70 percent level will appear in the platform of a party in government. Where it is implemented, it will be rolled back within five years by the movement of capital and of people. The first half of this prediction has already begun, in New York and Seattle in 2025.
7. **Distribution.** In the same 2030s, institutions for making every citizen an investor, meaning capital accounts opened at birth, sovereign funds held as a national stake, and universal stakes in automation capital, will appear as mainstream policy in several countries. This is the beginning of what this thesis calls the update to capitalism. The first country to implement it in earnest buys itself the social stability of the 2050s.
8. **Demand.** By the second half of the 2030s, in at least one major country, the center of policy debate moves from a shortage of labor to a shortage of demand. Over the same years, the difference between returns that do not depend on head-count and returns proportional to the volume of demand comes to be treated as a distinction between asset classes.
9. **Measurement and money.** What moves first is not currency but measurement. In the 2030s the first commercial cases will appear in which a direct record of use value, rather than the optimization of advertising and recommendation, settles what is paid. Exchange denominated in units of energy spreads outside regulated markets but does not displace a major currency within the first half of this century. The divergence between GDP and welfare is acknowledged from inside official statistics, as a satellite account.
10. **The unit of value creation.** On the bits side, assembly and dispersal without employment settles in as one of the forms in which major AI products are built. On the physical side the firm survives as a unit. The average headcount of a company falls in software and AI and does not fall in materials, equipment and power.
11. **Clusters and orbit.** Texas and the UAE stay near the top over the long run, Japan-Korea-Taiwan holds its place as a technological chokepoint, and Europe produces no cluster other than Loxbridge. Computation in orbit reaches a demonstration at the 50-kilowatt scale in 2031, but at that scale heat is not yet a test. Whether a gigawatt-scale system stands by 2050 turns on whether semiconductors that run hot or liquid-droplet radiators are demonstrated.

## 9.4 What would falsify this

Writing the predictions down is not enough. What would make me admit that one of them was wrong has to be written down to the same precision. The purpose of this table is not to protect the predictions but to keep them killable.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup>

What breaks in the skeleton of this thesis when one of them is falsified? If energy is lost, the timeline of Chapter 5 slides backwards, but the identification of the constraint survives. If the grid is lost, the identification itself, that the constraint sits at connection, is wrong. If China's births are lost, the central proposition of Chapter 4 is itself wrong. If labor is lost, the break between task and income, which is the premise of Chapter 6, was never happening in the first place. If politics and distribution are lost, the design argument of Chapter 6 was necessary and yet politically impossible. If measurement and the unit of value creation are lost, the direction of Chapter 7 survives but its timing slides a long way back. The most fragile is China's births. The one that most needs to hold is distribution. Some of these will be wrong, but the way they go wrong is the information. I intend to keep updating this reading by collision with reality.

## 9.5 What is not established here

Four things this thesis asserts and does not establish.

The link between energy prices, technological structure and the birth rate is set out as a mechanism, not measured as one; settling it takes a long-run panel of energy prices, generation mix, real wages and fertility across the advanced economies. The decomposition of the price system is qualitative; what is missing is an estimate, across housing, education, childcare and healthcare, of how much each cost driver contributes to the household Malthus, energy and non-automated labor above all. The account of urban growth rests on historical cases rather than on a model in which cheap energy, cheap labor and abundant land endogenously settle infrastructure investment and agglomeration. And the effect of automation on hours of work and on fertility behavior is argued rather than modeled: what shorter hours, redistributed income and reduced anxiety about the future do to the decision to marry and to have a child belongs in a household macro model this thesis does not build.

## 9.6 Past this, people decide

This thesis has restated, from one layer down, things that are usually discussed as questions of values or of policy: energy, computing capacity, technology, the price system, the household constraint. What geology and physics settle is whether the raw material of energy is there at all. What turns it into energy that is very nearly free, or leaves it in the ground, is institutions. Past that, people decide.

If this reading is right, we become the first generation in human history to design the far side of the constraint rather than the inside of it.

I intend to spend my working life on making that co-creation happen. It is not a thing one person can assemble, so the design goes out first.

Let us meet and talk.
