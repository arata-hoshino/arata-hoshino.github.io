## 7.1 Smith's fork

It may look like an odd choice, but the best guide to this chapter is Adam Smith, two hundred and fifty years ago.

In Book I, Chapter IV of *The Wealth of Nations*, Smith separated two senses of the word value.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Value in use, which expresses the utility of some object, and value in exchange, which expresses the power of purchasing other goods that possession of it conveys. Then he wrote the famous paradox. Nothing is more useful than water, and almost nothing can be bought with it. A diamond has scarcely any value in use, and it will exchange for a vast quantity of other goods. Immediately after setting the paradox down, Smith makes a quiet and decisive choice. He confines everything that follows to exchange value. The reason was practical, not metaphysical. For his purpose, the measurement of national wealth, exchange value was the easier of the two to understand and it gave a standardised measure.

Exchange value, in other words, was a proxy for measurement from the beginning. That proxy was then institutionalised as economics made itself a science on the model of Newtonian mechanics, and it hardened into the view that utility is embedded in goods, into marginal utility theory, into the neoclassical school, and into GDP. The paradox of water and diamonds was not solved. It was shelved, and then the shelving was forgotten.

## 7.2 Service-dominant logic: undoing a two-hundred-and-fifty-year detour

Service-dominant logic (SDL) undoes that detour of two hundred and fifty years. Vargo and Lusch's original paper, and the axiomatisation that followed it, come down to five axioms.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

Axiom 1: service, the application of competences for the benefit of another, is the fundamental basis of exchange. Axiom 2: value is always co-created by multiple actors, always including the beneficiary. Axiom 3: all social and economic actors are resource integrators. Axiom 4: value is always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the beneficiary. Axiom 5: value co-creation is coordinated through actor-generated institutions and institutional arrangements.

Goods are no more than a distribution mechanism for delivering service, a vehicle that carries value, and indirect exchange masks that fundamental basis. What a human being fundamentally needs is not goods. It is value. We have never, from Day 1, been buying coffee. What we feel and pay for is the value obtained through the coffee, the use value, value in context, felt according to the context. Every economy was an experience economy from the start.

And valuation is radically heterogeneous. One person finds value in flying from Dubai to New Haven for a dense intellectual conversation. Another finds it in going to a music festival. The moment value is defined phenomenologically (Axiom 4), both are equally rational. To take that heterogeneous set of valuations as input and translate it into the concrete experiences, services and arrangements that maximise perceived value: that is the role of AI in the world to come. AI becomes the delivery system for value. And Axiom 5 is the last thread laid. What coordinates value co-creation is not market price alone but the institutions actors themselves generate. Price is only one of those institutions.

## 7.3 The blind spot in GDP can be measured

Audit your own day from this vantage and something strange appears. Looking at myself, Arata Hoshino, the value creation I monetise may be only about 20 percent of the total value I create in a day. Full-time work is recorded in GDP as economic activity. But in fact we create value of many kinds across our daily activity. When I have an interesting conversation with a friend, I feel it would be worth fifty dollars to me, and my friend values it enough that he would want to pay fifty dollars too. Set up a company and stage the identical act as consulting, and it enters GDP.

That 20 percent intuition is in fact consistent with the measured values in the economics of measurement. Three of them, in order. First, household production. In the satellite accounts of the US Bureau of Economic Analysis, unpaid household production, meaning cooking, cleaning, childcare and care for the elderly, accounts for between 20 and 25 percent of extended output. Restated as an addition to published GDP it is around 30 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> It is value placed outside the national accounts for no other reason than that it does not pass through a market. Second, time. Averaging the OECD time-use surveys across ages 25 to 64, paid work is only four and a half hours a day, unpaid work takes four, and leisure a little under five.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> More than half of human waking time is spent outside price from the start. Third, free digital goods. In the large-scale choice experiments run by Brynjolfsson and colleagues, the median user demanded about 48 dollars to give up Facebook for a month.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> For a good with a price of zero and a contribution to GDP of very nearly zero. The GDP-B they propose is an attempt to measure that unrecorded consumer surplus, and the value of free goods runs to hundreds of billions of dollars a year.

What GDP measures, then, is not value but the part of value wearing the skin of monetisation. This is not a bug in GDP. It is the specification: Smith's proxy, institutionalised over two hundred and fifty years. As the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission formally acknowledged,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> we have measured what is easy to measure and called what we measured value.

## 7.4 The retirement of money

If that is so, the future can be described as follows.

Money is not a natural object. It is an institution. Simmel drew it as a pure form of social relation, Graeber as a technology of credit and accounting that precedes coinage, Harari as a shared intersubjective fiction.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> The wording differs; the implication is the same. Money and price are an institution (Axiom 5) that humanity generated in order to coordinate resource integration (Axiom 3) in an age of scarcity, and exchange value was its measuring device. An institution that loses its function is retired.

And the mechanics of that loss of function have already been observed in information goods. Where marginal cost is close to zero, competition pushes price down to marginal cost, which is to say to zero, and price stops working as a signal for allocating resources.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> The collapse in the cost of energy and automation extends the same mechanics into the material world (Chapter 5). When the marginal cost of goods and services goes asymptotically to zero, price as a rationing device for scarcity loses the thing it is supposed to measure (Chapter 6).

My view is that in this era the monetary institution collapses. Not as an apocalypse, but as the retirement of an accounting system. The substance of value creation, meaning the service exchange we have been performing since Day 1 and the use value mutually created in context, existed before the price institution appeared and can be defined without it. Shed one layer of measurement and the economy returns to the bare form service-dominant logic has been describing all along. As the previous section showed, that bare form already accounts for more than half of the economy, at this very moment. What retires is not the economy. It is only the ledger.

## 7.5 When use value becomes measurable for the first time

So far the retirement of price has been argued from the energy side. As marginal cost approaches zero, price as a device for rationing scarcity is out of work. But that is only half of it. Price has a second job, and that one is not settled by energy. Measurement.

As section 7.1 showed, Adam Smith granted that use value is the substance of value and then confined his inquiry to exchange value. The reason was practical, not metaphysical. Exchange value was easier to understand and it gave a standardised measure. The price institution, in other words, stands on a single engineering fact. Use value could not be observed. So something observable was put in its place.

The fourth axiom of service-dominant logic states the same fact from the other side. Value is always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the beneficiary. And the original goes on: firms and government agencies may attempt to assess value, but they assess it from their own goals and their own context, and their assessment always diverges from the beneficiary's. "Proxies such as economic efficiency and productivity can be measured, but actual value cannot be assessed."

So what happens when that engineering fact changes?

### Two hundred years on the side of the bit

Chapter 2 drew the history of direction 2 as fidelity in the reproduction of symbols. Language, writing, printing, the telegraph, the telephone, the internet. On Shannon's definition, what these two hundred years advanced is not the transmission of meaning but the reproduction of symbols.

But this sequence has a second effect that has never been counted. Each time fidelity rose, the range of economic activity that could be observed widened. The ledger made transactions observable, the telegraph made distant prices observable, and the smartphone made countless acts that had never carried a price into objects of measurement and billing. Riding in someone else's car, staying in a spare room, five minutes of advice: all of it used to sit outside monetisation. What technology changed is not human desire. It is the resolution of observation.

And this rise in resolution has not yet reached its endpoint. Yoichi Ochiai called the twentieth century the century of the image and the twenty-first the century of magic.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> Twentieth-century media confined reality inside a two-dimensional screen; twenty-first-century computers rewrite reality itself, and the technology dissolves into the environment until it can no longer be seen. Technology that cannot be seen is indistinguishable, from the user's side, from magic. Beyond that lies digital nature, a view of nature in which matter, mind and body alike are described in unified terms from the standpoint of the computer.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup>

The measuring surface moves towards the body as this proceeds. From the screen to the wristwatch. From the wristwatch to the smart contact lens. And then to the brain-machine interface. The point of observation moves from the transaction to the body, and from the body to the nervous system.

### The day the proxy is no longer needed

Here two lines cross.

Smith put exchange value in place because use value could not be measured. The side of the bit has spent two hundred years raising the resolution of observation. If the fact itself, that a beneficiary felt a benefit, becomes recordable, the reason for keeping a proxy disappears.

Concretely. Suppose I talk with someone and get something deep out of it. Under the present institution that value vanishes between the two of us. Set up a company, stage it as consulting, issue an invoice, and the identical act enters GDP. As section 7.3 showed, what GDP measures is not value but the part of it wearing the skin of monetisation.

But if heart rate, facial expression, and in time brain activity, record that I received a benefit, a claim can arise on the side that gave the value. Call it the right to three visits to a café. Without passing through money as a common measure, the receipt of value and the arising of a right against it connect directly. The exchange service-dominant logic described from the beginning, service exchanged for service, gets implemented as described for the first time.

There is a precedent for this structure on the political side. Yusuke Narita set out unconscious data democracy in *Democracy for the 22nd Century*.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> In place of the explicit expression of will that voting is, preferences are aggregated from unconscious data: words, expressions, reactions, attention, down to the level of hormone secretion, and policy is derived from them. Politicians stop being decision-makers. The structure is the same. Democracy replaces the explicit vote with implicit data; the economy replaces the explicit price with implicit value data. Two applications of one piece of architecture.

And the two directions of Chapter 2 both reach their terminus here. As the convergence cost of arranging matter goes to zero, price loses the thing it rations. As the convergence cost of predictive models goes to zero, price loses its reason to stand proxy. Price acquires two causes of death at once. That is the sense in which the retirement of money is not a metaphor. Currency becomes data.

The entrance to this transition is already visible. Tokens and carbon emission allowances are converging on currency. Both were designed as units of account. But for an AI company constrained by energy, the token is a unit of revenue and at the same time a unit of the scarce resource that has to be rationed. As section 5.6 of Chapter 5 showed, through the decade in which the supply of compute is rate-limited by connection to the grid, this scarcity is real. Anything issued, exchanged and stored as a unit of a scarce resource is performing the function of currency at that point. Emission allowances have the same structure. It is the institutional designer rather than the state who sets the quantity issued, and the market that sets the price.

It is more accurate to read these not as the first step in money becoming data but as a movement of money back towards a unit of energy. The quantity of energy consumption allowed per head. A world in which that is the unit sits just in front of a world that measures value in data. The two movements proceed at once, and which of them matures first is settled by which becomes cheap first, measurement or trust.

The claim of this thesis is therefore bounded to the following form. Price retires not only because scarcity disappears. It retires also because use value becomes observable and the reason for keeping a proxy is gone. The first is a problem of energy, and a problem of decades. The second is a problem of measurement and of trust, and it is probably the harder of the two. Reservations about this argument are gathered at the end of the chapter.

## 7.6 Expenditure released: Keynes's fifteen hours and Aristotle's shuttle

So what does a human being released from tasks and from price actually do? A prophecy ninety years old and a thought experiment two thousand three hundred years old have already answered.

In his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," Keynes predicted that within a hundred years humanity's "economic problem" would be on its way to solution, that living standards would be four to eight times higher, and that a fifteen-hour week would be enough.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> And he wrote that humanity would then once more value ends above means and prefer the good to the useful. The prophecy about wealth came true; the prophecy about hours did not. Why it did not is by now obvious to a reader of this thesis. The fruits of productivity were absorbed by task-based distribution (Chapter 6) and by Baumol's price structure (Chapter 4), and never reached the household as the freedom to step down from labour. Keynes's error was not in his economics. It was in leaving the institutional design of distribution out of the prophecy.

Further back still. Aristotle wrote in Book I of the *Politics*<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> that if the shuttle wove the cloth by itself and the plectrum played the lyre by itself, the master craftsman would have no need of subordinates and the master no need of slaves. Release from labour by automation had already been formalised, two thousand three hundred years ago, as a thought experiment about slavery. And here is the decisive observation. The ancient citizen who owned slaves did not think in terms of having his occupation replaced by a slave. He was simply released from labour, and he poured the time into the activity of the polis, into learning and the arts, and into festival. The Greek word for leisure, scholē, is the root of the word school. Free time was not unemployment to them. It was the main body of human activity. That we moderns can speak of automation only as having our jobs taken from us is nothing more than the result of growing up inside a task-based system of distribution. If distribution is guaranteed by another route (Chapter 6), the right name for automation is what the ancients knew it to be: release.

Here every thread laid in Chapter 2 is picked up. The value creation of a civilization has two axes: the direction that raises productivity, and a direction with nothing whatever to do with it, which deploys waste to produce culture and value. Festival, art, hospitality, play, expenditure. It is the side of expenditure Bataille placed at the centre of general economy in *The Accursed Share*, and the side of action Arendt distinguished from labour and work in *The Human Condition*.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> Until now more than half of human time has been bound to the productivity direction, and value creation in the inefficient direction has been allowed only the five or so remaining hours of the day. A world in which the productivity direction has been automated all the way through is a world in which, for the first time, more than half of human time is released into value creation in the inefficient direction.

And the other thread from Chapter 2. Ever since the externalisation of muscle, we have been required, in a form dictated by the macro structure of the economy, to maximise performance along dimensions other than the one we were designed for, muscle mass first and then computation, and we have suffered under that for two hundred years. In a future where computation has been completely externalised, the organisms of this planet are equivalent. That sentence should be read as release, not as nihilism. When the reason for ranking human beings by computational throughput disappears, the human being returns to the centre of the economy not as a computing device but as a being that phenomenologically experiences and co-creates value (Axiom 4), which is to say exactly as service-dominant logic has described it from the start. The brain, at last, gets used the way it was designed to be used.

Here the question of Chapter 4 comes back.

This thesis has treated the fall in births as a price phenomenon: so long as the expected cost per child keeps rising, no birth-rate policy works. But that is only one of the two gates. The second gate is what human time is being used for.

Having a child now competes with a career measured in productivity. What competes is not only time. The measure of who one is has itself been placed on the productivity side. Over two hundred years society has measured human beings by muscle mass and then by computation. On that ruler, having a child is always a deduction.

Retire the ruler and the deduction disappears. Not because prices fall, but because what is being measured changes. Distribution opens the gate on the income side; the redefinition of value opens the gate on the side of meaning. The path by which the equation of Chapter 4 starts running backwards is not disposable income alone.

The industrial revolution of the nineteenth century was richer than the fifteenth, and the twenty-first century now is richer than that. This transition too is an increase in richness. Designing how that connects to the richness of an individual, designing the society, thinking about distribution, and thinking about how population moves: all of that has been done in the chapters up to here. What remains is the question of where on this planet, and by whom, the physics of this civilization gets implemented first. On to the chapter of the map.

## Objections and Limits
### Four reservations about the argument that use value becomes measurable (7.5)

**First, service-dominant logic does not support this conclusion.** The original work has a side to it that defends money. Money is a device for learning relative exchange values quickly on a common scale, and without it actors would have almost no information about exchange value at all. Whether direct measurement by data can replace that learning function depends on whether measurement is cheap and, just as much, on whether it is trusted.

**Second, the current state of the technology is a long way off.** Invasive BMIs are at the clinical-trial stage, and what has reached practical use non-invasively is at the level of reading fragments of intention from muscle potentials. Smart contact lenses are at the prototype stage. And the quantification of subjective experience such as pleasure and satisfaction still depends, for now, on proxies of its own: dopamine response, electrodermal response.

**Third, a problem of principle remains there.** If we depend on proxies, we may simply have replaced one proxy, price, with another, biometric data. And what the fourth axiom says is that an external party cannot assess the beneficiary's value. Brain activity measured from outside is precisely an assessment from outside. **This argument holds only where measurement stops standing proxy and records the person's own phenomenological determination itself.** Including the inverted qualia problem, this is a question that remains on the side of philosophy rather than engineering.

**Fourth, what can be measured gets manufactured.** An institution that pays for measured pleasure simultaneously creates the incentive to manufacture measured pleasure. Price was a coarse measure, but it was hard to fake at scale. There is no guarantee that biometric data has the same property. Being that much easier to handle, it may be that much easier to manipulate.

### Service-dominant logic is not used as authority for the conclusions of this thesis (7.2 and 7.4)

This thesis uses SDL's five axioms as a foundation, but **SDL does not support the conclusion that money retires.** If anything the original work has a side to it that defends money, as set out in the first reservation above. **The step from the axioms to the conclusion belongs to this thesis, not to Vargo and Lusch.** Blur that distinction and an established theory looks as though it were guaranteeing an unestablished claim of this thesis.

### The limits of GDP are not in themselves a new point (7.3)

The observation about the uncounted economy was formally set out by the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission in 2009,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> and the satellite accounts for household production have a series running back to 1965.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> **What is new here is not the limits of measurement themselves but the direction: that those limits may be lifted by technology.** The first half is an introduction to existing knowledge, not a contribution of this thesis.
