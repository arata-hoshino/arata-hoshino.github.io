## 8.1 The cluster, not the nation

The body of the world economy is not the nation but the cluster. A cluster is a bundle of metropolitan areas made continuous by economic activity, by commuting and trade and the movement of people, and it does not follow administrative boundaries.

Cut the world's land by the continuity of its lights at night and the lumps of the economy come out on their own. The 40 megaregions Florida and his colleagues extracted this way hold 17.7 percent of the world's population, and out of them come 66 percent of world output and 85.6 percent of its patents.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

Agglomeration has not come apart, even as the cost of communication approaches zero and economic life moves into virtual space. What draws people is density.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> Working from home in the 2020s was a natural experiment on this structure. Of those who left the downtowns of the twelve largest American metropolitan areas, 58 percent moved to suburbs of the same metropolitan area and 4 percent moved to rural areas.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Embodied discovery, and the cocreation that happens between adjacent cities, have not yet been reproduced in virtual space.

The clusters this thesis takes up are nine. The San Francisco Bay Area, Texas, BosWash, the UAE, Northeast Asia (Japan-Korea-Taiwan), Loxbridge, the Yangtze Delta, the Pearl River Delta (GBA), and Bengaluru-Hyderabad. Drawn at their most generous, these nine come to about a quarter of world GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

And seen in this unit, the argument about the social contract changes shape too. Taxation, elections and distribution all look as though they close at the border. But the places where value is made are far finer than the border, and the range over which talent and capital move is far coarser than it.

## Notes and Sources

1. Megaregions. Richard Florida, Tim Gulden & Charlotta Mellander, "The rise of the mega-region," *Cambridge Journal of Regions, Economy and Society* 1(3), 2008, 459–476. The figures are from the authors' working-paper version of October 2007. Output is estimated from light intensity and then renormalized to national GDP, so this is a spatial redistribution of world GDP rather than an independent measurement of output. The data are from around 2000 and contain almost none of the rise of China and India.
{: #note-1 .note}

2. Density and learning. Jorge De la Roca & Diego Puga, "Learning by Working in Big Cities," *Review of Economic Studies* 84(1), 2017, 106–142: a year worked in one of the top five metropolitan areas raises wages 3.1 percent more than a year worked elsewhere, and the difference stays with the worker wherever they go next. On the contribution of sorting to the urban wage premium: Pierre-Philippe Combes, Gilles Duranton & Laurent Gobillon, "Spatial wage disparities: Sorting matters!," *Journal of Urban Economics* 63(2), 2008, 723–742 (workers' skills explain 40 to 50 percent of spatial wage disparities). The productivity elasticity of density itself is around 0.04, that is about 2.8 percent for a doubling (Gilles Duranton & Diego Puga, "The Economics of Urban Density," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 34(3), 2020, 3–26).
{: #note-2 .note}

3. Working from home, and concentration. The concentration of tech employment in the top eight metropolitan areas rose from 35.2 percent in 2010 to 38.4 percent in 2020. Nicholas Bloom, Arjun Ramani & Joel Alcedo, "How working from home reshapes cities," *PNAS*, 2024 (the remaining destinations are another of the top twelve at 9 percent and smaller cities at 29 percent). The tech employment share is from Mark Muro & Yang You, "Superstars, rising stars, and the rest," Brookings Institution, March 2022. The hollowing of downtowns is real: the top twelve metropolitan areas lost roughly 8 percent of their downtown residents.
{: #note-3 .note}

4. The nine clusters. The total is this thesis's own aggregation; drawn at their most generous, taking whole states and regions, they come to 28.9 trillion dollars in 2024, or 25.9 percent of world GDP, and 22.0 percent redrawn at metropolitan grain. Sources: US Bureau of Economic Analysis GDP by county, IMF *World Economic Outlook*, local statistics for the Yangtze and Pearl River Deltas, PRS Legislative Research state GSDP, and ONS regional GDP for the United Kingdom. The total moves by ten to twenty percent with the drawing of the boundaries.
{: #note-4 .note}
