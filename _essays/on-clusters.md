## 8.1 The body of the world economy is not the country but the cluster

The world economy should not be looked at with the country as the unit. Its body is not the country but the cluster.

By a cluster I mean not a single city but a bundle of metropolitan areas made continuous by commuting, by trade and by the movement of people. The line is drawn by economic continuity, not by administrative boundary.

There are grounds for where the line falls. Cut the world's land by the continuity of its lights at night and the lumps of the economy come out on their own. The 40 megaregions Florida and his colleagues extracted this way hold 17.7 percent of the world's population, and out of them come 66 percent of world output and 85.6 percent of its patents.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Take the 300 largest metropolitan economies instead and the shape is the same: two tenths of the world's population and of its employment, and 47 percent of its output.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> Counted by countries, this structure is invisible.

Agglomeration does not come apart as the cost of communication approaches zero, because agglomeration is made of learning rather than of prices. Half of the urban wage premium is explained by selection: the people there were abler to begin with. What explains the other half is the years themselves, worked in a large city, and the gain is portable. On De la Roca and Puga's estimates, a year worked in one of the top five metropolitan areas raises wages 3.1 percent more than a year worked elsewhere, and the difference stays with the worker wherever they go next.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Density does not sort people; it makes them.

Working from home in the 2020s was a natural experiment on this structure. What dispersed was the inside of cities, not the space between them. Of those who left the downtowns of the twelve largest American metropolitan areas, 58 percent moved to suburbs of the same metropolitan area and 4 percent moved to rural areas.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> The concentration of tech employment in the top eight metropolitan areas rose, from 35.2 percent in 2010 to 38.4 percent in 2020. Embodied discovery, and the cocreation that happens between adjacent cities, have not yet been reproduced in virtual space.

The clusters this thesis takes up are nine. The San Francisco Bay Area, Texas, BosWash, the UAE, Northeast Asia (Japan-Korea-Taiwan), Loxbridge, the Yangtze Delta, the Pearl River Delta (GBA), and Bengaluru-Hyderabad. Drawn at their most generous, these nine come to about a quarter of world GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

And seen in this unit, the argument about the social contract changes shape too. Taxation, elections and distribution all look as though they close at the border. But the places where value is made are far finer than the border, and the range over which talent and capital move is far coarser than it. Design distribution with the country as the unit and the object of the design, the place where value is made, and the place capital escapes to all end up on three different maps.

## 8.2 The center of gravity has gone to sea

Clusters line up along the coasts.

The reason is the physics of trade. More than eighty percent of world merchandise trade by volume, and more than seventy percent by value, moves by sea. Air cargo carries less than 1 percent of world trade by weight but close to three tenths by value.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> Heavy things go by sea; light and expensive things go by air. And the physical substrate this thesis treats, the transformers and turbines and reactors and building materials, is all on the heavy side. Distance from a port is, directly, distance to construction.

The band within 100 kilometers of the coast and below 100 meters of elevation is 9 percent of the world's land. On it live 27 percent of the world's people, and out of it comes 42 percent of world GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> More than half of the cities above ten million people sit inside that band.

And over the past fifty years the center of gravity of that band has moved to the Indo-Pacific. Take the littoral states of the Indo-Pacific together and they hold 69 percent of the world's people and produce 66 percent of world GDP at purchasing power parity. In 1980 the figure was 53 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> On Quah's calculation the world's economic center of gravity lay under the Atlantic in 1980, had moved east of Helsinki and Bucharest by 2008, and arrives between India and China by 2050. Projected onto the surface of the earth that is 9,300 kilometers, one and a half times the planet's radius.

On the other side of the same structure is a disadvantage. The 32 landlocked developing countries hold 7 percent of the world's population and 1.2 percent of its exports. Their development level is about 20 percent lower, and about half of that gap runs through the trade channel.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup>

This is not a statement about diligence. It is the same kind of explanation Jared Diamond gave when he argued that Europe rose because Eurasia was long from east to west.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> Crops and domesticated animals spread only along latitudes, so on a continent laid out east to west, technique travelled fast. The shape of the earth dealt the initial conditions, with no reference to the effort of the people living on it. The present-day equivalent is the fact that heavy things go by sea. For a civilization that connects gigawatts, seats transformers and reactors and puts up buildings, distance from a port is not a cost term. It is a term in what is possible at all.

So the relative retreat of Europe, Russia and the Eurasian interior, the running out of breath in Latin America, and the slowness of the interior of Africa to take off, are not separate stories but one structure appearing in different places. And in the language of Chapter 1, what the two shores of the Atlantic hold is inventory. Where building is happening is the rim of the Indo-Pacific.

## 8.3 Migration is urbanization across a border

Migration is the movement of people into a cluster across a border, and in substance it is urbanization.

Nobody calls a person who moves from Ohio to the San Francisco Bay Area a migrant. Move from Nigeria to the Bay Area and you are one. The destination is the same cluster and the gradient being climbed is the same. The only difference is whether a line was crossed on the way.

For that one line we leave the first case alone and turn the second into the question that splits a country's politics in two. We gave one phenomenon two names, filed them under two disciplines, and built two systems of policy on top. Because there are two names, what is known on one side never reaches the other.

This was not missed. In the hypothesis of the mobility transition that Zelinsky set out in 1971, the two sit in the same cell of the same phase. Among the features of the second phase of modernization he listed, side by side, "massive movement from countryside to cities" and "major outflows of emigrants to available and attractive foreign destinations".<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> In both cases people are climbing a gradient of income. What differs is only the steepness, and across a border it is far steeper. A worker with the same observable characteristics who moves from home to the United States earns 4.5 times as much at the median, and 6.8 times weighted by population.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> No move inside a country pays a multiple like that.

The pull points toward the city because the city takes less physical quantity per head. Double a city's population and output goes up 2.2 times while the electrical cable needed goes up 1.8.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> The physical quantity needed to keep one person falls as density rises. This is not a value or a policy. In the same sense in which water runs downhill, the direction of the pull was not chosen.

So what the border changes is not the direction of the flow but its form. Pritchett explained the difference with two place names. In the Great Plains of the United States people could leave, so the population fell by nearly a third and the income of those who stayed went on growing at the rate of the rest of the country. Zambia took a shock of the same size, but people could not leave, so the population did not fall and only income fell, from its peak in 1964 onward. A region that can be exited becomes a ghost; a region that cannot is lived in as a dead economy. Inside this structure, the motive of a young Cameroonian heading for Paris and the motive of a young Russian from the provinces heading for Moscow are the same motive.

Restriction, too, only changes the form. Forty years of hardening the American border took the probability of being caught on a crossing from 0.24 to 0.44. The inflow did not stop. What stopped was the going home: a probability of 0.48 in 1980 reached zero by 2010, and the settled undocumented population went from three million to twelve million.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> What enforcement stopped was not the people coming in but the people going back.

The pull has a clock on the supply side. Fertility in the countryside runs up to twice the urban rate,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup> and that difference is what sends people to the city. But a countryside that keeps sending loses its women aged 20 to 39. What is moving is the reproductive base itself, so the countryside ages, births fall, and it goes. In Japan, the 2024 analysis put 744 of 1,729 municipalities at risk of disappearing, down from 896 ten years earlier, and attributes the improvement principally to the inflow of foreign residents.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> The role the domestic countryside can no longer perform is already being performed by the countryside outside the border. The pump did not stop. It moved further away.

From this comes a rule for reading clusters. What settles the population of a cluster is not the fertility rate of its country but the gradient. The collapse of births that Chapter 4 saw at the level of the country and the agglomeration this chapter sees at the level of the cluster are not on the same map. That a country's population is falling and that the cluster inside it is shrinking are two different propositions.

Where the world's people will sit after several centuries of this, nobody knows. That they might sit inside a few thousand megaregions would not be surprising. This is not a projection but a reading of a direction, and on the direction I can find no reason to argue.

## 8.4 Not countries but the terms of the equation

The seven chapters to here have been about when things happen. This chapter takes up where.

And the moment the question is put, the country breaks down as a unit. Take the equation derived in Chapter 1 down to the grain at which a place can actually be observed.

<p class="equation">Civilizational competitiveness C ≈ Institutions I × Effective energy abundance E × Labor input L</p>
>
<p class="equation">L ≈ Deep tech T × Large capital K</p>
None of these terms exists as a national average. The number called the industrial electricity price of the United States is the result of averaging Texas and California, and it is of no use in either siting decision. Whether land can be assembled is settled at the level of the county and the municipality. Institutions acquire meaning only once you descend to state law and municipal permitting. What attracts a genius is not a nationality but a density: the density in which, if you get up in the morning and walk to a cafe, you meet a founder and an investor you have never met. So what has to be looked at is not three terms per country. It is the decomposed terms, cluster by cluster.

**I, institutions.** On the side that calls in capital: property rights, investor protection, taxation, and the speed of permitting, which together settle whether the return on capital placed there can be believed to hold for the life of the asset. On the side that calls in talent: that failure is permitted, that the society is inclusive, and that residency is detached from the employer. On that second side the number to watch is not total inflow but the average length of stay, and whether people raise their children there.

**E, effective energy abundance.** How far a place has come toward power that can be drawn without watching the meter. As Chapter 5 showed, what binds a decision is not the level of the price but the sense that the supply is finite. What it is observed by is the effective price of industrial electricity, the reliability of supply, and the lead time for a new grid connection, of which the third is the newest and the most often missed. This term measures a state, not a technology.

**T_1, deep tech in bits.** Foundation models and deep learning research, the proprietary data that trains them, and the technologies that settle physical production while having no physical form themselves, meaning the design tools and the design IP for chips. These are the chokepoints of direction 2 in Chapter 2.

**T_2, deep tech in the physical.** Materials, semiconductor production equipment, lithography, leading-edge logic, high-bandwidth memory, industrial robots and precision motors. These are the chokepoints of direction 1.

**K, large capital.** A balance that can be moved, on a single decision, at a CAPEX of a different order.

The division is not arbitrary. Of the three terms of the Chapter 1 equation, labor input splits into deep tech and large capital, and deep tech splits into the two directions of Chapter 2. Institutions and energy do not split. It does not divide further, and nothing is left out.

Labor input does, however, still have a third way of being filled: labor cheap enough that neither deep tech nor large capital need be bought. That route, filled by hands, still works widely on the present earth, and it tells in the construction speed of the Gulf and in the manufacturing cost of the two deltas. This chapter does not make it a term because, as 2.7 showed, the point where the price of the robot crosses under the price of that route is already in sight. Cheap labor is an input that is working now, not an asset that accumulates.

And not one cluster on earth holds them all at the highest level at once. Taipei holds T_2 more deeply than anywhere else and has the thinnest E of the three. Palo Alto holds T_1, K and I, and not T_2. Abu Dhabi holds E, K and I, and neither side of T. And that Japan-Korea-Taiwan holds T_2 is a different fact from America being able to reach it.

What settles a cluster's standing is therefore not how many terms it has assembled for itself but where it can reach for the ones it lacks. This chapter calls the bundle of those reaching relations a techno-bloc. The terms are about the inside of a cluster; the techno-bloc is about the outside, whose permission gives access to what. What this chapter draws is that diagram.

There are at present only two techno-blocs in the world in any substantive sense. One assembles the terms by division of labor; the other tries to assemble them inside a single border.

Which of the two is faster? This thesis's framework reduces the answer to a single comparison: where the bottleneck sits. What holds back the implementation of abundance is neither models nor capital but the speed at which gigawatts are connected and buildings are put up. These two are hard to divide up. Neither electricity nor land can be exported. If the division-of-labor structure wins, therefore, it wins only in the case where computation and capital can move to the clusters that are strong on the physical side. If vertical integration wins, it wins only in the case where it holds every term inside, and then its greatest weakness becomes demand.

## 8.5 The American techno-bloc

The essence of the American techno-bloc is not that one country holds every term but that a division of labor holds. America holds T_1, along with K and I. T_2 sits outside: Taiwan takes leading-edge logic, Korea takes high-speed memory, and Japan takes production equipment and materials. E is supplied as connectable gigawatts by Texas and the Gulf. On top of that, the Gulf supplies capital and a regulatory sandbox, and England supplies the people of basic research.

This chain does not function if any one link is pulled out. Put the other way, whether a cluster is built into this division of labor is that cluster's standing.

The first plate of Figure 8-2 shows that shape. Every one of the five clusters is a lopsided shape pulled out along a single direction. The San Francisco Bay Area toward T_1, Japan-Korea-Taiwan toward T_2, Texas and the UAE toward I and E, BosWash toward K. Yet laid over one another the five very nearly fill the pentagon. Not one of them is complete, and put together the gaps disappear.

**The United States.** The terms are most nearly complete here. It holds T_1 and it holds K, and in Texas it holds an E at the world's highest level that was made by decision. On T_2 it holds a corner of the production equipment, while neither leading-edge logic nor lithography is homegrown; what is missing arrives through alliance and capital. And I is extremely strong on the side that calls in capital. This country takes the highest value in this thesis's framework because it is filling from the side of physics.

**The Gulf states.** Their strength is capital, energy and location, which is another way of saying that it is the one place where the three conditions of Chapter 1 are assembled in the present era. Barakah's 5.6 gigawatts, solar at 1.35 cents per kilowatt-hour which was a world record at the time, and Abu Dhabi's 5-gigawatt-class AI campus (Chapter 1). What the Gulf carries is not a technological term but the flow of capital between terms. Its function is to be a node that belongs to every pole while trading with all of them.

There is one more input that does not show up well in the figures: construction labor. Building costs in the Gulf are low because people hired at the wage level of the sending country are building inside the price level of the receiving one. Neither Europe nor Northeast Asia has labor at that price. So the same drawing does not get built for the same money. This is also the shortest-lived part of the Gulf's advantage. When direction 1 of Chapter 2 comes down here, the cheapest hands are the first thing it replaces. What survives of the Gulf's advantage is E and the speed of decision, not this input.

But that capital, energy and location are assembled does not mean that talent comes.

What tells in the production function of an innovation corridor is not the population as a whole but a particular stratum: frontier researchers, zero-to-one founders, deep engineers. The net inflow of that stratum into the Gulf, tax advantage notwithstanding, is still thin. The reason is simple. What this stratum uses to choose where to move is not a tax rate but a density. The probability of running into somebody smarter than you every week by accident. The confidence that if you fail, the next job is within walking distance. The number of peers you can argue with at midnight. This is why the San Francisco Bay Area does not let go of the top tier of talent despite high taxes and a high cost of living, and it is equally why the talent flowing into the Gulf keeps repeating the pattern of coming to earn and leaving in a few years.

There are two touchstones: the average number of years of stay, and whether people raise their children there. The Gulf's foreign population is still, in essence, a revolving door. In the language of 8.4, the Gulf has succeeded better than anywhere in the world at making E, while on the talent-calling side of institutions it has not yet finished making the coefficient. And the asymmetry of time constants tells here as well. E was made in ten years; institutions take generations. Whether Abu Dhabi can truly become a pole is settled not by whether the 5GW campus gets built but by whether the children of the people working there graduate from local schools.

The UAE recognizes this weakness and is acting on it. The golden visa introduced in 2019 detaches residency from the employer and grants investors, researchers and professionals a self-contained ten-year right of stay; it is an institutional change aimed at the revolving-door structure itself.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> I am not saying that this attempt will fail. I am saying that the answer is not yet in. A few years from enactment, neither the median length of stay nor whether a second generation stays on can yet be observed. Abu Dhabi succeeded in making E in ten years. The coefficient on the talent side cannot be judged until twenty years after enactment. Whether an allocation to the Gulf is framed as a story about energy and capital or as a story about the agglomeration of talent, the time horizon required differs by a factor of two.

**Japan-Korea-Taiwan.** Japan carries chemicals and materials, machinery, industrial robots, semiconductor production equipment, automobiles, and the advanced industries that hang off them. Korea carries semiconductors and electronics, and shipbuilding. Taiwan carries the AI semiconductor itself. Only the three together constitute the physical-side supply chain of physical AI. The role of Japan-Korea-Taiwan is not the brain but the body. When physical AI comes down into the physical world, the parts on the physical side pass almost entirely through these three, which is to say they hold the very capacity to implement "direction 1" seen in Chapter 2. But stated precisely in this chapter's framework, they do not hold T_1. Their fate is therefore settled not by their own demand but by the demand of whatever they are connected to.

What is missing is E alone. The three hold the upstream of L between them. Materials, production equipment, industrial robots, high-speed memory, and leading-edge logic. They stand closest to the supply chain of automation, and that is also a bargaining card. As parties for the American stack to hand computing resources or orbital infrastructure to, these three are at the top of the list. On the capital-calling side, institutions are high enough as well. There is ownership, there is investor protection, there is predictability of contract. What is weak is the social inclusiveness on the side that calls in people. And E is structurally low. No resources, dependence on imports, a tight grid. Taiwan is the sharpest case of it: the most valuable node of L in the world sits on the thinnest E of the three. For all three, therefore, the implementation of next-generation energy, fusion among it, is not an option but a precondition. Fill that alone and every term is complete. Leave it unfilled and they remain places that hold the world's best L and cannot run it at home.

**England.** England sits on the innermost rim of the first pole. What London and the Oxbridge corridor supply is the intelligence of basic research, and England is also the party to which America grants top-level access first. But that intelligence is inclined to leak. The emblem of it is the fact that the most important AI research organization this corridor produced ended up under American capital. The place that produces intelligence and the place that holds the equity in that intelligence do not have to be the same. This is the same structure as the capital flight argued in Chapter 6, and it corroborates from the side of talent this thesis's claim that the siting of clusters is decisive. But as seen in Chapter 1, industrial electricity is the highest of 28 compared countries, and high-speed rail costs roughly 396 million pounds per mile. It is the type case of a cluster where only the talent-calling side of institutions is high.

## 8.6 China: what is missing is institutions

The China techno-bloc is not a division of labor but vertical integration. A single political unit tries to assemble every term for itself. The advantage is speed of decision and resilience against a cut in supply; the defect is that if any one of the terms is structurally low, it becomes the bottleneck for the whole. And in fact T_2, leading-edge semiconductors, is two to three generations behind because of sanctions. However excellent the models, there is a ceiling on computing capacity.

What is missing is I. Of the terms, E it made at the largest scale in the world. L, T_2 aside, is thick as well. But I does not turn. Precisely stated, what is not working is not the I on the side that calls in capital but the side that distributes the wealth produced to the population. Wealth is not distributed, so households do not become wealthy, people are in surplus, they tire of competition, youth unemployment stays high, births collapse, demand shrinks, and the oversupply deepens further. The choice between a wealthy China and a strong China is what has stopped this gear train.

That China's I is weak is not a statement about ownership or contract. The institutions on the side that calls in capital did in fact call in the fastest investment in the world over these forty years. If supply alone keeps being reinforced while that circuit stays shut, the sequence seen in 2.7 is cut partway. Supply turns, but it does not reach demand.

In the China plate of Figure 8-2, on E, on T_1, on T_2 and on K alike, the four shapes reach the size of the American plate. Only the top differs, and the height of that top follows a rule: the further from the center, the higher. Beijing is lowest, the Yangtze Delta above it, then the GBA, and highest of all is Chengdu-Chongqing, inland. Distance from the grip is what sets the height of institutions. The movement of people and capital inland to Chengdu, and the money that routes through Hong Kong, both run along that gradient. Even so, Chengdu-Chongqing at the highest is at four and a half, an order away from the other four terms. Laying the shapes over one another does not fill that bite. When what is missing is missing from everyone, combination does not help.

**Not a country but a continent.** Treat China as a monolith and this map becomes unreadable. The body of it is the coast. The Yangtze Delta has a population of about 240 million and a GDP of about 4.65 trillion dollars, already larger than Japan as a whole. The Pearl River Delta has a population of about 86 million and a GDP of about 2.1 trillion dollars, the equivalent of Spain. These two regions alone account for about 38 percent of China's GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup> Shenzhen is the hardware of physical AI, Hangzhou is the AI ecosystem and Shanghai is capital and finance, and China's computation, manufacture and capital are converging on those three points. Beijing does not produce a pole; it exists by administering one.

Beijing does not join that list, because it has no water. The water constraint of the North China Plain structurally forbids Beijing from becoming a more concentrated population region than it already is. There is land, but water sets the ceiling. Over the next ten years the interior moves to the outflow side of the population and will pass through a decline on a scale human history has not yet seen. What is happening in China is a selection: people vanish from the countryside, gather in the cities, and in the process winners and losers are settled fairly plainly region by region.

The two deltas differ in character as well. The Yangtze Delta is finance and software, inward-facing, oriented to the domestic market. The Pearl River Delta is hardware and manufacture, outward-facing, oriented to the global market. And the objective functions of both fail to coincide with Beijing's. What a Shenzhen engineer wants to use is the world's toolchain, and what Shanghai finance wants to face is global expansion. Being told to develop inside a closed stack is political compulsion, not an engineer's natural choice. In the language of Chapter 2, they want to lower the convergence cost of direction 2, and their connection has been cut.

**The weakness is not models.** China's constraint is not on T_1. The framing of the six companies once called the "six little AI tigers" no longer captures the reality. As of 2026, the top of the Chinese providers has Xiaomi and Alibaba's Qwen side by side, with DeepSeek following, and MiniMax, Zhipu and StepFun, the survivors among the six tigers, in between. In domestic use, ByteDance's Doubao has weekly users on the order of 155 million. And the share of Chinese providers in OpenRouter's weekly tokens moved from under 2 percent a year earlier to over 45 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-18">18</a></sup> In other words the constraint is in semiconductors, not in models. Get this distinction wrong and you misread both China's fragility and its strength.

China has an independent AI stack, and decoupling will proceed further from here. Completing a domestic stack inside a closed ecosystem is probably possible in itself; the problem is that it will be a completion achieved on top of a severed connection to the world market. If a phase arrives in which the integrative force of the Chinese vessel weakens, the two deltas will move toward exit from the China stack, and since the world's stacks are effectively a choice of two, exit means almost automatically a joining with the American stack. There is a precedent: when Qing sovereignty weakened, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Yokohama, Kobe and Busan formed, as a treaty-port network, what was in effect a single economic zone.

**Physical AI does not solve the contraction of demand.** Whether or not that branch occurs, this can be said. As seen in Chapter 4, annual births fell to 7.92 million in 2025, and the population of women aged 20 to 34, who account for 85 percent of births, will roughly halve by 2050, which is already fixed by the number of people already born. From here on it is not a question of demography but the arithmetic of an economy.

**How the trap closes.** An oversupplied economy whose property bubble has burst has only three roads left: expand domestic demand, expand exports, or cut supply back. Japan took thirty years over that adjustment, and the exit came not from policy but from the side of population structure, in the form of a labor shortage. China is attempting to pass through these thirty years on a far lower GDP per capita than Japan's and on a far thinner accumulation. And labor, too, is in oversupply.

What happens if, at this point, one raises slogans such as "new quality productive forces" and pushes productivity higher still? Supply is reinforced further and the situation grows harsher still. As derived in 2.7, what is scarce in a world where supply is automated is not the capacity to supply but demand, and productive capacity nobody can be sold to is not an asset but a liability. And demand is a function of population. Weighting toward supply becomes a self-reinforcing trap. Oversupply produces unemployment, unemployment kills births, falling births kill future demand, and vanished demand produces further oversupply.

**The three northeastern provinces have already answered.** If you want to see China's future you do not need to wait twenty years. Liaoning's births in 2024 were 180,000, a birth rate of 4.32 per thousand. That is far below even China's national birth rate of 5.63 per thousand in 2025. Jilin's births were 97,000. Heilongjiang has not published the disaggregated birth-count item in its statistical communiqué for two years running. Heilongjiang's age structure has those aged 0 to 14 at 8.4 percent of the total population and those aged 65 and over at 19.4 percent. The resident population of the three provinces fell by roughly 12.5 million in total over the ten years from 2014. And part of that is not death but outflow. In 2024 alone, net outflow was 108,000 from Jilin and 49,000 from Liaoning.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-19">19</a></sup>

The order in which this happened matters. Industry sank, the young left, the remaining population aged, births collapsed, housing stopped carrying a price, and public finance stopped turning. Hegang, in Heilongjiang, was the first provincial city in China to enter a state equivalent to fiscal reconstruction.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-19">19</a></sup> To the question of what becomes of a society whose population has fallen, the three northeastern provinces have already given an answer.

The population that disappeared from the three northeastern provinces did not disappear from China. It moved. The destinations are the Yangtze Delta and the Pearl River Delta. Seen at the level of the country it is population decline; seen at the level of the cluster it is relocation. Inside China, winners and losers are already being settled. As stated in 8.4, a national average is of no use in a siting decision. The cruellest instance of that proposition is inside China.

**Talent, the second cliff.** As seen in Chapter 2, talent is not an input factor of production but an input factor of direction. What must be asked, then, is not the total but the thickness of the pool from which selection is made. Researchers selected from among some 20 million young people a year and researchers selected once that pool has become 5 million a year are not the same. Even the AI and physical AI sectors are, if not labor-intensive, talent-intensive. Involution occurred in China because millions of people are supplied every year from excellent universities. Every time a spotlight falls on a new topic, excellent young people swarm into that industry. This is what the economy of the stampede actually is, and its effect, too, thins along with the pool.

The alternative route is thin as well. The United States has evaded this problem by gathering geniuses from all over the world. As seen in 8.5, what attracts talent is not a tax regime but a cluster, and the premise of a cluster is institutions and freedom. A fortress is not an architecture well suited to gathering geniuses.

**A wealthy China or a strong China.** There is an exit. If energy is abundant, automation advances, and the wealth produced explodes, and if that wealth comes to be distributed naturally, then individuals become wealthy and the fertility rate reverses. China is now at the brink of deciding which it will make real first: the strengthening of supply capacity, or the wealth of the individual. On my own observation, China looks to be giving up on a wealthy China and moving to a strong China. If not repeating the century of humiliation is something close to a national creed, then where this ends is a fortified state with extremely strong supply capacity, and a society cut off from the world in its access to computing resources. If that is so, I judge the probability of failure to be high. It is precisely because personal wealth becomes abundant that people become able to have children.

What becomes of a society whose population has collapsed has a precedent. Demand vanishes. The provinces are folded up. Investment flees the country. And as the least visible loss of all, the resource called youth, the population on the side that starts change, disappears from the society as a whole. China is attempting to pass through the process Japan experienced, in a poorer state than Japan, faster than Japan, at ten times Japan's scale. For the world economy this is the announcement of the largest demand shock of the century. And as stated in 5.7, the trap China now faces is one the world as a whole will experience once. The difference is that China experiences it under the constraint, while the world experiences it with the constraint off.

**The renewable fleet is not a stranded asset.** Vertical integration is often said to carry the weakness that overinvestment comes back as the cost of pivoting. The sight of Xinjiang covered end to end in solar panels and wind turbines looks less like the result of a return calculation than the result of a national order, and if so, will it not become a legacy asset with maintenance costs attached when the next technological generation arrives? I thought so myself at first. But when I went to the numbers, this reading is not supported. Over the process in which installed capacity roughly quadrupled, the wind curtailment rate fell from about 17.6 percent in 2016 to 2.7 percent in 2023, and national utilization as of 2025 is 94.0 percent for wind and 95.0 percent for solar.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-20">20</a></sup> Had the building genuinely been uneconomic, the proportion thrown away should have kept rising as capacity rose. In IRENA's 2024 compilation, China's levelized cost of onshore wind is 0.029 dollars per kilowatt-hour, the cheapest in the world.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-21">21</a></sup> And the policy authorities deliberately lowered the 2025 to 2027 utilization target from 95 percent to 90 percent or above, on the published reasoning that paying 0.5 yuan of storage cost in order not to throw away 0.3 yuan of electricity is not rational.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-22">22</a></sup> Reading a rise in the curtailment rate straight off as evidence of overinvestment is an error that China's own energy planning authorities identified and corrected.

What remains at issue is not the existing fleet but the projects still to be built. From June 2025 wind and solar entering service moved from a fixed price linked to the coal benchmark to contracts for difference set by auction, and Shandong's auction that September cleared solar at 0.225 yuan per kilowatt-hour, a level said to sit below what new development needs in order to raise finance. And that a capital stock built in deeply to a particular technological generation raises the cost of turning to the next is not a property peculiar to China. It is asset specificity, a problem economics has handled for a long time. This thesis itself, in 5.6, records the same concern about the AI capital expenditure proceeding in the middle of a market economy. Overinvestment does not occur because a state gives an order; when conviction concentrates capital, it occurs regardless of the system. If China suffers in the next change of technological mode, the reason will not be the generating capacity it built too much of but the population seen in the first half of this section and the constraint on the constraint on T_2.

## 8.7 India: it will not become a pole

India has the conditions of a pole assembled. The magnetism of three thousand years of civilizational accumulation. A population that will shortly be the world's largest. The ease of integration into Western supply chains that comes with being English-speaking. Substantive competitiveness in IT services, pharmaceuticals and space. And the legitimacy of democracy. The diplomatic doctrine of strategic autonomy is likewise a declaration of intent not to be wholly subordinate to any pole.

But the condition of population itself needs to be doubted. India's demographic dividend is correct as a statement about the country as a whole and is already incorrect as a statement about clusters.

According to the Sample Registration System of the Registrar General of India, the total fertility rate of the five southern states is 1.3 in Kerala, 1.3 in Tamil Nadu, 1.4 in Andhra Pradesh, 1.5 in Karnataka and 1.5 in Telangana. All India is 1.9. Bihar, by contrast, is 2.8 and Uttar Pradesh 2.6.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-24">24</a></sup> The demographic dividend is in the north, and it is no longer in the south, which is the economic center.

Norway's total fertility rate in 2024 was 1.44, and all five southern states are below it. Yet income per head is roughly 4,700 dollars in Karnataka, 4,500 in Tamil Nadu and 3,800 in Kerala. Norway's GDP per capita is 94,594 dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-24">24</a></sup> The same fertility rate, achieved on a twentieth of the income. The proposition that fertility decline arrives before wealth is already complete in southern India.

Bengaluru-Hyderabad is in the south. The pool that has supplied it with talent is now the fastest-shrinking pool there is. The population of the north is large in number, but it does not necessarily carry the same quality or the same institutional environment as talent supplied to the southern clusters. India's story of "the world's largest young country" is correct at the level of the country and needs to be read at a discount at the level of the cluster.

Even so, this thesis's reading is that India will not become an independent pole. The reasons narrow to two: brain drain, and the absence of a full-stack semiconductor industry. The first, in the language of Chapter 2, is that the pool for selection is thick while its top layer keeps being drawn off to other clusters. The second is that stacking T_1 without holding T_2 leaves the ceiling set from outside.

There are therefore two branches: a road on which India aims at an AI stack of its own, and a road on which it settles inside the American stack. This thesis's reading leans to the latter. An independent stack demands T_2, and that demands decades of accumulation, enormous capital, and above all the settling of talent. India converges not on an independent pole but on a regional great power with a sphere of influence inside the American techno-bloc. The time frame is the 2040s to the 2050s. This is also where the ranking could move most: if the feasibility of energy and land improves, this reading is rewritten.

That the India plate of Figure 8-2 is a small shape, reaching toward K with almost nothing standing on T_2, is the shape of this reading.

## 8.8 Europe: the research comes out, the implementation does not

E can be filled; L cannot. If fusion is implemented, the energy side may be solved. But on the automation side there is no technological asset equivalent to what Japan-Korea-Taiwan holds.

In the Europe plate of Figure 8-2, what comes out highest in this region is T_2, with the Netherlands reaching seven and Germany five. The lithography machine, the machine tools and the industrial robots are real, and on this one axis Europe stands on the same ground as the two deltas. It does not reach the ten of Japan-Korea-Taiwan, and the reason is the depth of the layers. Materials, production equipment, lithography, leading-edge logic, high-bandwidth memory, precision motors: the only region where all of them are assembled inside one place is Japan-Korea-Taiwan. Europe is strong at particular steps and does not hold every layer.

On E the Nordics reach six on hydro and geothermal and France follows at five and a half on nuclear, and given that Europe's gigawatt-class loads actually land in the north, those two are real. Germany is at close to nothing, and as a region it is not on the same plate as Texas, the UAE or the two deltas. On T_1 the ceiling is Loxbridge at five and France at three and a half; with Mistral it is not zero, and it is a different height from the Bay Area's nine and a half or the Yangtze Delta's nine. K is lower still.

And there is a problem with I as well. Regulatory density is part of institutions, and it tells directly on both the preservation of returns and the speed of permitting. High industrial electricity, slow construction and heavy regulation are not separate problems. They are three faces of one term.

Loxbridge is BosWash at a smaller scale. Institutions, capital and research, with no energy and nothing physical: the same shape, differing only in size. The other four in Europe do not overlap even with that. That Loxbridge is the region's only cluster follows from the same shape.

## 8.9 Southeast Asia: fertility decline arrives before wealth

The twentieth-century development model was a single sequence. There is cheap labor; manufacturing agglomerates there; a middle class is born; a domestic consumption market forms; industry moves upmarket. Japan, Korea, Taiwan and China all climbed that staircase.

This sequence is breaking from two sides at once.

First, fertility decline arrives before wealth. Vietnam's total fertility rate has already fallen below 2, and urban areas are below 1.5. GDP per capita at that point is about 5,100 dollars. Korea and Taiwan met the same level of fertility at a stage past 10,000 dollars per head, and Vietnam is entering the same problem at roughly half that level. Indonesia too is at about 5,100 dollars per head, and urban Java is falling sharply.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-25">25</a></sup>

Why this happens can be explained directly in the framework of Chapter 4. It used to be that wealth came first and the price structure throttled births afterwards. Now only the price structure is imported first. Urbanization pushes up housing and education costs, and the smartphone carries in the lifestyle of the advanced countries and the sense of opportunity cost at the same time. Income has not yet arrived, but the denominator of household Malthus arrives ahead of it.

Second, manufacturing does not come in the first place. The entrance to the twentieth-century sequence was cheap labor. But in a world where the robot is cheaper, cheap labor does not call in investment. As seen in Chapter 2, the automation of direction 1 has not stopped once in two hundred years. The largest reason Bangladesh or Ethiopia cannot become the next Vietnam is neither politics nor geography but that the labor-intensiveness of manufacturing is itself being lost.

The model of becoming wealthy by making use of a demographic dividend has a high probability of ending with Vietnam and Indonesia as the last generation, and even they are short of the trap. This is not the failure of one region but the end of the twentieth-century development model itself.

## 8.10 Sub-Saharan Africa: the longest dividend and the largest blank

With the twentieth-century sequence broken from both sides, an ironic map appears. The demographic dividend lasts longest in sub-Saharan Africa, which can enjoy it from the 2030s into the 2060s. But it does not have the institutions and infrastructure to make use of it. Where the institutions are, fertility decline has already begun.

In the global south plate of Figure 8-2, for Indonesia, Vietnam, Nigeria and Kenya none of the five terms is yet standing. The largest blank is this blank.

South America and sub-Saharan Africa, while settling the world's future in population, are placed outside the connective relations of the stack. As Figure 4-6 in Chapter 4 shows, a little over three tenths of the world's children are now born in sub-Saharan Africa. Being outside the connections and being unimportant are entirely different things. This thesis's position is that investment should go into designs that reach there, and that this is the largest blank on the current map.

In 2024 a currency devaluation shrank Nigeria's dollar GDP by about 47 percent and it lost its position as Africa's largest economy. The more structural number is on the power side. For a population of 237 million, average generation output stands at roughly 4,100 megawatts against 13,625 megawatts of grid-connected capacity, and the product of the three terms of Chapter 1 is structurally low.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-26">26</a></sup> As for East Africa, outlooks have been offered for twenty years, and this thesis has not been able to confirm a record of connections that bears them out. The gap with Vietnam appears not as a difference of political system or of population but as a difference in state execution capacity, which is to say the capacity to connect gigawatts and put up buildings.

As section 5.7 of Chapter 5 showed, in a world where supply is automated the scarce thing is demand. The region where the demographic dividend lasts longest is also the only region where head-count is still rising during exactly the forty years in which head-count is the scarce factor. What is missing is purchasing power, and purchasing power is a function of income, and income is a function of connection. This is a region that is an object of investment and, on the right conditions, a party with a bargaining position.

And if the cost of energy and automation really does collapse, the first and largest beneficiary ought to be not the frontier but the societies now made to pay the highest effective prices. The ethical meaning of the cascade hangs here.

## 8.11 Orbit

This thesis is not claiming that humanity will live off Earth. For the twenty-first century at least, habitation is economically inefficient. Life support is the use that demands energy, land and labor all at the highest unit price.

What is claimed is that the use of space will proceed at overwhelming speed. The order runs from data centers in orbit, generation follows, the automation and acceleration of transport follows, and production sites go up last. In the terms of the equation, an orbital infrastructure business is built out of T_2 and K, and what it sells is E.

The reason is that in orbit the conditions of energy differ from those on the ground in two respects. Sunlight arrives unobstructed, and there is no queue for grid connection. The reliability of supply, however, is if anything harsher than on the ground. If what sets the speed is lead time, then a place with no queue is structurally strong. But it means accepting, in exchange for the absence of a queue, a different constraint: the waste heat discussed below.

The largest constraint in orbit is not energy but waste heat. A vacuum has no medium to move heat into. Waste heat can only be radiated as infrared, and the quantity radiated is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature under the Stefan-Boltzmann law.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-27">27</a></sup> 

A double-sided radiator at 20 degrees Celsius with an emissivity of 0.85 sheds 712 watts per square meter. But the International Space Station's ammonia radiators measure 166 watts, less than a fifth of the ideal. Putting one gigawatt of computation in orbit therefore needs a radiating area of about 1.4 square kilometers on the ideal figure and about 6.0 on the measured one. And mass is a heavier constraint than area. Extrapolate an ISS-class design and the radiator comes to about 100 tonnes per megawatt against about 10 tonnes for the computing racks, an order of magnitude more. One gigawatt means a hundred thousand tonnes, and at a hundred tonnes per launch for current heavy rockets that is a thousand launches of radiator alone. On present designs, putting gigawatt-class computation in orbit is a business of launching radiators rather than computers.

There are two ways through: temperature, and the form of the radiator. Radiation tells as the fourth power of absolute temperature, so raising the operating point from 20 to 120 degrees Celsius alone cuts the required area to under a third. The bottleneck of an orbital data center is not the performance of the semiconductor but how hot the semiconductor still runs at. And in the concept of a liquid-droplet radiator, which replaces the solid panel with droplets of liquid, a figure of 450 watts per kilogram is reported. If that figure is real, shedding one gigawatt takes about 2,200 tonnes, roughly a forty-fifth of the hundred thousand tonnes extrapolated from solid panels, and in launches the difference between a thousand and twenty. That is not a difference of cost but of possible and impossible. Europe's ASCEND sets a timeline of 50 kilowatts demonstrated in 2031 and one gigawatt in 2050. The 50 kilowatts of 2031 is a scale at which the heat problem can be evaded; the real test is on the 2050 side.

Computation in orbit is not physically closed off. But what opens it is not the side of area; it is the side of temperature. And the time frame is not fast enough to make today's energy investment on the ground a stranded asset immediately.

Even so, an asymmetry remains. The more heavily a cluster has invested ahead of others in ground-based energy infrastructure, the greater its downside when this move begins to tell. And at present the only holder of cheap access to orbit is the American techno-bloc. This is the question of which technology Wright's law of Chapter 5 fires on first, and depending on the answer, the map's center of gravity moves.

## 8.12 What comes after victory

Extend the readings so far straightforwardly and the first half of the twenty-first century is a phase in which the relative advantage of the American techno-bloc accelerates. China contracts relatively under population decline and a closed ecosystem, India converges, and Europe lacks technological autonomy.

But what history shows repeatedly is that at the moment hegemony is completed, the enemy moves from outside to inside. Rome entered civil war after defeating Carthage; the Mongols, having unified Eurasia, came apart in succession struggles; Britain wore out at the point where the cost of maintenance exceeded the benefit. America itself has deepened its internal division from immediately after winning the Cold War.

In this thesis's framework, the enemy that moves inside decomposes into four.

First, distribution. As argued in Chapter 6, task-based distribution breaks not where the external enemy is but from where AI enters most deeply first. That is, it breaks from the center of the American techno-bloc. A cluster that leaves its conduit shut is stopped from inside by politics.

Second, competition inside the bloc. When the common enemy vanishes, a division of labor is reinterpreted not as an alliance but as a conflict of interest. Between brain and body, between capital and technology, the negotiation over who takes the profit of which term comes into the open.

Third, the separation of the state from technological power. Space infrastructure, intelligence infrastructure and computing infrastructure are all monopolized by companies rather than states. Whether the American state wins and whether American-origin technological capital wins are not the same question.

Fourth, the question of purpose. The Cold War produced the space program and military competition produced semiconductors. Once the competitor is gone, the question of what progress is for remains. This lands in the same place as the subject of Chapter 7, which is what measures value once price, the measuring instrument, has been retired.

## 8.13 How the speed of connection can be measured

To measure the effective abundance of energy, one has to look at the lead time for a new grid connection in addition to price and reliability. The third is the newest and the most overlooked.

**The United States.** On the compilation by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, the generation and storage capacity waiting for grid connection at the end of 2025 was 2,061 gigawatts across 8,244 applications.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-28">28</a></sup> And for projects that entered operation in 2025, the median period from connection application to entry into operation was 61 months. In 2015 it was 36 months; in 2008, 22. It has stretched by almost threefold in seventeen years.

There is a heavier number. Of the projects applied for between 2000 and 2020, only 13 percent by capacity had actually reached operation by the end of 2025. Seventy-five percent were withdrawn. The length of the queue does not represent the size of demand. It represents the size of the graveyard of applications that did not get through.

The load side is more extreme still. ERCOT had roughly 410 gigawatts of large-load connection applications as of April 2026, about 87 percent of them data centers. The same ERCOT reports large load actually in operation of under 4 gigawatts.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-29">29</a></sup> The ratio of application to operation is about a hundred to one. In Virginia, Dominion Energy states that connecting a large load above 100 megawatts takes four to seven years. The company's contract pipeline stood at 53.8 gigawatts in July 2026, but confirmed service contracts were 12.0 gigawatts, a little over two tenths of the total.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-30">30</a></sup> Eight tenths of the pipeline is still only an option.

**The United Kingdom.** NESO's connection queue was above 800 gigawatts as of October 2025, more than four times the capacity needed for the 2030 clean power target. Connection dates offered run into the 2040s, and the gap between requested and offered dates had been widening by roughly 10 months a year. And the demand-side queue tripled in seven months, from 41 gigawatts in November 2024 to 125 gigawatts in June 2025. In the Netherlands, across a wide part of North Holland, no new capacity is expected to be available for the next ten years.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-31">31</a></sup>

Here there is a large difference inside the same continent. On the same LBNL compilation, ERCOT processes connection applications significantly faster than other grid operators, averaging 24 months. Its rate of entry into operation for projects applied for between 2000 and 2020 is also the highest in the country at 24 percent by capacity, nearly twice the national average of 13 percent. In 2024, connection agreements were signed for more than 25 gigawatts of large-scale generation. On the same North American continent, in the same industry, a national median of 61 months and an average of 24 months coexist. The physics is the same. What differs is the procedure.

The mechanism is plain. In ERCOT, generators do not bear the cost of grid reinforcement. Congestion is handled not by building transmission in advance but by redispatch and curtailment after the fact. Because the transmission network does not have to be finished before connection, there is no waiting.

The opposite case exists as well.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-32">32</a></sup> With the move to a "ready-in-order" cycle process approved in November 2022, PJM stopped accepting new applications for roughly four years, from 2022 to April 2026. The first cycle reopened in April 2026 has drawn 811 applications and 220 gigawatts. PJM itself states a post-transition processing period of one to two years, but this is a self-reported outlook referring only to the study stage, not a measured record of the period from application to entry into operation. The standard the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission set for cluster studies in Order 2023 is 150 days, with penalties of 1,000 to 2,500 dollars per business day for delay. One to two years is two and a half to five times that standard. A queue looking short and a queue being fast are different things.

A queue is not a physical constant. It is the output of institutions. This is the most concrete instance available of the proposition that the effective abundance of energy is an output of institutions. Neither endowment nor technology: the design of the procedure and the allocation of who bears the cost are what settle what can be built on that land. And the regulator has already declared what the number ought to be: 150 days. The present 61 months can be read as the degree of attainment against that target.

There is, however, a floor that institutions cannot shorten. The delivery time for large power transformers has stretched from under a year before the pandemic to a standard of three years now and a maximum of five. On the industry series it went from 50 weeks in 2021 to 128 weeks in the second quarter of 2025, with generator step-up transformers at 144 weeks.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-33">33</a></sup> The procedure is a variable; the two and a half to three years of physics underneath it is not. And that floor is common to the whole world.

The implication for an investor comes down to the choice of indicator. Ranking countries directly by speed of connection cannot at present be done from published data. The United States publishes times required, the United Kingdom offers connection dates, the Netherlands publishes geographical exhaustion, and China and the Gulf publish nothing. One therefore works with proxies. Not the capacity in the queue but the proportion of the queue that actually reached energization. Not the gross pipeline but the share held by confirmed contracts. And whether the grid operator has the will to offer a connection date. The last is probably the most eloquent. An operator that can offer a connection date knows its own queue.

## Objections and Limits
### Figures 8-1 and 8-2 are not measured values

Both are original schematics to this thesis. The thickness of the ribbons in Figure 8-1 is the author's ordinal judgment about how much of a term a place carries in the world, not a measured share. The levels in Figure 8-2 are likewise ordinal judgments. The two figures do not claim quantities. They claim an ordering. Where the ranking could change places, I have said so at each point in the body. That several shapes laid over one another fill the pentagon in Figure 8-2 carries one further assumption: that the best value for each term inside a group is actually available to the rest of it, which is to say that the chain holds. As 8.12 says, that assumption does not last forever.

### The blank on the Gulf (8.13)

The absence of information most damaging to this thesis's claim is that for China and the Gulf no published statistics exist that measure the period from application to energization. The United States publishes times required, the United Kingdom offers connection dates and the Netherlands publishes geographical exhaustion, but the two regions on which this thesis places its strongest expectations are the only ones publishing nothing. The speed of construction can be inferred from various reports, but being said to be fast and being measured as fast are different things. And since transformer delivery times are common to the whole world, the floor should be the same unless one holds a dedicated procurement network. Until this blank is filled, this thesis's assessment of the Gulf has not been tested on the same ground as the other regions.

### Do not generalize from ERCOT (8.13)

ERCOT's average of 24 months is not a case of slow procedures being made fast. That grid lies outside federal regulatory jurisdiction and was designed this way from the start. What it shows is therefore that the design of the procedure settles the time required, not that changing institutions can shorten it. And speed has its price. Since grid reinforcement is not done in advance, the curtailment risk is borne by the developer. Queue risk has not vanished; it has been moved across into curtailment risk. Which of the two is preferable has a different answer depending on the project's financing structure.

### This thesis has withdrawn its initial reading on China's renewables (8.6)

The reading that overinvestment comes back as stranded assets was not supported when I went to the numbers. Over the process in which installed capacity quadrupled, the curtailment rate in fact fell, and the levelized cost of generation is the cheapest in the world. The criticism this thesis retains is on four points, all of them duller than the original story. The reported utilization rate counts only curtailment for reasons on the grid, and economic curtailment caused by falling prices sits outside it. In mid-2024 the National Energy Administration stopped publishing the province-by-province monthly utilization series, during a period when it was falling. The capacity factor is about 22 percent for wind and about 13 percent for solar against 34 and 24 percent in the United States, so China holds roughly 4.7 times the American wind and solar capacity while generating about 2.7 times the electricity;<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-23">23</a></sup> that the same gigawatt does not mean the same electricity is the point most often missed when national strength is compared by installed capacity. And the change to the revenue regime in 2025 moved the stranding risk from the existing fleet to the marginal project. This withdrawal is not, however, a claim that there is nothing wrong with China's renewable investment. The stranding risk sits not in the existing fleet but in the marginal projects built after the change to the revenue regime in 2025. And the general problem of asset specificity works regardless of the system. The concern recorded in 5.6 about the AI capital expenditure proceeding in the middle of a market economy is of the same form.

### The five terms carry no axis for cheap labor

As 8.4 says, labor input is still filled in part by labor cheap enough that neither deep tech nor large capital need be bought. This chapter does not make that a term because the input does not accumulate and the point at which it is replaced is in sight. Figures 8-1 and 8-2 therefore understate the part of the Gulf's construction cost and the two deltas' manufacturing cost that this input carries. If the replacement runs slower than this chapter reads it, the present position of both is stronger than the figures show.

### This chapter does not treat geopolitics

The contest of power between states, alliances, deterrence and the succession of hegemony are not treated. The map of this chapter can therefore be overwritten by changes in power. I wrote "what comes after victory" in 8.12 in order to show where that overwriting could occur, from the side of the variables rather than the side of power. This thesis deliberately leaves blank a subject that requires an independent book of its own.

### Whether coastal concentration is still increasing is disputed (8.2)

The paper by Kummu and colleagues cited in the body shows that the band within 100 kilometers of the coast and below 100 meters produces 42 percent of world GDP, and concludes at the same time that the common belief in a continuing accumulation of people and wealth along coasts is not supported by its results. The population share of that band is close to flat from 1900 to 2010. In the tighter bands of 10 to 50 kilometers, concentration is observed. What this thesis rests on is the level, not the trend.

### On geographic determinism (8.2)

Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson showed that among regions colonized by Europeans the places that were rich in 1500 are the poor ones now, and argued that geography cannot explain this reversal. They also write, however, that relative prosperity in 1500 may itself have been geographic. What this thesis attributes to geography is initial conditions and the cost of transport, not the quality of institutions. That distinction is why institutions stand as an independent term in the equation of Chapter 1.

### Urban scaling laws depend on where the boundary is drawn (8.3)

Arcaute and colleagues showed that redrawing city boundaries thousands of ways for England and Wales returns most indicators to linearity and leaves the non-linear exponents unstable. What this thesis uses is the direction, that the physical quantity per head falls as density rises, and not the values of the exponents.

### This is not a claim that restricting migration does nothing (8.3)

Walls and border enforcement do reduce inflows. The estimated effects are small and the costs are high. The claim here is not that restriction fails, but that what restriction changes is mainly the form of the flow rather than its direction.
