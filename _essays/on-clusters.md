## 8.1 The cluster, not the nation

The body of the world economy is not the nation but the cluster. A cluster is a bundle of metropolitan areas made continuous by economic activity, by commuting and trade and the movement of people, rather than by administrative boundary.

Cut the world's land by the continuity of its lights at night and the lumps of the economy come out on their own. The 40 megaregions Florida and his colleagues extracted this way hold 17.7 percent of the world's population, and out of them come 66 percent of world output and 85.6 percent of its patents.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

Agglomeration has not come apart, even as the cost of communication approaches zero and economic life moves into virtual space. What draws people is density: get up in the morning, walk to a cafe, and you meet a founder or an investor you have never met.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup>

Working from home in the 2020s was a natural experiment on this structure. Of those who left the downtowns of the twelve largest American metropolitan areas, 58 percent moved to suburbs of the same metropolitan area and 4 percent moved to rural areas.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> The concentration of tech employment in the top eight metropolitan areas rose, from 35.2 percent in 2010 to 38.4 percent in 2020. Embodied discovery, and the cocreation that happens between adjacent cities, have not yet been reproduced in virtual space.

The clusters this thesis takes up are nine. The San Francisco Bay Area, Texas, BosWash, the UAE, Northeast Asia (Japan-Korea-Taiwan), Loxbridge, the Yangtze Delta, the Pearl River Delta (GBA), and Bengaluru-Hyderabad. Drawn at their most generous, these nine come to about a quarter of world GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

And seen in this unit, the argument about the social contract changes shape too. Taxation, elections and distribution all look as though they close at the border. But the places where value is made are far finer than the border, and the range over which talent and capital move is far coarser than it.

## 8.2 The center of gravity has gone to sea

As of the twenty-first century, most clusters are arranged along the rim of the Indo-Pacific. Or rather, the cities along the rim of the Indo-Pacific are the ones that became clusters.

More than eighty percent of world merchandise trade by volume, and more than seventy percent by value, moves by sea.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> Access to the world market, to trade, is what the ocean supplies. And the physical substrate this thesis treats, the transformers and turbines and reactors and building materials, is all on the heavy side. Distance from a port is, directly, distance to construction. Against that, the band within 100 kilometers of the coast and below 100 meters of elevation is 9 percent of the world's land. On it live 27 percent of the world's people, and out of it comes 42 percent of world GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> More than half of the cities above ten million people sit inside that band.

And over the past fifty years the center of gravity of that band has moved to the Indo-Pacific. Take the littoral states of the Indo-Pacific together and they hold 69 percent of the world's people and produce 66 percent of world GDP at purchasing power parity. In 1980 the figure was 53 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> On the other side of the same structure is a disadvantage. The 32 landlocked developing countries hold 7 percent of the world's population and 1.2 percent of its exports. Their development level is about 20 percent lower, and about half of that gap runs through the trade channel.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup>

Diamond argued that crops and domesticated animals spread only along latitudes, so that on a continent laid out east to west technique travelled fast.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> The shape of the earth dealt the initial conditions, with no reference to the effort of the people living on it. The present-day equivalent is the fact that heavy things go by sea. That the merchant cities strung along the rim of the largest ocean on earth grew giant is not a difference of effort; they were dealt that condition first.

The relative retreat of Europe, Russia and the Eurasian interior, the running out of breath in Latin America, and the slowness of the interior of Africa to take off, are one structure appearing in different places. In the language of Chapter 1, what the two shores of the Atlantic hold is inventory; where building is happening is the rim of the Indo-Pacific.

## 8.3 Migration is substantially urbanization across a border

People gather into cities in search of a better job opportunity, better wages, a better standard of living, better serendipity.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> When that is done across a border we call it migration, but in substance it is urbanization.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> Inside this structure, the motive of a young Cameroonian heading for Paris and the motive of a young Russian from the provinces heading for Moscow are the same motive.

The pull points toward the city partly because the city takes less physical quantity per head. Double a city's population and output goes up 2.2 times while the electrical cable needed goes up only 1.8 times.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> The physical quantity needed to keep one person falls as density rises.

For several hundred years the world's population has been structurally headed toward the city, and agglomeration into clusters is what is to be expected from here. Border control can slow the speed of it; it cannot structurally dam the flow.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup>

The pull has a clock on the supply side. Fertility in the countryside runs up to twice the urban rate,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup> and that difference is what sends people to the city. But a countryside that keeps sending loses its women aged 20 to 39. What is moving is the reproductive base itself, so the countryside ages, births fall, and it empties out.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup>

What settles the population of a cluster is not the fertility rate of its country but the gradient. That a country's population is falling and that the cluster inside it is shrinking are two different propositions.

After several centuries of this, that the world's people might sit inside a few thousand megaregions would not be surprising.

## 8.4 Not countries but the terms of the equation

First, a recap of the equation derived in Chapter 1, taken down to the grain at which a place can actually be observed.

<p class="equation">Civilizational competitiveness C ≈ Institutions I × Effective energy abundance E × Labor input L</p>
<p class="equation">L ≈ Deep tech T × Large capital K</p>
**I, institutions.** On the side that calls in capital: ownership, investor protection, the tax regime and the speed of permitting, which comes down to whether the return on capital placed there can be believed to be preserved for the life of the asset. On the side that calls in people: that failure is allowed, social inclusiveness, and whether residency is detached from the employer.

**E, effective energy abundance.** How far a place has come toward power that can be drawn without watching the meter. As Chapter 5 showed, what binds a decision is not the level of the price but the sense that the supply is finite.

**T_1, deep tech in bits.** Foundation models and deep learning research, the proprietary data they are trained on, and the technology that settles physical-side production while holding no physical form of its own: the design tools and the design IP for chips. This is the chokepoint of direction 2 in Chapter 2.

**T_2, deep tech in the physical.** Materials, semiconductor production equipment, lithography, leading-edge logic, high-bandwidth memory, industrial robots, precision motors. This is the chokepoint of direction 1 in Chapter 2.

**K, large capital.** A balance that can be moved, on a single decision, at a CAPEX of a different order.

Of the Chapter 1 equation, the labor input splits into deep tech and large capital, and deep tech splits into the two directions of Chapter 2. Labor input does, however, still have a third way of being filled: labor cheap enough that neither deep tech nor large capital need be bought. That route, filled by hands, still works widely on the present earth, and it tells in the construction speed of the Gulf states and in the manufacturing cost of China's two delta regions. But as 2.7 showed, the point where the price of the robot crosses under the price of that route is already in sight. Cheap labor is an input that is working now, not an asset for the future.

What settles a cluster's standing is not how many terms it has assembled for itself but whether it can reach the ones it lacks. This chapter calls the bundle of those reaching relations a techno-bloc. There are at present only two techno-blocs that actually have the terms assembled. The American techno-bloc assembles them by division of labor; the Chinese techno-bloc tries to assemble them by vertical integration. The third candidate is India, which so far belongs to neither way of assembling them.

What holds back the implementation of abundance on the American side is the speed at which gigawatts are connected to the grid and buildings are put up. These two are hard to divide up. Neither electricity nor land can be exported. If the division-of-labor structure wins, therefore, it wins only in the case where computation and capital can move to the clusters that are strong on the physical side. If the Chinese vertical integration wins, it wins only in the case where it holds every term inside, and then its greatest weakness becomes demand.

## 8.5 The American techno-bloc

The essence of the American techno-bloc is not that one country holds every term but that a division of labor holds. The chain does not function if any one link is pulled out. The first plate of Figure 8-2 shows the shape. Every one of the five clusters is a lopsided shape pulled out along a single direction. Yet laid over one another the five very nearly fill the pentagon. Not one of them is complete, and put together the gaps disappear.

**The United States.** The terms are most nearly complete here. It holds the world's strongest foundation models and computing resources and enormous capital, and Texas supplies abundant energy. On the physical side it holds a corner of the production equipment while falling short in manufacturing capacity of its own for leading-edge technology; what is missing arrives through alliance and capital. And on institutions it is extremely strong on both faces of the pull, the one that calls in capital and the one that calls in people.

**The GCC.** Its strength is capital, the construction capability underwritten by abundant energy and by location, and a sandbox for innovation: the one place where the three conditions of Chapter 1 are assembled in the present era. Barakah's 5.6 gigawatts, solar at 1.35 cents per kilowatt-hour which was a world record at the time, and Abu Dhabi's 5-gigawatt-class AI campus (Chapter 1). What the GCC carries is not a technological term but the flow of capital between terms, a node that belongs to every pole while trading with all of them.

There is one more input that does not show up well in the figures: the labor that goes into construction. The labor input of the Gulf states is still carried by large-scale, low-cost unskilled migration. Building costs there are low because people hired at the wage level of the sending country are building inside the price level of the receiving one. Neither Europe nor Northeast Asia has labor at that price, and neither can match the Gulf on construction capability.

In the Gulf states the soil for growing at home the deep tech that would automate cheap labor has still not formed. Both AI and the physical substrate are therefore handled by buying them with capital, and buying it keeps the labor input close to full. It is also the shortest-lived part of the Gulf states' advantage.

The UAE recognizes this weakness, and to create an ecosystem that produces deep tech it is widening the institutions that call in capital and people. The golden visa introduced in 2019 detaches residency from the employer and grants investors, researchers and professionals a self-contained ten-year right of stay; it is an institutional change aimed at the revolving-door structure itself.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> But a few years from enactment, neither the median length of stay nor whether a second generation stays on can yet be observed.

What counts in the production function of an innovation corridor is a particular stratum: frontier researchers, zero-to-one founders, deep engineers. What this stratum uses to choose where to move is not only a tax rate but the density of the cluster itself: the probability of running into somebody smarter than you every week by accident, the confidence that if you fail the next job is within walking distance, the number of peers you can argue with at midnight. This is why the San Francisco Bay Area does not let go of the top tier of talent despite high taxes and a high cost of living. The foreign population of the Gulf states is still, in essence, a revolving door, and whether the cluster matures is what settles the rest.

**Japan-Korea-Taiwan.** Japan carries chemicals and materials, machinery, industrial robots, semiconductor production equipment, automobiles, and the advanced industries that hang off them. Korea carries semiconductors and electronics, and shipbuilding. Taiwan carries the AI semiconductor itself. Only the three together constitute the physical-side supply chain of physical AI. The role of Japan-Korea-Taiwan is not the brain but the body, which is to say the very capacity to implement direction 1 seen in Chapter 2. But they hold no large foundation model of their own. Their fate is therefore settled not by their own demand but by the demand of the stack they are built into.

Holding the upstream of that leading-edge physical supply chain is also a bargaining card, and as parties for the American stack to hand computing resources or orbital infrastructure to, Northeast Asia is at the top of the list. On the capital-calling side, institutions are high enough as well. Against that, the region is structurally set to run out of talent over the long run as its population falls, while the social inclusiveness on the side that calls in people is extremely low. And effective energy abundance is structurally low, dependent on imports, with a tight grid. For Northeast Asia, therefore, the implementation of next-generation energy, fusion among it, and the admission of high-skilled talent are not options but preconditions. If they go unfilled, the region remains one that holds the most complex and deepest technological supply chain in the world and cannot run it at home.

**England.** England sits on the innermost rim of the first pole. What London and the Oxbridge corridor supply is the intelligence of basic research, and England is also the party to which America grants top-level access first. But that intelligence is inclined to leak. The emblem of it is the fact that the most important AI research organization this corridor produced ended up under American capital. It is the same structure as the capital flight argued in Chapter 6.

## 8.6 The Chinese techno-bloc

The China techno-bloc holds an independent AI stack. Its essence is not a division of labor but vertical integration.

The advantage is speed of decision and resilience against a cut in supply; the defect is that if any one of the terms is structurally low, it becomes the bottleneck for the whole. What is actually behind is leading-edge logic alone, and it is behind because of sanctions. But the lag is not as large in computing capacity actually running as the generation gap in lithography would suggest. Separation of the supply chain from the American techno-bloc will proceed further, and technically the aim will be to complete a domestic stack inside a closed ecosystem.

Labor input has historically been carried by sheer weight of hands. That is being replaced, fast, by the expansion of physical AI. Effective energy abundance, too, has been built at the largest scale in the world.

Against that, institutions are blocked on both faces, capital and people. On capital, inward direct investment has turned down amid economic-security concerns, and local government finances are strained by shrinking land revenue.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup> The side that calls in people is thinner still. On top of the difficulty of language and social integration, residency is not detached from the employer and the route to permanent settlement is effectively closed. There are constraints on taking assets out. No pronounced movement of the world's high-skilled talent into China is observed at present. And the side that distributes the wealth produced to the population is not working. Wealth is not distributed, so households do not become wealthy, people are in surplus, they tire of involution, youth unemployment stays high, births collapse, demand shrinks, and the oversupply deepens further.

**Not a country but a continent.** The body of it is the coast. The Yangtze Delta has a population of about 240 million, more than half the European Union, and a GDP of about 4.65 trillion dollars, larger than Japan as a whole. The Pearl River Delta has a population of about 86 million and a GDP of about 2.1 trillion dollars. These two regions alone account for about 38 percent of China's GDP.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

The Yangtze Delta assembles, in balance, the AI model ecosystems of Hangzhou and Hefei, the machine manufacturing of Suzhou, and the capital and finance of Shanghai. The Pearl River Delta, on the axis of Shenzhen, is strong in the hardware and manufacture of physical AI. China's computation, manufacture and capital are gathered in these two.

Beijing holds an AI model ecosystem of its own, built around the link between industry and the universities, but its strength lies less in producing a pole than in turning out the people and administering the pole politically.

The objective functions of the two deltas do not necessarily coincide with Beijing's. The crackdown on Alibaba that began in 2020 is one instance; telling a hardware engineer in Shenzhen or an AI engineer in Hangzhou to develop inside a stack closed off from the world is political compulsion, not an engineer's natural choice. That fact puts a gradient of institutions across the clusters: the further from the grip of the center, the higher the score, if only slightly. Some of the people who graduate from Beijing's universities move to Hangzhou or Shenzhen, and some of those tired of the competition go to Chengdu.

**Physical AI does not solve the contraction of demand.** Over the coming decades what happens in China is a selection: people vanish from the countryside, gather in the cities, and in the process winners and losers are settled fairly plainly region by region.

An oversupplied China has only three roads left: expand domestic demand, expand exports, or cut supply back. Japan took thirty years over that structural adjustment, and the exit came not from policy but from the side of population structure, in the form of a labor shortage. China is attempting to pass through these thirty years on a far lower GDP per capita than Japan's and on a far thinner accumulation. And labor, too, is in oversupply.

As derived in 2.7, what is scarce in a world where supply is automated is not the capacity to supply but demand, and productive capacity nobody can be sold to is not an asset but a liability. And demand is a function of population. Weighting toward supply becomes a self-reinforcing trap. Oversupply produces unemployment, unemployment kills births, falling births kill future demand, and vanished demand produces further oversupply.

**The three northeastern provinces foreshadow the whole country.** Liaoning's births in 2024 were 180,000, a birth rate of 4.32 per thousand, far below even China's national rate of 5.63 per thousand in 2025. The resident population of the three provinces fell by roughly 12.5 million over the ten years from 2014, and part of that is not death but outflow.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-18">18</a></sup> Industry sank, the young left, the remaining population aged, births collapsed, housing stopped carrying a price, and public finance stopped turning. The population that disappeared did not disappear from China; it moved to the Yangtze Delta and the Pearl River Delta. Seen at the level of the country it is population decline; seen at the level of the cluster it is relocation.

**The catch-up model stops working.** China was fast to here not because it ran on its own but because it held a machine for catching up. Every time America changed the game, the state pointed the direction, poured in subsidy, sent industrial spies, formed joint ventures with German and Japanese firms dazzled by the size of the domestic market and drew the IP out of them, and, on the stampede of demand that population scale makes possible, put up the infrastructure first and disregarded the return on it. The geniuses flowing back from the United States counted too. That combination produced explosive growth in a straight line.

That machine is coming apart in several places at once. China is now a target of Western economic security, so neither capital nor technology arrives as it did; even Japan has stood up an intelligence agency and tightened its monitoring. The population is shrinking, so neither the return flow of geniuses nor that stampede of demand is easy to reproduce. As seen in Chapter 2, talent is an input factor of direction, and what must be asked is not the total but the thickness of the pool from which selection is made: researchers chosen from among some 20 million young people a year and researchers chosen once that pool has become 5 million a year are not the same. Before that, young people worn out by excessive competition have begun stepping off it. Population decline and deflation obscure the outlook, so investment thins; aging swells social security, so subsidy thins. And once a country settles into stagnation it can no longer ignore the return. Being able to ignore it was its single greatest weapon. When America next changes the game, the stamina to follow will be smaller than it was.

The United States has evaded this problem by gathering geniuses from all over the world, but the premise for attracting talent is institutions and freedom. A fortress is not an architecture well suited to gathering geniuses. On top of that, inside the US-China rivalry the American stack takes on the mission of rebuilding an independent set of manufacturing sites. The further that goes, the colder the winter for Chinese manufacturing.

**A wealthy China or a strong China.** There is an exit. If energy is abundant, automation advances, and the wealth produced explodes, and if that wealth comes to be distributed naturally, then individuals become wealthy and the fertility rate reverses. China is now at the brink of deciding which it will make real first: the strengthening of supply capacity, or the wealth of the individual. On my own observation, China looks to be giving up on a wealthy China and moving to a strong China. If not repeating the century of humiliation is something close to a national creed, then where this ends is a fortified state with extremely strong supply capacity. If that is so, I judge the probability of failure to be high. It is precisely because personal wealth becomes abundant that people reproduce their population and demand grows.

Having watched the population of my own country, Japan, fall throughout my life, I may be permitted this much: the least visible loss in a falling population is that the resource called youth, the part of the population that starts change, disappears from the society as a whole.

## 8.7 The Indian techno-bloc

India has the conditions of a pole assembled. The magnetism of three thousand years of civilizational accumulation. The world's largest population. The ease of integration into Western supply chains that comes with being English-speaking. Competitiveness in IT services, pharmaceuticals and space. And the legitimacy of democracy. The diplomatic doctrine of strategic autonomy is likewise a declaration of intent not to be wholly subordinate to any pole.

But India's demographic dividend is correct as a statement about the country as a whole and already incorrect as a statement about clusters.

According to the Sample Registration System of the Registrar General of India, the total fertility rate of the five southern states is 1.3 in Kerala, 1.3 in Tamil Nadu, 1.4 in Andhra Pradesh, 1.5 in Karnataka and 1.5 in Telangana. All India is 1.9. Bihar, by contrast, is 2.8 and Uttar Pradesh 2.6.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-19">19</a></sup> The demographic dividend is in the north, and it is no longer in the south, which is the economic center.

Norway's total fertility rate in 2024 was 1.44, and all five southern states are below it. Yet income per head is roughly 4,700 dollars in Karnataka, 4,500 in Tamil Nadu and 3,800 in Kerala. Norway's GDP per capita is 94,594 dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-19">19</a></sup> The same fertility rate, achieved on a twentieth of the income. The proposition that fertility decline arrives before wealth is already complete in southern India.

The reasons for reading India as never becoming an independent pole narrow to two: brain drain, and the absence of a full-stack semiconductor industry. The first is that the pool for selection is thick while its top layer keeps being drawn off to other clusters. The second is that stacking foundation-model technology without holding leading-edge physical technology leaves the ceiling set from outside.

There are therefore two branches: a road on which India aims at an AI stack of its own, and a road on which it settles inside the American stack. This thesis's reading leans to the latter. An independent stack demands a leading-edge physical supply chain, and that demands decades of accumulation, enormous capital, and above all the settling of talent. India converges not on an independent pole but on a regional great power with a sphere of influence inside the American techno-bloc. The time frame is the 2040s to the 2050s.

## 8.8 Europe

If fusion is implemented, the side of effective energy abundance may be solved. But on the automation side there is no technological asset equivalent to what Japan-Korea-Taiwan holds.

What comes out highest for Europe is leading-edge physical technology, pulled up by the Netherlands and Germany. The lithography machine, the machine tools and the industrial robots are real, and on this one axis Europe stands on the same ground as China's two deltas. But it does not reach Japan-Korea-Taiwan, and the reason is the depth of the supply-chain layers. Materials, production equipment, lithography, leading-edge logic, high-bandwidth memory, precision motors: the only region where all of them are assembled inside one place is Japan-Korea-Taiwan. Europe is strong at particular steps and does not hold every layer.

On effective energy abundance the contribution comes from the Nordics, with hydro and geothermal, and from France, with nuclear. On foundation models too, sovereign-AI development has had a degree of success. Taken as a region, though, Europe is not on the same ground as Texas, the UAE or China's two deltas.

And there is a problem on the institutional side as well. Regulatory density is part of institutions, and it bears directly on both the preservation of returns and the speed of permitting. High industrial electricity, slow construction and heavy regulation are not separate problems. They are three faces of one term.

Loxbridge is BosWash at a smaller scale. Institutions, capital and research, with no energy and nothing physical: the same shape, differing only in size. France, Germany, the Netherlands and the Nordics do not overlap even with that. That Loxbridge is the region's only cluster follows from the same shape.

## 8.9 The global south

The twentieth-century development model was a single sequence. There is cheap labor; manufacturing agglomerates there; a middle class is born; a domestic consumption market forms; industry moves upmarket. Japan, Korea, Taiwan and China all climbed that staircase. The sequence is now breaking from two sides at once.

First, fertility decline arrives before wealth. Vietnam's total fertility rate has already fallen below 2, and urban areas are below 1.5. GDP per capita at that point is about 5,100 dollars. Korea and Taiwan met the same level of fertility at a stage past 10,000 dollars per head, and Vietnam is entering the same problem at roughly half that level. Indonesia too is at about 5,100 dollars per head, and urban Java is falling sharply.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-20">20</a></sup> The framework of Chapter 4 applies directly. It used to be that wealth came first and the price structure throttled births afterwards. Now only the price structure is imported first. Urbanization pushes up housing and education costs, and the smartphone carries in the lifestyle of the advanced countries and the sense of opportunity cost at the same time. Income has not yet arrived, but the denominator of household Malthus arrives ahead of it.

Second, manufacturing does not come in the first place. The entrance to the twentieth-century sequence was cheap labor. But in a world where the robot is cheaper, cheap labor does not call in investment. As seen in Chapter 2, the automation of direction 1 has not stopped once in two hundred years. The largest reason Bangladesh or Ethiopia cannot become the next Vietnam is neither politics nor geography but that the labor-intensiveness of manufacturing is itself being lost.

The model of becoming wealthy by making use of a demographic dividend has a high probability of ending with Vietnam and Indonesia as the last generation, and even they sit on the near side of the trap. This is not the failure of one region but the end of the twentieth-century development model itself.

And an ironic map appears. The demographic dividend lasts longest in sub-Saharan Africa, which can enjoy it from the 2030s into the 2060s. But it does not have the institutions and infrastructure to make use of it. Where the institutions are, fertility decline has already begun. In the global south plate of Figure 8-2, for Indonesia, Vietnam, Nigeria and Kenya none of the five terms is yet standing.

In 2024 a currency devaluation shrank Nigeria's dollar GDP by about 47 percent and it lost its position as Africa's largest economy. The more structural number is on the power side. For a population of 237 million, average generation output stands at roughly 4,100 megawatts against 13,625 megawatts of grid-connected capacity, and the product of the three terms of Chapter 1 is structurally low.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-21">21</a></sup> As for East Africa, outlooks have been offered for twenty years, and this thesis has not been able to confirm a record of grid connections that bears them out. The gap with Vietnam appears not as a difference of political system or of population but as a difference in state execution capacity, which is to say the capacity to connect gigawatts to the grid and put up buildings.

South America and sub-Saharan Africa, while settling the world's future in population, are placed outside the stack. As Figure 4-6 in Chapter 4 shows, a little over three tenths of the world's children are now born in sub-Saharan Africa. And as section 5.7 showed, in a world where supply is automated the scarce thing is demand. The region where the demographic dividend lasts longest is also the only region where head-count is still rising during exactly the forty years in which head-count is the scarce factor. What is missing is purchasing power, and purchasing power is a function of income, and income is a function of whether you are inside the stack. This is a region that is an object of investment and, on the right conditions, a party with a bargaining position.

And if the cost of energy and automation really does collapse, the first and largest beneficiary ought to be not the frontier but the societies now made to pay the highest effective prices. The ethical meaning of the cascade hangs here.

## 8.10 Orbit

For the twenty-first century at least, habitation off Earth is economically inefficient. Life support is the use that demands energy, land and labor all at the highest unit price. What this thesis claims is not habitation but that the use of space will proceed at overwhelming speed. The order begins with data centers in orbit; generation follows, then the automation and acceleration of transport, and production sites go up last. In the terms of the equation, an orbital infrastructure business is built out of T_2 and K, and what it sells is E.

The reason is that in orbit the conditions of energy differ from those on the ground in two respects. Sunlight arrives unobstructed, and there is no queue for grid connection. The reliability of supply is if anything harsher than on the ground, but if what sets the speed is lead time, then a place with no queue is structurally strong.

The largest constraint in orbit is not energy but waste heat. A vacuum has no medium to move heat into. Waste heat can only be radiated as infrared, and the quantity radiated is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature under the Stefan-Boltzmann law.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-22">22</a></sup> 

A double-sided radiator at 20 degrees Celsius with an emissivity of 0.85 sheds 712 watts per square meter. But the International Space Station's ammonia radiators manage 166 watts per square meter, less than a fifth of the ideal. Putting one gigawatt of computation in orbit therefore needs a radiating area of about 1.4 square kilometers on the ideal figure and about 6.0 on the measured one. And mass is a heavier constraint than area. Extrapolate an ISS-class design and the radiator comes to about 100 tonnes per megawatt against about 10 tonnes for the computing racks, an order of magnitude more. One gigawatt means a hundred thousand tonnes, and at a hundred tonnes per launch for current heavy rockets that is a thousand launches of radiator alone. On present designs, putting gigawatt-class computation in orbit is a business of launching radiators rather than computers.

There are two ways through: temperature, and the form of the radiator. Radiation tells as the fourth power of absolute temperature, so raising the operating point from 20 to 120 degrees Celsius alone cuts the required area to under a third. The bottleneck of an orbital data center is not the performance of the semiconductor but how hot the semiconductor still runs at. And in the concept of a liquid-droplet radiator, which replaces the solid panel with droplets of liquid, a figure of 450 watts per kilogram is reported. If that figure is real, shedding one gigawatt takes about 2,200 tonnes, roughly a forty-fifth of the hundred thousand tonnes extrapolated from solid panels, and in launches the difference between a thousand and twenty. That is not a difference of cost but of possible and impossible. Europe's ASCEND sets a timeline of 50 kilowatts demonstrated in 2031 and one gigawatt in 2050. The 50 kilowatts of 2031 is a scale at which the heat problem can be evaded; the real test is on the 2050 side.

Computation in orbit is not physically closed off. But what opens it is not the side of area; it is the side of temperature. And the time frame is not fast enough to make today's energy investment on the ground a stranded asset immediately.

Even so, an asymmetry remains. The more heavily a cluster has invested ahead of others in ground-based energy infrastructure, the greater its downside when this shift begins to bite. And at present the only holder of cheap access to orbit is the American techno-bloc. This is the question of which technology Wright's law of Chapter 5 fires on first, and depending on the answer, the map's center of gravity moves.

## 8.11 The loop nobody owns

Extend the reading to here in a straight line and the first half of the twenty-first century is a phase in which the relative advantage of the American techno-bloc accelerates. China shrinks in relative terms under population decline and a closed ecosystem while keeping the strength of an overwhelming capacity to supply. India converges. Europe lacks technological autonomy.

Where, then, on this map should capital sit? The answer of this chapter is not to pick one cluster. It is to ride the division of the terms itself.

The money comes from the Gulf states, and above all from the sovereign wealth of the UAE, which holds large capital and effective energy abundance at the world's highest level and stands on the buying side of deep tech. That money goes to the side that holds deep tech. On the bits side, the foundation models and AI basic research of the San Francisco Bay Area, Texas and BosWash; on the physical side, the leading-edge physical supply chain of Japan-Korea-Taiwan, and the next generation of nuclear reactors. Most of what is invested in is startups, but not only startups: large companies holding a technology, individuals holding an idea and the IP to it, university and corporate laboratories and studios. If the counterparty holds a term, the legal form it arrives in does not matter.

And then those same parties choose the UAE as the place to run their physical experiments. That is how the loop closes. Energy is cheap and there is no need to watch the meter: not only fossil fuel but effectively unlimited sunlight. Regulation is light and land is spare. Physical AI, energy, robotics. Whether a technology that has left the laboratory works on top of an actual city can only be found out where those three conditions are assembled at once. That is what makes Dubai and Abu Dhabi the places on earth where implementation goes furthest.

The structure works because the map is shaped that way. As Figure 8-1 shows, no place carries every term at world level. If that is so, the return is not in the places that have assembled the terms but on the routes that connect one term to another. Capital leaves from the side of energy and large capital, enters the side of deep tech, and the implementation comes back to the side of energy and institutions. That loop belongs, for now, to no one.

I believe this model works.

## Objections and Limits
### Figures 8-1 and 8-2 are not measured values

Both are original schematics to this thesis. The thickness of the ribbons in Figure 8-1 is the author's ordinal judgment about how much of a term a place carries in the world, not a measured share. The levels in Figure 8-2 are likewise ordinal judgments. The two figures do not claim quantities. They claim an ordering. Where the ranking could change places, I have said so at each point in the body. That several shapes laid over one another fill the pentagon in Figure 8-2 carries one further assumption: that the best value for each term inside a group is actually available to the rest of it, which is to say that the chain holds. As 8.11 says, that assumption does not last forever.

### The five terms carry no axis for cheap labor

As 8.4 says, labor input is still filled in part by labor cheap enough that neither deep tech nor large capital need be bought. This chapter does not make that a term because the input does not accumulate and the point at which it is replaced is in sight. Figures 8-1 and 8-2 therefore understate the part of the Gulf states' construction cost and China's two delta regions' manufacturing cost that this input carries. If the replacement runs slower than this chapter reads it, the present position of both is stronger than the figures show.

### Can China get out of oversupply on demand from outside? (8.6)

The idea of absorbing the supply into Central Asia, Southeast Asia, Pakistan and sub-Saharan Africa has three ceilings. The scale does not match: the whole GDP of sub-Saharan Africa is about two trillion dollars, a little over a tenth of China's, and taking all of those markets still does not fill the hole in the domestic demand of 1.4 billion people. Demand is purchasing power, and purchasing power is a function of income; to sell into markets where the income has not yet arrived is to lend to them, and that Belt and Road lending has shrunk from its 2016 peak with renegotiations running ever since is the cost of this route appearing on the collection side. If the buyer cannot pay, what you hold is not demand but a receivable. And as 8.9 shows, an inflow of cheap goods kills the seedling of that country's own manufacturing: the strategy thins, in the act of selling, the middle class that was to become the future demand. The idea is not itself wrong; the time frames do not match.

### This chapter does not treat geopolitics

The contest of power between states, alliances, deterrence and the succession of hegemony are not treated. The map of this chapter can therefore be overwritten by changes in power. This thesis deliberately leaves blank a subject that requires an independent book of its own.

### Whether coastal concentration is still increasing is disputed (8.2)

The paper by Kummu and colleagues cited in the body shows that the band within 100 kilometers of the coast and below 100 meters produces 42 percent of world GDP, and concludes at the same time that the common belief in a continuing accumulation of people and wealth along coasts is not supported by its results. The population share of that band is close to flat from 1900 to 2010. In the tighter bands of 10 to 50 kilometers, concentration is observed. What this thesis rests on is the level, not the trend.

### On geographic determinism (8.2)

Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson showed that among regions colonized by Europeans the places that were rich in 1500 are the poor ones now, and argued that geography cannot explain this reversal. They also write, however, that relative prosperity in 1500 may itself have been geographic. What this thesis attributes to geography is initial conditions and the cost of transport, not the quality of institutions. That distinction is why institutions stand as an independent term in the equation of Chapter 1.

### Urban scaling laws depend on where the boundary is drawn (8.3)

Arcaute and colleagues showed that redrawing city boundaries thousands of ways for England and Wales returns most indicators to linearity and leaves the non-linear exponents unstable. What this thesis uses is the direction, that the physical quantity per head falls as density rises, and not the values of the exponents.

### This is not a claim that restricting migration does nothing (8.3)

Walls and border enforcement do reduce inflows. The estimated effects are small and the costs are high. The claim here is not that restriction fails, but that what restriction changes is mainly the form of the flow rather than its direction.
