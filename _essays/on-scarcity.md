## 3.1 The 1970s as a fault line

The slowing of growth across the advanced economies over this past half-century is not the middle-income trap that stalls developing countries but something better called an advanced-country trap. Of the three terms, the one that broke was energy.

Coal, then oil, then natural gas: each time a new and more efficient source of energy was found and its cost brought down, the advanced countries turned round and set limits on their own use of it. The period from the beginning of the energy transition in the 1970s to the present may well have been a kind of transitional age. The move off oil after the oil shock, environmental regulation, the shift to renewables, nuclear phase-out in some countries, price management by OPEC+. These overlapped, and the balance of energy supply and demand broke down in ways nobody intended. Energy prices then ran for decades above the level that the available technology and the available resource base would otherwise have implied. In most advanced importing countries the cost of the energy transition ended up carried by households over a long horizon, the cost of living crept up faster than real wages, and disposable income was shaved away.

The problem is not the quantity of energy as such. It is what the effective price of energy permits a household to do and what it forbids. Fertility, housing and education all sit on that list of prohibitions.
