## 2.1 One substrate, two directions

The progress of civilization is made of one substrate and two directions of development. The substrate is energy, and the two directions ride on top of it. Not two axes placed side by side, but two layers stacked.

Direction 1 is productivity in the physical world. Turning energy into momentum, building machines, transporting, constructing, travelling farther. The goal of moving matter with less effort and at lower cost has not changed since the industrial revolution. Jensen Huang divides AI into five layers: energy, chips, infrastructure, models, and applications.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> I agree with placing energy at the bottom. But seen from the side of civilization, two layers suffice: energy, and the applications that run on it. The layers in between rise and fall as means to that goal.

Direction 2 is productivity in the world of bits. A lineage that begins with language and continues through writing, printing, the telegraph, and the telephone: development on the side where mass is minimal. Solutions here do not necessarily take physical form. We imagined a future in which a shopping robot took our order and came back with the goods; what delivered it was Amazon, a mechanism. At the far end of this direction lies what Yoichi Ochiai calls technology becoming magic, which is to say technology that dissolves into the environment and disappears from view.

## 2.2 The two endgoals

Each of the two directions has an endpoint. For the physical side it is driving the convergence cost of matter configuration to zero; for the virtual side it is driving the convergence cost of predictive models to zero. The entire history of civilization along the axis of efficiency is the asymptotic process of those two falling toward zero.

Why can it be stated in that form? The quickest route is to trace it from the side of direction 2.

The clue lies in the distinction between meaning and symbol. Language and writing are only vehicles for conveying meaning. And what one notices is that what the telegraph, the telephone, the internet, and Zoom have overwhelmingly improved over the past century is not the conveyance of meaning but the fidelity of symbol reproduction.

That intuition coincides exactly with the definition given by the father of information theory. Claude Shannon opened "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" by defining the fundamental problem of communication as "reproducing at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point," and added, deliberately: "Frequently the messages have meaning. (...) These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem".<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> The past century of information technology has been a century of solving exactly the problem Shannon defined, faster, more accurately, and more cheaply. The telegraph reproduces letters, the telephone reproduces sound waveforms, television reproduces images, the internet reproduces arbitrary bit strings. Generative AI is the phase transition in that lineage: what it reproduces is not symbols but the distribution that generates symbols, which is to say a model of the world itself.

> **The physical side drives the convergence cost of matter configuration to zero. The virtual side drives the convergence cost of predictive models to zero.**

The convergence cost of matter configuration (C_config) is the total cost of getting arbitrary matter to an arbitrary place in an arbitrary configuration. Transport, manufacture, construction, agriculture, and the opening of space are all special cases of convergence on a desired configuration of matter, and thermodynamics sets the ceiling on their efficiency. The convergence cost of predictive models (C_pred) is the total cost of establishing to hand, at the required accuracy, a model of the state and behaviour of some system: another person's mind, a market, a protein, a city. Communication, computation, science, education, and AI are all special cases of convergence on a sufficiently good predictive model. The wheel, the sailing ship, the steam engine, the container, and reusable rockets are milestones of the first; language, writing, printing, the telegraph, the internet, and deep learning are milestones of the second.

One limit of scope. What is treated here is only the direction of efficiency. The creation of value in a civilization has another axis entirely unrelated to productivity, one that generates culture and value through the deployment of waste. Festival, art, hospitality, play, expenditure. What happens on that axis once the two directions of efficiency have been fully automated is treated later in this thesis.

## 2.3 The law of competition

The two directions compete over the same problem. And the side with the lower convergence cost wins.

> **The law of competition: for any given problem, a direction-1 solution and a direction-2 solution are potentially in competition, and the one with the lower convergence cost wins. The more the essence of the problem is information processing, the more direction 2 wins; the more matter configuration is unavoidable, the more direction 1 remains.**

The two puzzles of the prologue are its first application. The shopping robot was a direction-1 solution to the problem of shopping: a humanoid machine physically rearranges the configuration of matter. Amazon is a direction-2 solution to the same problem. Most of shopping, meaning search, comparison, ordering, and payment, is not matter configuration but information processing, and Amazon solved that part in virtual space and pushed only the residual matter configuration through the existing logistics network. What decided the outcome was not the superiority of one vision but cost structure. A direction-2 solution replicates at nearly zero marginal cost and is less bound by physical law and safety regulation.

To make this law a tool, one has to be able to measure the mixing ratio before seeing who wins. There is one way to measure it. Decompose the problem into steps and count the mass that must be moved at each step. For shopping, search, comparison, ordering, and payment all move zero mass. The only step that does not is the last leg, from warehouse to doorstep. So this problem could be read in advance as one that would mostly move to direction 2. Apply the same question elsewhere: what Zoom substituted for was the leg of a business trip that carries a human body, and what Uber Eats relocated was the mass-bearing step of cooking, moved from the household to a network. Steps of zero mass migrate to direction 2 sooner or later.

There is, however, a domain in which the two compose rather than compete. Contemporary drug discovery is the type case. Structure prediction, of which AlphaFold is the representative, converges a predictive model of a physical system, the protein, in bit space, and only the narrowed candidates are sent onward to the matter-configuration process of synthesis and trials. As much of the predictive-model component of the problem as possible is moved to direction 2, and only the matter-configuration component is left to direction 1. To place a barrier against excessive expectations: current predicted structures accelerate experimental structure determination but do not replace it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> What is needed is not replacement but a shift in the mixing ratio. The same structure repeats in the training of autonomous vehicles, in sim-to-real in robotics, in materials science, and in the design of fusion reactors. Direction 2 has become the lever that collapses the development cost of direction 1.

## 2.4 Thirty years of asymmetry, and the collision of the 2020s

Capital and talent flowed one-sidedly into direction 2 for thirty years because that side needed less energy. And in the 2020s direction 2 collided head-on with the wall of energy.

As Marc Andreessen declared, software ate the world. The Founders Fund line, "we wanted flying cars, instead we got 140 characters," is correct as an observation of the asymmetry.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> As an explanation of the cause it is insufficient. Of the three properties of direction 2 listed in the previous section, replication at zero marginal cost, low friction, and small energy requirement, only the last varies with the era. In a constrained world capital flows toward the looser constraint. That is all it is, and it is not a moral question.

Then why could improved efficiency not avert the wall? Should consumption not fall as efficiency rises? It does not fall. That improved efficiency instead increases total consumption is the paradox known since William Stanley Jevons pointed it out in *The Coal Question*.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> In the framework of this thesis it is not even a paradox. A fall in convergence cost unlocks applications that were not previously viable, so demand grows by more than the efficiency gain. On the International Energy Agency's estimate, world data centre electricity demand rose 17 percent year on year in 2025 and will roughly double by 2030; for AI-specific facilities, roughly triple over the same period.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> Over that same period, the electricity consumed per AI task has been falling rapidly. Cheaper computation was not saved. It was used more widely, more often, and for heavier purposes.

So the wall came closer. As of 2025 the IEA estimated that world data centre electricity demand would more than double by 2030, to a scale slightly above Japan's entire electricity consumption today. Goldman Sachs put the increase at up to 165 percent by 2030 and later revised it upward to 175.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> However magical direction 2 becomes, somebody has to turn a turbine. This is not the point that virtual worlds also require energy to run. It is the point that the material substrate of civilization, meaning cities, transport, housing, and food, does not move by a single gram in direction 2.

The movement of capital speaks more plainly than forecasts. On 25 August 2025 the Palisades plant in Michigan became the first reactor in American history to return from the decommissioning process to operating status.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> The inventory of who ordered how much generation is left to a later chapter. What this thesis depends on is a single point, the structural change by which computing demand made investment in energy supply politically unavoidable, and as the unprecedented sight of a reactor pulled back out of the decommissioning queue shows, that has already happened.

This is what the present turn actually is. The bet on physical AI and energy is not "the next trend." It is the historical return of capital to the side that was left behind, out of twelve thousand years of two directions.

## 2.5 The industrial revolution never ended

I wrote "return," but only one side had stopped. The pressure toward efficiency itself has not broken once in two hundred years.

What speaks to this most plainly is a completed experiment with two centuries of data behind it: British agriculture. On Wrigley's account, roughly three quarters of the labour force was in agriculture under the Tudors. By the early eighteenth century it was about half, and by 1800 under two fifths. Broadberry and colleagues show the agricultural labour share already down to about 37 percent by 1759. Today agriculture is under 1 percent of the British labour force. And with that under 1 percent Britain produces the equivalent of roughly 60 percent of the food it consumes.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> Two caveats. The remaining 40 percent is externalised to the labour and land of other countries, so the system does not close within Britain alone. And the fall in labour share is mixed with a denominator effect, as other sectors swelled. Even so, the fact that the same crops on the same island can now be grown in greater quantity with under a fortieth of the hands does not move.

What matters is that nowhere in that process is there an end of the revolution. From enclosure through crop rotation, seed improvement, mechanisation, Haber-Bosch fertiliser, the tractor, the combine, and now precision agriculture and automated glasshouses, one and the same pressure, to produce with less effort and at lower cost, worked without interruption for two centuries. The habit of numbering industrial revolutions first, second, third, and fourth is a set of graduations laid retrospectively over that continuum. The graduations change; the sign of the derivative has not changed once.

The size of the current AI boom settles in this light as well. AI is the present form of that continuum, not a rupture. Discourse that narrates it as a rupture stirs excitement and fear while blurring the practical question. What rate-limits the progress of AI? The rupture view prefers to speak of the mystery of algorithms; the continuity view knows the answer. For two hundred years what has rate-limited the speed of automation is energy and capital goods, and this time is the same. There is one question worth asking: does it run inside the energy constraint, or outside it?

## 2.6 The measure of human worth has changed twice

The measure of human worth has changed twice in the past two hundred years. Both times it changed immediately after an externalisation.

**The first change, early nineteenth century.** The externalisation of muscle. Until then human worth was measured mainly by physical capacity in agriculture and manufacture. Labourers, sailors, farmhands. Wage structures and conscription examinations alike were measuring the body. Steam and mechanisation replaced most muscular work, and as a result the computational side of the human came to the front. From the 1820s, society began to look at the human brain with the gaze it had used on muscle.

**The second change, from the 1950s.** The externalisation of the brain. Until the 1940s the world's total computing capacity depended almost entirely on the sum of human brains. This is not a metaphor. The English word "computer" was, until the middle of the twentieth century, a job title for a person who computed.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> Those calculating ballistic tables, almanacs, and actuarial values were human computers at rows of desks. After the arrival of the electronic computer, the share of computation carried by human brains fell sharply. On William Nordhaus's measurement, the price-performance of computation has improved by between 1.7 trillion and 76 trillion times since the era of hand cranking.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> No other good in human history has seen its price collapse at that speed.

Here is the most important observation of this chapter. The human brain is not an organ designed to maximise computational capacity. It is an organ optimised across multiple purposes: survival, emotion, sociality, intuition. And yet, because muscle was externalised, society placed at the centre of value the work of the head, and specifically only its computational aspect. Which is to say that for two hundred years we have been required, as the macro structure of the economy dictated, to maximise performance on a dimension other than the one we were designed for.

There is corroboration from robotics. Hans Moravec wrote in *Mind Children* (1988) that it is comparatively easy to make a computer perform at adult level on intelligence tests, and difficult or impossible to give it the perceptual and motor skills of a one-year-old. The reason lies in evolutionary time. "The large, highly evolved sensory and motor portions of the human brain encode a billion years of experience. (...) The deliberate process we call reasoning is, I believe, the thinnest veneer of human thought." The proposition is intuitively powerful but has not been subjected to empirical verification, which has been pointed out; this thesis therefore treats it as a tendency claim.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup> Even so, the present sight of white-collar intellectual work being eroded by AI ahead of skilled blue-collar physical work is Moravec's view enacted.

And in a future where computation has been fully externalised, on the ruler of computational volume, differences between humans disappear. The differences themselves do not disappear. Judgment remains, relationships remain, the body remains. What retires is the single ruler that stood in front of them for two hundred years. The argument that this is liberation rather than nihilism is placed later in this thesis.

## 2.7 Rewriting the equation of national power

The contents of the production function are being quietly replaced. Production was once the product of productivity and the working-age population, but as the human input thins, the productivity term is absorbed into the penetration of automation, and the production function converges asymptotically on the product of institutions, effective energy abundance, and labour input. There is no need to erect a new production function. The equation of Chapter 1 is itself the answer.

Classical growth accounting is written Y = A·f(K, L), pushing whatever capital and labour cannot explain into total factor productivity A. As set out in "Objections and Limits" at the end of this chapter, the share of that A explicable by thermodynamic efficiency improvement is, on the most rigorous replication, about a quarter.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> Not the bulk of it. But if a quarter of a nameless residual has now been given a name, the rest may yet get names too.

The measure of national power therefore converges on the same equation. The twentieth-century equation of national power was "population times productivity." The twenty-first-century one is institutions times effective energy abundance times labour input. What can be built, how much can be produced, how strong a country is. The three look like separate questions and are the same product seen from different angles. This is why there is so little point in staring at GDP per capita and lamenting. That is a report card, not a cause.

Three things follow from this replacement.

### One: this is an equation of supply

Of the three terms, effective energy abundance and labour input are both quantities on the supply side. Institutions, too, act on the supply side, in the sense of calling in capital and talent. Demand appears nowhere.

That is not an omission but the central view of this thesis. Bring about a supply revolution and demand follows. Demand is contracting now not because people have lost things they want, but because population, the substance of demand, is contracting under the supply constraint. The prices households face are a function of supply, and births are a function of those prices. The order runs from supply to demand, and it does not run in reverse.

So if the three terms turn, capital arrives afterward. And if the wealth created is properly distributed, population returns and demand returns. A rotation that began on the supply side comes round and reaches demand. Whether that circuit closes is the subject of the latter half of this thesis. There is a cost, however. In a world where supply is automated, what becomes scarce is not productive capacity but demand. Productive capacity nobody can sell to is a liability, not an asset.

### Two: the contents of labour input have been swapped

The method of throwing in large volumes of hands to make things cheaply, which is to say the human-wave approach, ended in China in the 2010s. From the 2020s onward, labour input is another name for automation, and its substance is technology access and large capital. Possessing cheap labour is no longer an entry ticket. If a society sets out to climb the same road now, automation will have gone farther before it catches up. What is asked of this term, therefore, is not population but whether chokepoint technologies are within reach and whether capital expenditure of another order of magnitude can be mobilised.

For the same reason the meaning of talent changes. Talent is no longer an input to production but an input to direction. Once machines carry what to make and how much, what remains to humans is deciding what ought to be made, and seeing possibilities nobody is looking at. So what is asked is not quantity but the depth of the pool from which selection is made, and the power to attract that pool.

### Three: GDP moves away from value

As supply is automated, GDP moves away from the concept of the sum of value created. There are two reasons. First, wealth produced by machines is counted regardless of whom it was useful to. The construction by which only what sells counts as value was an approximation for an era when there were humans on the supply side. Second, because those creating value stop being conscious of productivity. Once human time is released from the direction of productivity, the value that time generates is, by definition, not recorded on an index of productivity. GDP moves away from value not because GDP breaks. It is because human activity moves outside GDP's domain of definition.

## Objections and Limits
The body carries only the bones of the argument. Anticipated objections and how they are handled are gathered here. Each heading indicates the passage in the body it corresponds to.

### Is the law of competition only judgeable after the fact (2.3)

This is the heaviest objection. Does "the more the essence of the problem is information processing, the more direction 2 wins" not amount to identifying the essence after seeing which side won? Shopping looked like a physical problem until Amazon won.

The decomposition by mass in the body is written to answer this. Counting the mass that must be moved at each step can be done before the outcome is known. **But the granularity of the decomposition is itself a judgment.** Cut shopping into search, comparison, ordering, payment, and delivery, or into going to the shop, choosing, and carrying home, and the proportion of zero-mass steps changes. This law is therefore best read as **a tool of classification rather than a tool of prediction.** It does not tell you which side will win; it points to the axis along which the outcome will be decided. The same limit written about the three conditions in Chapter 1 applies here.

### Is the division into two directions itself arbitrary (2.1 and 2.2)

The division into matter configuration and predictive models is neither exclusive nor exhaustive. Which is the production of energy itself? Which is the building of institutions? **This thesis uses the division not as an ontological classification but as a working partition covering only what can be measured on a single scale, convergence cost.** Energy production can be handled as a special case of matter configuration; institutions cannot. That is why institutions are erected as a separate term in Chapter 1. The two directions do not cover the whole of civilization, and no claim is made that they do.

### The proviso that two layers suffice, against the rest of this thesis (2.1)

Section 2.1 collapses five layers into two. Yet Chapter 1 makes chokepoint technology T half of labour input, and the latter half of this thesis argues that holding a particular layer is decisive. **Is it a contradiction to fold layers away and then call them decisive?**

It is not a contradiction, but the risk in how it is written is acknowledged. Two layers is the resolution appropriate to asking where things are headed; five layers is the resolution appropriate to asking where things are. At the former, intermediate layers turn over; at the latter, the location of the intermediate layers is everything. **The same object is being viewed at different resolutions according to the question.** A chapter that does not say so plainly will confuse its reader, which is why the proviso is stated in the body.

### Does this depend on Moravec's paradox (2.6)

It does not, and that is set down here. The claim in the body is that the human brain is not an organ designed for computation, and the general understanding of evolutionary biology is sufficient for that. Moravec is cited only as corroboration. **And the present scene cited as corroboration, that white-collar work is being eroded first, is equally well explained by the availability of training data.** Which of the two is operating cannot be separated at present, and the argument of that section is written so as to survive Moravec being wrong.

### The objection that "energy is only 6 percent of GDP"

American energy expenditure runs between 5.6 and 6.7 percent of nominal GDP (EIA, "U.S. energy spending increased by more than 20% in 2022," *Today in Energy*, August 28, 2024). But a small share for an input that enters the production function of nearly every good, and whose short-run elasticity of substitution is extremely low, is a statement about the size of the damage under a supply shock. The GDP lost to a single day of widespread blackout runs to many tens of times that day's energy expenditure.

The residual in growth accounting accounted for roughly nine tenths of growth in Solow's measurement (Robert M. Solow, "Technical Change and the Aggregate Production Function," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 39(3), 1957, 312–320). Ayres and Warr showed that part of it is improvement in thermodynamic efficiency (Robert U. Ayres & Benjamin Warr, *The Economic Growth Engine*, Edward Elgar, 2009), but the most rigorous replication gives roughly 25 percent of the increase in UK GDP from 1971 to 2013, and this thesis claims no more than that. The bulk of the residual has not disappeared. A quarter of it has been given a name.
