The diagnosis is finished. From here this thesis is about the future.

## 5.1 The traveller with 2GB

Start with a thought experiment. On a week away, with only 2GB of roaming data, we hold back as far as we can. Whether a thing is finite or infinite works on the mind out of all proportion. Give us genuinely unlimited data and we keep the phone running and do anything at all with it: quite possibly walking the sights from morning to night with a voice agent open the entire time. What matters here is that our judgments about which business models are worth opening out are made from inside a constraint. Latent business models branch like a tree, and to stop short because a constraint is in the way may be to cut away, wholesale, every branch that would have flowered further on. And from inside the constraint we cannot, in principle, imagine what we would do in a world without it.

On energy, our civilization is that traveller with 2GB.

So what happens when the cost of energy structurally collapses is not a discount on existing demand. It is an explosion of demand. This is not a hopeful reading. Three centuries of evidence stand behind it.

## 5.2 Three centuries of light: the cascade demonstrated

One of the goods whose cost humanity has driven down for longest is artificial light. William Nordhaus, in a classic study, measured the true price of light from open fire through tallow candles, whale oil, gas lamps, incandescent bulbs and fluorescent tubes, and showed that the official price indices had overstated the rise in the price of light by between 900 and 1,600 times. In real terms, the price of a thousand lumen-hours fell by 99.97 percent between 1800 and 1992.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Setting computation aside, no good in human history has become as cheap as light.

So when light became ten thousand times cheaper, did humanity spend less on light? Here is the decisive finding. On the work of Tsao and colleagues, humanity has for three centuries, across six continents and five distinct lighting technologies, gone on spending consistently about 0.72 percent of GDP on light.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> Exactly to the extent that efficiency rose, and by exactly that much, humanity consumed more light. It invented the city at night, the twenty-four-hour factory, the screen that never sleeps. In the language of economics, the rebound effect for light is close to 100 percent.

This is the purest modern version of the paradox Jevons saw in coal (Chapter 2). It is also the demonstration of this chapter's central proposition. A collapse in cost is absorbed not as a contraction of spending but as an explosion of consumption. There is no reason why what happened to light should not happen to energy as a whole. Light, after all, is only one form of energy.

## 5.3 Anatomy of the demand explosion: what gets unlocked

In the end, the more the efficiency of energy conversion in the physical world improves and the further cost falls, the more our demand explodes and the more the business models that have been held down come into flower. The further the constraint is stripped away, the more abundant energy becomes, the more our energy demand expands without limit, applications expand, and energy production rises again in a virtuous circle. Space holds resources enough to go on increasing energy production forever.

<p class="equation">E↑ → P_E↓ → application demand D↑ → investment↑ → E↑</p>
Let me set out the inventory of what gets unlocked, concretely, from the cost structure.

**Water.** Reverse osmosis desalination has come down to roughly 0.5 dollars per cubic metre, and between 30 and 50 percent of that cost is energy.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Cut the price of energy to a fraction of itself and fresh water stops being the binding variable of arid regions. Food. Energy accounts for 40 to 70 percent of the operating cost of vertical farming, and within that energy, in a dedicated growing room, lighting takes 60 to a little over 80 percent and climate control 20 to a little over 30 percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> In a world of cheap electricity, agriculture is cut loose from land and climate and moves into the city. Cities. As Chapter 1 showed, whether a city can be built from nothing is the product of institutions, energy and labour input, and fusion and automation set that product high in any location at all. Space. Launch cost has fallen twentyfold, from roughly 54,500 dollars per kilogram on the Space Shuttle to roughly 2,720 dollars per kilogram on Falcon 9. Starship is spoken of as aiming two orders of magnitude below that.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> Complete the third order of magnitude and orbit becomes an industrial location. And computation. That the leading edge of the demand explosion is computation is no longer a forecast but an observation (Chapter 2).

What matters is that the constraint on a civilization is industrial energy demand, not household demand. Industrial demand runs overwhelmingly ahead of household demand. The macro sketch is drawn by the IEA itself. Electricity as a share of final energy consumption rises from about 20 percent today to more than 50 percent by 2050 in the net-zero scenario, and world electricity demand rises by between 75 and 150 percent by 2050 depending on the scenario.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> And that estimate has not yet priced in a world in which the global south converges on OECD levels of consumption per head. Recall the arithmetic of eight billion robots at the end of Chapter 3. There is no upper bound on demand.

## 5.4 The order of supply, and Wright's law

If demand grows without limit, the question moves to supply. What supplies it, in what order, on what cost curve?

The order follows from the arguments of Chapters 3 and 4. At the top, fusion: solar energy captured at industrial scale, and the likely core answer to surging industrial demand. But there is a transitional period before fusion is everywhere, and that period is dominated by fission, both conventional reactors and SMRs as a more flexible, modularised baseload. Fossil fuels as a bridge. And renewables riding on top of firm power as a supplement.

Here I introduce the most important law on the supply side. Wright's law: the learning curve on which unit cost falls by a constant rate for every doubling of cumulative production. The learning rate for solar panels is about 20 percent, and as prices fell 20 to 25 percent with every doubling of cumulative capacity, module prices dropped roughly 90 percent in the 2010s alone. Way and colleagues at Oxford, having tested learning-curve forecasts against more than fifty technologies, concluded that a rapid energy transition, far from being expensive, saves roughly 12 trillion dollars net against a slow one.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> Cost reduction is not a function of will. It is a function of volume.

That law fixes the strategic meaning of nuclear and of SMRs. As Chapter 3 showed, the cost escalation of reactors is a product of institutions rather than of physical law, and France and Korea, repeating a standard design, held their costs stable (Chapter 3). The SMR bet is the bet that a reactor can be converted from a civil engineering project built on site into a product manufactured in series in a factory, putting nuclear on Wright's law for the first time in its history. And the giants on the demand side have begun to guarantee the early demand for that bet. Google contracted with Kairos Power for up to 500 megawatts, Amazon with X-energy for more than 5 gigawatts by 2039, Meta ran a tender for up to 4 gigawatts of nuclear procurement, and Microsoft locked in 835 megawatts on a twenty-year contract through the restart of Three Mile Island unit 1. The capacity the four announced or tendered across four months in the autumn of 2024 comes to something on the order of 10 gigawatts.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> The order book that mass production presupposes is being built now.

The orders from the demand side are not confined to nuclear. OpenAI's Stargate reached roughly 7 gigawatts of planned capacity and more than 400 billion dollars of investment as of September 2025, with a publicly stated path to 10 gigawatts and 500 billion dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> In fusion, Microsoft signed the world's first fusion power purchase agreement with Helion, and Google placed a 200-megawatt purchase agreement on CFS's ARC reactor.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> Here is where 2026 stands. Helion's Polaris became the first privately funded device to measure fusion on deuterium-tritium fuel, reaching 150 million degrees. CFS's SPARC is targeting first plasma in 2027.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> Those dates should be read as targets, not as achievements.

On the fusion timeline I want to be careful and exact. Fusion demands enormous front-loaded investment. SMRs and fusion alike carry extremely large construction CAPEX, but their fuel costs once running are low, which makes them low-OPEX sources across a long operating life. Like data centres and semiconductor fabs, they have the scaling property that once the initial investment is cleared, the marginal cost of expanding use is low. So the cost of capital does not fall merely because fusion has become technically possible. It falls only after commercial operation, after decades of large-scale deployment, learning-by-doing and supply chain maturity have pushed unit cost down: only, that is, once Wright's law is running. Energy at near-zero marginal cost is not the immediate consequence of the first commercial reactor. It is a consequence of the late phase of diffusion. Given where 2026 stands (Chapter 2), the honest reading is that the main battleground for first power to the grid is the end of the 2020s and the 2030s, and the main battleground for price impact is the 2040s. Diffusion of fusion itself, on the other hand, is not the hard part, because it is compatible with the existing grid.

One more thing. If energy supply can be the constraint on the non-linear development of AI, then turned around it is also the largest weapon the human side holds over AI. Large-scale AI takes enormous electricity for training and for inference alike, and that electricity cannot be hidden, cannot be smuggled, and can be counted. If computing resources are the effective chokepoint of AI governance,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> energy is the more physical chokepoint sitting further upstream still. Energy policy becomes AI governance by other means.

## 5.5 The consequence for population

And then physical AI. If the mass production of humanoids and automated plant begins at the end of the 2020s, the marginal cost of labour begins to collapse in the wake of energy. Returned to the framework of Chapter 4, this means that the price of energy P_E and the degree of automation A move at the same time, and that the effective prices of the components of the expected cost per child C_child, meaning housing, education, childcare and healthcare, meaning the Baumol sectors, can fall structurally for the first time. There has only ever been one known cure for Baumol's cost disease. Raise productivity in the sick sector directly, with technology. Until the productivity revolution reaches childcare, education and medicine, the price of raising a child does not fall. Physical AI is another name for the productivity revolution in the Baumol sectors.

If the fall in fertility is a price phenomenon, its reversal can only occur as a price phenomenon too. It does not occur through family-policy subsidies (Korea's roughly 380 trillion won, seen in Chapter 4, is the largest instance of the attempt on record). So I think there is little point in committing the bulk of scarce investment resources to pro-natal spending. Stagnation in growth and in population is, in the first instance, an energy problem. Let the price of energy fall dramatically and available energy per head expand, and the discovery of new business models and the forward motion of civilization accelerate deeply, and the population problem moves toward resolution of its own accord. The structure is the mirror image of the classical Malthusian trap. In Malthus's world, population was bound by the food constraint. When technology loosened the food constraint, population could grow again. Today's situation is its analogue, with energy now playing the part food once played.

As with ESG investing when it was first proposed, my hypothesis is that this megatrend persists. Action is needed on both the short and the long horizon, but the consistently decisive priority, ahead of everything else, is energy R&D.

## 5.6 Honesty about the timeline

If the chapter to this point has read as a claim that everything is solved in the 2030s, let me correct that here. I am confident about the direction. On the speed I am wary of the bias produced by the environment I happen to sit in.

The physical is not the digital. Delivering physical AI and energy abundance in full within the 2030s is probably not achievable. It is a different story from AI in digital space. Physical AI in the real world, self-driving or the autonomous development of space by robots, takes an extraordinary amount of time. Software has a zero marginal cost of copying; robots have to be built one at a time, and factories, parts, regulation, accidents and repairs all proceed on physical-world time. In the terms of Chapter 2, C_pred falls exponentially while C_config is bound by physics. Confusing the speeds of the two is the single most common error in this field.

It is quicker to look at the numbers. The unit cost of humanoids is indeed beginning to ride Wright's law. On Morgan Stanley's estimate, the bill of materials for Tesla's Optimus Gen2 currently runs at 50,000 to 60,000 dollars, and Musk talks about a sale price at volume of 20,000 to 30,000 dollars. Industry estimates as of 2026 put unit costs for the main platforms (Optimus, Figure, 1X, Apptronik) in a range of 50,000 to 200,000 dollars. But on the January 2026 earnings call Musk himself said the machine is "not being used in any meaningful way in the factory". Mass production is said to start in the second half of 2026, on a repurposed Fremont line.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> Unit cost is falling. Quality is still in the middle of being demonstrated. The two curves move separately.

**The wall of fusion CAPEX.** Reaching final energy abundance will require fusion for that reason alone. But to put it precisely: fusion may have low operating costs, yet the CAPEX that delivers them is of an entirely different order. For Wright's law, as set out in this chapter, to begin to bite, fusion reactors and their components have to be mass produced cheaply, so that the reactor itself can be supplied cheaply. That may be out of reach even in the 2040s. Between the ignition of the first machine and a tenfold reduction in the cost per unit lie several decades and several hundred reactors. When this thesis writes that the main battleground for price impact is the 2040s, that is not optimism. It is the result of counting that distance.

And this is a story about the advanced economies alone. That is the most important reservation of all. We also have to build lavatories in India. We must not neglect building basic schools in Africa and then get the timeline wrong. A substantial share of the world's population has still not reached stable electricity, water and sanitation, or basic education. On the same planet on which we are discussing fusion and humanoids.

We take our bias from the bubble around us and mistake the timeline, believing that humanity as a whole can move to the next phase. But we have to recognise that we simply happen to sit in the fortunate environment of Silicon Valley or the UAE. The claim of this thesis is not about when it arrives but about what the constraint is. The identification of the constraint can be correct while the speed of its removal differs completely from place to place according to capital, institutions and geography. If anything, if the cost of energy and automation really does collapse, the first and largest beneficiary ought to be not the frontier but the societies now made to pay the highest effective prices. Fresh water, electricity and education all arriving as goods whose marginal cost approaches zero. That is where the ethical meaning of the cascade lies.

**The possibility that capital markets break first.** Chapter 1 said that the capacity to build is the product of institutions, energy and labour input. There is a variable there that has not yet been made explicit: the appetite of capital markets to invest. In Bank of America's fund manager survey of February 2026, the share of respondents saying that companies are overinvesting in capex reached the highest level in the history of the survey, 25 percent named an AI bubble as the largest tail risk, and 30 percent named hyperscaler capex as the epicentre of a systemic credit event.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> This is the most direct scepticism the financial markets offer about this thesis's supply-side scenario. I do not dismiss it. Let AI investment break badly once and the expansion curves of energy and computing power themselves bend. At that point the identification of the constraint stays correct and only its removal slips back by a decade or more.

And the growth itself is not exponential. The market of 2026 prices in AI growing exponentially. In my reading it will not. But the reason has to be stated precisely.

Generation technology itself is growing exponentially. As Figure 5-3 shows, cumulative installed solar capacity climbed six orders of magnitude, from 0.54 megawatts in 1975 to 1.85 million megawatts in 2024. China accumulated 1,840 gigawatts of wind and solar alone by the end of 2025, reaching 47 percent of total installed capacity.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup> The claim that energy supply is linear is contrary to fact.

The rate limiter is not on the generation side. It is the set of steps between the generator and the load. The queue for new grid connections, manufacturing capacity for transformers and switchgear, land acquisition and permitting for transmission lines, and substation construction. Every one of these is a process that shortens only by years even if you add factories, and every one of them tightened worldwide at the same time from the early 2020s. The electricity that can be generated and the electricity a new load can actually connect to are two different quantities.

So my reading is this. Generating capacity grows exponentially, connectable capacity grows for the time being at a slower rate than that, and the effective supply capacity of AI is rate limited by the latter. The point where prices that have discounted an exponential meet a reality rate limited by connection is where the adjustment begins. And in this thesis's framework, what is most valuable in that phase is not generating assets but sites that are already connected, and the order of connection itself.

Beyond that lies a second stage. As section 8.6 of Chapter 8 sets out, once orbital computation becomes practical the energy constraint is cut loose from the terrestrial grid. But as that section also shows, the constraint on the orbital side is not energy but heat rejection, and if it opens it will open from the side of temperature rather than the side of area. The first demonstrations will probably appear within five to ten years. The era of commercial AI in earnest begins on whichever side the constraint comes off first. And economics predicts clearly what happens in this transition. When demand explodes and supply is constrained, prices rise. The intuition that things keep getting cheaper does not hold over this period.

So a pattern of use emerges for this period. People will move back and forth between commercial AI and the AI in their own hands, and they will economise. The AI in your hand runs on memory. Demand for memory therefore rises. And in the phase where the unit cost of computation really begins to bite, quantum computation becomes the subject. In lower-income regions this pattern will be more extreme still, and many people will use only the AI in their hands. The decade before abundance wears the opposite face to abundance. Nothing this thesis claims denies that decade.

The reservations about speed do not end here. The last of them concerns not when the constraint comes off but what happens after it does: the fact that population, as a variable, moves later than everything else. It gets a section of its own.

## 5.7 The valley of demand

One more reservation about time has to be added to the argument so far. Between the moment supply increases and the moment demand increases, there is a gap of several decades.

First, confirm that the exponential is conditional. Establish energy abundance and the deployment of physical AI and computing power grows exponentially, and output grows exponentially with it. Fail to establish it and the growth of both is held down by the speed of connection seen in the previous section. The arithmetic of the eight billion robots in Chapter 3 is the reason. Energy does not set the level of the growth rate. It sets the functional form of growth.

The problem is that the things that follow do not happen at matching speeds. As Chapter 6 argues, in a world where the constraint has been removed, wealth can be distributed broadly through stakes in automation capital. But it takes time for fertility to move after household disposable income recovers, and more time still for total population to move after fertility moves. Suppose a baby boom began somewhere in the 2030s: that generation enters the labour market in the 2050s, and twenty years on the change showing up in total population is slight. Population is the slowest-moving variable in an economy.

In the first and second industrial revolutions, population grew exponentially after the constraint came off. As the reconstruction by Wrigley and Schofield seen in Chapter 1 shows, what drove the surge in English population after 1750 was not falling mortality but rising fertility, whose contribution was roughly twice that of mortality. What pushed it up was rising real income. What this thesis predicts is a repeat performance of the same phenomenon. The decisive difference is that this time a gap of several decades is inserted in front of it.

What happens inside that gap? Supply capacity grows exponentially and demand grows only at the speed of population. As section 2.7 of Chapter 2 derived, in a world where supply is automated, the scarce thing is not supply capacity but demand. That proposition appears in its sharpest form precisely in this period.

The world is likely to suffer a shortage of demand throughout it. Here a proposition I set out in Chapter 4 comes back. On China I wrote that a bias toward supply becomes a self-reinforcing trap. Excess supply produces unemployment, unemployment kills births, falling births kill future demand, and the disappearance of demand produces further excess supply. The world immediately after energy becomes abundant is a phase in which the largest supply capacity in history sits alongside a population that has not yet recovered. The trap China faces now is one the whole world will pass through once. The difference is that China experiences it under the constraint while the world experiences it with the constraint removed. So there is an exit. But the time to that exit does not disappear.

### What automation can bypass and what it cannot

Let me make the structure of this gap one degree clearer. For an economy to work, it needs at least four head-counts. Labour supply, demand, risk-takers and taxpayers.

Of these, the only one automation can bypass is labour supply. Hands in the factory, processing in the back office, and eventually delivery and care work too, can be replaced by machines. The other three cannot.

Demand is a head-count. If nobody wants the thing, however much you make it is only inventory. Machines cannot supply demand.

Risk-takers are a head-count too. The number of people who will stake their own lives on a business nobody has tried is not a function of productivity but a function of the size of the population and of the institutions that let that population fail. This is the sense in which section 2.7 of Chapter 2 called talent a directing input.

Taxpayers are a head-count as well. You can tax the wealth machines generate, but what carries that taxation, decides its distribution and maintains its institutions is a human community, and the scale of a community is its population.

So the meaning of a falling population in a heavily automated world is not a shortage of labour. It is the simultaneous contraction of demand, of challengers and of the tax base. This is why, in a phase where supply alone grows exponentially, the valley goes on being a valley.

### The four head-counts do not come back at the same speed

The argument so far has been qualitative. Put numbers in it.

The time it takes one human being to join each of the head-counts can be read off the National Transfer Accounts, a framework that estimates consumption, labour income and public transfers by age, country by country. What comes out of it is the fact that the four head-counts do not come back together.

Demand is same-day. From the moment of birth a person is a consumer. But the size is small. In the US age profile for 2011,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> consumption per head at ages 0 to 4 is 44 percent of the average for ages 20 to 64, and by 15 to 19 it is 95 percent, close to adult. Demand is not delayed, but at first it is less than half.

Labour supply is twenty-six years. Labour income per head at 20 to 24 is only 27 percent of peak. It reaches half of peak at 26 and 80 percent at 34. On a synthetic cohort basis, what a person earns by 29 is only a tenth of lifetime labour income. Entry to the labour market comes twenty years out, but becoming a real contributor comes five to ten years after that. Where this thesis has written twenty years up to now, it was, conservatively, five years short.

Taxpayers are twenty-six years as well. Until then, though, the sign is reversed. The age at which net payments into the public sector turn positive is 26 in the United States, 22 in Japan, and 23 in Germany and Sweden. Before that a person is a net recipient, and the peak of receipt is not infancy but school age. In the United States it is minus 0.14 of adult labour income at 0 to 4, minus 0.33 at 5 to 9, and minus 0.29 at 15 to 19. The peak of net payment comes at 45 to 59. A reversal in fertility worsens the public finances for a quarter of a century before it starts to improve them. This is not an argument against it. It is the time structure of the cost. When a debate about solving pension sustainability through fertility says nothing about the first twenty-five years, it is because it has not looked at this structure.

Risk-takers are forty-five years. A study tracking 2.7 million founders of employer firms in the United States through administrative data puts the mean age of all founders at 41.9, the mean for founders of firms in the top 0.1 percent by growth at 45.0, and the mean for founders who exited successfully at 46.7.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> And the probability of success rises with age. A founder of 50 is 1.8 times as likely as a founder of 30 to reach the top 0.1 percent, and the rise continues to nearly 60. The story of the young entrepreneur appears only when the population is drawn from award lists and press coverage. The same study shows the mean age of TechCrunch award winners at 31.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> It is a question of how the sample is taken.

The structure of the delays therefore comes out like this. Demand same-day, labour twenty-six years, the public finances twenty-six years and with a reversed sign along the way, and challengers forty-five years. The valley is a valley because the four head-counts do not come back together.

Let me place one reservation on this calculation myself. The NTA age profiles are a cross-section of a single year, not a cohort followed through time. And the cross-section itself keeps sliding back. In the United States the age at which a person turns net contributor moved from 24 in 1981 to 29 in 2011, and the peak age of labour income moved from 39 to 49. So for a child born today, every one of the delays above is likely to be an underestimate.

### The depth and length of the valley

How long, then, does the valley last? Calculate it in the form least favourable to this thesis: that is, on the most optimistic assumptions available.

Suppose the constraint comes off completely in 2035, and that at that instant the total fertility rate of high-income countries jumps in a single step from 1.42 to 2.1 and stays there. This cannot happen in reality. But since no reversal faster than this is physically possible, the valley this calculation produces is a lower bound. Run the cohort component method from the World Bank's 2025 age structure and it comes out as follows.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup>

| High-income countries (population 1.40bn) | 2035 | Valley floor | Year of floor | vs 2035 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20–64 (labour supply) | 796m | 579m | 2085 | −27.3% |
| 26–64 (net taxpayers) | 702m | 499m | 2090 | −28.8% |
| 38–52 (core founding cohort) | 284m | 175m | 2073 | −38.4% |
| Total population | 1.401bn | 1.127bn | 2115 or later | −19.5% |

Fifty years. Even if fertility returned to replacement tomorrow, the working-age population of the high-income countries goes on shrinking until 2085, and the floor is more than a quarter lower. The head-count of net taxpayers bottoms out five years later still. And the core founding cohort bottoms out earliest but sinks deepest, by close to 40 percent.

For China the same calculation is harsher. The working-age population falls 43 percent by 2085 and the core founding cohort 60 percent by 2073. For Japan the working-age population falls 40 percent by 2084.

Even so, it is worth doing. The difference between reversal and no reversal is that in 2085 the high-income working-age population is 31 percent larger, China's 69 percent larger and Japan's 52 percent larger. By 2100 the difference is 67 percent for the high-income countries and 161 percent for China. That the valley is deep and that reversal is pointless are entirely different statements. Reversal does not remove the valley. It determines whether there is a far side to it.

And the price of delay can be calculated. If the reversal begins in 2050 rather than 2035, the floor only deepens from 27 percent down to 31 percent down, but the year of the floor slides fourteen years, from 2085 to 2099. Fifteen years of delay add four points of depth and push recovery back fourteen years. Time costs more than depth.

The investment implication comes in two stages. The forty years from 2035 to roughly 2075 are a period in which supply capacity grows exponentially while demand, the tax base and the challengers contract at the same time. What can be relied on in this phase is returns that do not depend on head-count: stakes in automation capital itself, and the infrastructure of energy and computation. Businesses whose returns are proportional to the volume of demand sit in a structural headwind for those forty years. And from 2075 the four head-counts return in sequence. When Chapter 6 writes that distribution is a question of demand before it is a question of fairness, it is in the sense of how these forty years are to be crossed.

But this structure also shows the exit. If energy and labour genuinely become abundant and the wealth created is properly distributed, the household constraint loosens, fertility reverses and population returns. When population returns, demand, risk-takers and taxpayers all return with it. When the equation of Chapter 4 runs in reverse, what recovers is not population alone. The three head-counts an economy needs in order to work recover together.

Distribution is a question of demand, of risk-taking and of the fiscal base before it is a question of fairness. When you read Chapter 6, do not forget that order.

From an investment point of view, this valley is the longest phase of all. Describing the world after the removal of the constraint is complete, and designing a way to survive the several decades that lead there, are two different jobs.

Designing distribution is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one, because distribution solves the problem of income and does not solve the problem of time. Even so, without designing distribution the valley cannot be crossed. Where does the wealth the cascade releases fall, if it is left to itself? That is the subject of Chapter 6.

## Objections and Limits
### The rebound effect is not a general law (5.2)

Whether the size of the rebound effect across an entire economy, meaning a backfire above 100 percent, is a general law is still disputed in the literature. **This thesis does not settle that dispute.** What it rests on is not a general law but a single point: that in domains where suppressed demand is large, there is no end to the places a cost collapse can be absorbed into. In a world that contains the cities that could not be built, the children that could not be had and the science that was never implemented, all seen in Part II, a collapse in cost does not show up as a contraction in spending. **Read with that limitation removed, as "efficiency always increases total consumption," it is a stronger claim than this thesis makes.**

### The assumptions behind the valley calculation (5.7)

**The assumptions of the calculation.** The World Bank's 2025 population structure by five-year band and by sex was interpolated to single years of age, smoothed, and run through the cohort component method. Age-specific fertility was set as a skew-normal distribution with a mean of 29.0, a standard deviation of 5.2 and a skewness of 1.2, scaled by the total fertility rate. Mortality was fixed using single-year survival rates of the Gompertz-Makeham form, calibrated to match life expectancy in the region concerned. **Migration was set to zero.** Because migration substantially governs the actual path of the working-age population in high-income countries, that assumption works in the direction of making the valley look deeper. But the claim of this section concerns **timing** rather than level, and the year of the floor does not move outside the range 2085 to 2090 even when the life expectancy assumption is shifted four years either way. And migration sums to zero for the world as a whole: to the extent it fills the high-income valley, it deepens the valley somewhere else. **This calculation is a model, not a forecast.** Its purpose is to show that even under the most optimistic assumptions the valley lasts half a century.

**The strongest objection to this model is migration.** Because migration substantially governs the actual path of the working-age population in high-income countries, the assumption of zero migration works in the direction of making the valley look deeper. What lets the claim of this section survive nonetheless is that the claim concerns **timing** rather than level. The year of the floor does not move outside the range 2085 to 2090 even when the life expectancy assumption is shifted four years either way. And migration sums to zero for the world as a whole: to the extent it fills the high-income valley, it deepens the valley somewhere else.

### What this thesis is not confident about on the timeline (5.6)

The reservations set out in 5.6 are every one of them about the **speed** of removal, not about the **identification** of the constraint. **There is exactly one case in which that distinction breaks down: the case in which capital markets break first and AI investment bends badly.** Removal then slips back by a decade or more, and the investment implications of this thesis need more than an extension of the time axis. Of the priorities in section 8.8 of Chapter 8, the judgment about returns that do not depend on head-count is the one most directly affected.
