## 7.1 Smith's fork

There was never a line between economic activity and everything else.

To create value is to apply one's own competence for the benefit of another.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Under that definition, serving coffee in a cafe and a mother loving her child are the same act. The only difference is that one of them carried a price. The side that carried one was called the economy, sorted into industries, counted in statistics and added up into GDP. The side that did not was called nothing at all.

So treat the coffee as a production unit and pay seven dollars for it: that reading is the one to drop. What is happening is that I receive a benefit from what the person behind the counter does, and that person, with the wage they are paid, receives a benefit in the same way from someone else's work in their own hours. Each is putting their competence at the other's disposal, and money is only the vehicle. The story in which a production unit gets replaced by AI comes from mistaking the vehicle for the substance.

What AI accelerates is the part of value creation a machine can take over: making the coffee, moving the freight, drawing the plans, writing the code. The range of human action, that part included, is far wider. So what happens is that the counted part shrinks against the whole and the weight of the part that was never counted rises. Human beings are not switching to something else. What changes is what gets counted.

Adam Smith drew the line. The distinction itself, though, was not his invention. Aristotle separated use-value from exchange-value in the fourth century BCE, and the medieval Schoolmen explicitly held that use-value came first, on the grounds that exchange is motivated by human need.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> What Smith did was not to distinguish but to choose.

In Book I, Chapter IV of *The Wealth of Nations*, Smith notes the two senses of the word value.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Use-value, which expresses the utility of some object, and exchange-value, which expresses the power of purchasing other goods that possession of it conveys. Then he wrote the famous paradox. Nothing is more useful than water, and almost nothing can be bought with it. A diamond has scarcely any use-value, and it will exchange for a vast quantity of other goods. Immediately after setting the paradox down, Smith makes a quiet and decisive choice. He confines everything that follows to exchange-value.

The reason was practical, not metaphysical. His concern was not economics in general but how England, at the start of the industrial revolution, could grow wealthy through international trade. For that purpose exchange-value was the easier of the two to understand and it gave a standardized measure. And it was use-value that he called real value. The real value of a thing is the toil and trouble of acquiring it, because the necessities, conveniences and amusements of life can only be drawn from the labor of others, and the only thing anyone has to offer in return is their own labor. Having defined real value that way, Smith moved to the measurable one, nominal value, the price paid in the market. He did so knowing that value is in fact settled inside the experience of the person who receives it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

His distinction between productive and unproductive is misquoted almost as often. Smith did not say that services have no value. The criterion was whether the result could be carried forward and exchanged again. A barber shaves and cuts a person's hair. However valuable that act, the person who received it cannot put it into another exchange. Receive a tangible good and it can be sent on into the next exchange. So the view grew up that a good comes with transportable, transferable value embedded in it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> Smith called productive those activities that contribute to producing and distributing exportable tangible goods, and for the purpose of explaining how to increase national wealth it is the right line. The line was not wrong. Later generations took a distinction drawn for that purpose and began using it as the measure of what people are getting. The paradox of water and diamonds was not solved but shelved, and then the shelving was forgotten.
