## 7.1 Smith's fork

There was never a boundary between economic activity and everything else.

To create value is to apply one's own competence for the benefit of another.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Under that definition, serving coffee in a cafe and a mother loving her child are the same act. The only difference is that one of them carried a price. The side that carried one was called the economy, sorted into industries, counted in statistics and added up into GDP. The side that did not was called nothing at all.

So treat the coffee as a production unit and pay seven dollars for it: that reading is the one to drop. What is happening is that I receive a benefit from an experience I enjoyed, and the person who gave it to me is put in a position, through the vehicle of a wage, to receive benefits of the same kind in their own hours. Each is putting their competence at the other's disposal, and money is only the vehicle. The story in which a production unit gets replaced comes from mistaking the vehicle for the substance.

What AI accelerates is the part of value creation that mechanization reaches. The range of human action, that part included, is far wider. So what happens is that the counted part shrinks against the whole and the weight of the part that was never counted rises as a result. Human beings are not switching to something else; only the way the line is drawn changes.

Adam Smith drew the line. The distinction itself, though, was not his invention. Aristotle separated use value from exchange value in the fourth century BCE, and the medieval Schoolmen explicitly held that use value came first, on the grounds that exchange is motivated by human need.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> What Smith did was not to distinguish but to choose.

In Book I, Chapter IV of *The Wealth of Nations*, Smith notes the two senses of the word value.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Use value, which expresses the utility of some object, and exchange value, which expresses the power of purchasing other goods that possession of it conveys. Then he wrote the famous paradox. Nothing is more useful than water, and almost nothing can be bought with it. A diamond has scarcely any use value, and it will exchange for a vast quantity of other goods. Immediately after setting the paradox down, Smith makes a quiet and decisive choice. He confines everything that follows to exchange value.

The reason was practical, not metaphysical. His concern was not economics in general but how England, at the start of the industrial revolution, could grow wealthy through international trade. For that purpose exchange value was the easier of the two to understand and it gave a standardized measure. And it was use value that he called real value: the labor required to afford the necessities, conveniences and amusements of human life through the labor of others. Having defined real value that way, he moved to the measurable one, nominal value, the price paid in the market, with the caveat that he recognized value to be determined phenomenologically by the beneficiary.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

His distinction between productive and unproductive is misquoted almost as often. Smith did not say that services have no value. The criterion was whether the result could be carried forward and exchanged again. A barber shaves and cuts a person's hair. However valuable that act, the person who received it cannot put it into another exchange. Receive a tangible good and it can be sent on into the next exchange. So the view grew up that a good comes with transportable, transferable value embedded in it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> Call productive those activities that contribute to producing and distributing exportable tangible goods, and for the purpose of explaining how to increase national wealth it is the right line. The line was not wrong; the purpose was mistaken afterwards. The paradox of water and diamonds was not solved but shelved, and then the shelving was forgotten.

## 7.2 Undoing a 250-year detour

There is a body of theory that undoes that detour of two hundred and fifty years: Vargo and Lusch's service-dominant logic (SDL), whose axiomatization comes down to five statements.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

Axiom 1: applying competence for the benefit of another is the fundamental basis of exchange. Axiom 2: value is always co-created by multiple actors, including the beneficiary. Axiom 3: all social and economic actors are resource integrators. Axiom 4: value is always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the beneficiary. Axiom 5: value co-creation is coordinated through actor-generated institutions and institutional arrangements.

Goods are a mechanism for delivering that competence, a vehicle for value, and nothing more; indirect exchange masks that fundamental basis. What a human being needs is not a good but value. We have never, from Day 1, been buying coffee. We feel, and pay for, the value that comes through the coffee, which is to say the use value that arises in context.

For the same reason, SDL has no producers and consumers. The picture in which one party creates value and the other uses it up renders the economy as a cycle of making stuff, selling stuff and destroying stuff. But is education consumed? Are books? Is entertainment? An entrepreneur, a salesperson, a customer, the head of a household, a parent driving the carpool, a grocery shopper and a restaurant patron are not different kinds of actor. Quite often they are, literally, the same person.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup>

And the assessment of value is heterogeneous all the way down. One person finds value in flying from Dubai to New Haven for a dense intellectual conversation. Another finds it in going to a music festival. The moment value is defined phenomenologically (Axiom 4), both are equally rational. To take that heterogeneous assessment as input and translate it into the specific experiences and arrangements that maximize perceived value: that is the role of AI in the world to come. AI becomes a delivery system for value. And then Axiom 5. What coordinates the co-creation of value is not the market price alone but the institutions actors generate.

## 7.3 The measurable side won

The line was disputed from the moment it was drawn.

Jean-Baptiste Say held that production is the creation of utility rather than a change in the form of matter, defined services as what is consumed at the time of production itself, and called them immaterial products.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> John Stuart Mill went further. Making a thing is a rearrangement of matter; no human being can produce one particle of matter. What labor produces, therefore, is not objects but utilities. And Mill asked why all labor that produces utility should not be accounted productive, including labor consisting of a mere service rendered.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>

The one who went furthest was Frédéric Bastiat.

> The great economic law is this: services are exchanged for services. It is trivial, very commonplace; it is nonetheless the beginning, the middle, and the end of economic science. Once this axiom is clearly understood, what becomes of such subtle distinctions as use-value and exchange-value, material products and immaterial products, productive classes and unproductive classes?<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>

That was written in 1848. Everything this chapter has said so far was said a hundred and seventy-five years ago. What is new is not the claim but the means of erasing the line.

The line survived all the same. Not because the objections were wrong. Say and Mill both took issue with Smith's classification and then built their own arguments on top of it anyway. Only the inside of the line could be measured, and what could be measured took possession of the word economy.

And the line still has the same character. What decides the classification is not the act but who performs it. That the line is not a natural object is conceded by the authority that draws it. The Economic Classification Policy Committee of the US Bureau of Economic Analysis writes:

> One and the same activity, such as painting, may be classified as goods or service production depending purely on the organization of the overall process of production. If the painting is done by employees within the producer unit that makes the good, it will be treated as part of the goods production, whereas if it is done by an outside painting company, it will be classified as an intermediate input of services. Thus, when a service previously performed in a manufacturing establishment is contracted out to a specialized services firm, data will show an increase in services production in the economy even though the total activity of painting may be unchanged.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup>

If an automaker runs its own staff cafeteria the people working there are counted in manufacturing employment, and contract it to an outside catering firm and the same people doing the same cooking are counted in services employment.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup> This is how the statement that seventy to eighty percent of a developed economy is services gets manufactured. And once the statement is believed, government and industry leaders act accordingly and adopt the measures held to suit a service economy. The line produces, by measuring it, the reality it is supposed to be measuring.

## 7.4 The blind spot in GDP can be measured

What GDP measures is not value but the monetized part of value. Looking at myself, the monetized share of my value creation may be something like twenty percent of what I create in a day. Full-time work is booked as economic activity in GDP. An interesting conversation with a friend is worth fifty dollars to me, and to my friend as well. Incorporate, though, and stage exactly the same act as consulting, and it enters GDP.

That intuition about twenty percent is consistent with what measurement economics finds. Three of them, in order. First, household production. In the satellite accounts of the US Bureau of Economic Analysis, unpaid household production such as cooking, cleaning, childcare and elder care accounts for twenty to twenty-five percent of extended output. Restated as an addition to published GDP, around thirty percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> Value placed outside the national accounts for no reason other than that it does not pass through a market. Second, time. Averaging the OECD time use surveys over ages 25 to 64, paid work is only four and a half hours a day, against four hours of unpaid work and just under five hours of leisure.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> More than half of human waking time is outside price from the start. Third, free digital goods. In the large-scale choice experiments of Brynjolfsson and colleagues, the median user's compensation for giving up Facebook for a month was about forty-eight dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> For a good with a price of zero and near-zero contribution to GDP. Their proposed GDP-B is an attempt to measure this uncounted consumer surplus. All three ways of measuring point the same direction. The value we create through employed tasks is the minority of the value we create in a day.

This is not a bug in GDP. It is the specification, Smith's proxy institutionalized over two hundred and fifty years. As the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission formally acknowledged,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> we have measured what was easy to measure and called what we measured value.

## 7.5 Price does two jobs

Price does two jobs: it rations scarcity and it measures value. If the monetary institution is to retire, both have to go.

Money is not a natural object but an institution. Simmel drew it as a pure form of social relation, Graeber as a technology of credit and accounting that precedes coinage, Harari as a shared intersubjective fiction.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> SDL's vocabulary is blunter: money is a claim on the future, a right to have some other actor apply their competence on your behalf one day.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> A balance is not value itself but an inventory of those rights.

An institution that loses its function is retired. With two jobs, retirement takes two conditions.

The first condition has already been observed in information goods. Where marginal cost is close to zero, competition pushes price down to marginal cost, which is to say to zero, and price stops working as a signal for allocating resources.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> The collapse in the cost of energy and automation extends the same mechanics into the material world (Chapter 5). When the marginal cost of goods and services goes asymptotically to zero, price as a rationing device loses the scarcity it was rationing (Chapter 6).

That is only half of it. The second job is not settled by energy.

## 7.6 Rising resolution ends the measuring job

As section 7.1 showed, Smith granted that use value is the substance of value and then confined his inquiry to exchange value. The price institution, in other words, stands on a single engineering fact. Use value could not be observed. So something observable was put in its place.

SDL's fourth axiom states the same fact from the other side. Value is always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the beneficiary. The original goes on: firms and government agencies may attempt to assess value, but they assess it from their own goals and their own context, and their assessment always diverges from the beneficiary's. Proxies such as economic efficiency and productivity can be measured, but actual value cannot be assessed.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup>

Chapter 2 drew the history of direction 2 as fidelity in the reproduction of symbols. Language, writing, printing, the telegraph, the telephone, the internet. That sequence has a second effect that has never been counted. Each time fidelity rose, the range of economic activity that could be observed widened. The ledger made transactions observable, the telegraph made distant prices observable, and the smartphone made countless acts that had never carried a price into objects of measurement and billing. Riding in someone else's car, staying in a spare room, five minutes of advice: all of it used to sit outside monetization. What technology changed is not human desire but the resolution of observation.

And this rise has not yet reached its endpoint. As Ochiai has it, the twenty-first-century computer does not stay inside the screen: it rewrites reality itself and dissolves into the environment until it can no longer be seen.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> Measurement moves toward the body as this proceeds. From the screen to the wristwatch, from the wristwatch to the smart contact lens, and then to the brain-machine interface. The point of observation moves from the transaction to the body, and from the body to the nervous system.

## 7.7 The day the proxy is no longer needed

Here two lines cross. Smith put exchange value in place because use value could not be measured. The side of the bit has spent two hundred years raising the resolution of observation. If the fact itself, that a beneficiary felt a benefit, becomes recordable, the reason for keeping a proxy disappears.

Suppose I talk with someone and get something deep out of it. Under the present institution that value vanishes between the two of us. But if heart rate, facial expression, and in time brain activity, record that I received a benefit, a claim can arise on the side that gave the value. Call it the right to three visits to a café. Without passing through money, the claim goes from the person who received the value to the person who gave it. The exchange SDL described from the beginning, competence put at one another's disposal, gets implemented as described for the first time.

The same measurement technology has an application on the political side. In *Democracy for the 22nd Century*, Yusuke Narita set out aggregating preferences from unconscious data, from words and expressions and reactions down to the level of hormone secretion, in place of the vote.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> Democracy replaces the explicit vote with implicit data; the economy replaces the explicit price with implicit value data.

And the two directions of Chapter 2 both reach their terminus here. As the convergence cost of arranging matter goes to zero, price loses the thing it rations. As the convergence cost of predictive models goes to zero, price loses its reason to stand proxy. Price loses both of its jobs at once. That is the sense in which the retirement of money is not a metaphor.

The entrance, though, is visible from the side of energy rather than the side of data. Tokens and carbon emission allowances were both designed as units of account and are both converging on currency. For an AI company constrained by energy, the token is a unit of revenue and at the same time a unit of the scarce resource that has to be rationed. Through the decade in which the supply of compute is held down by connection to the grid, this scarcity is real (Chapter 5). Anything issued, exchanged and stored as a unit of a scarce resource is performing the function of currency at that point. A world in which the quantity of energy allowed per head is the unit arrives before a world that measures value in data.

The claim of this thesis is therefore limited to the following. Price retires not only because scarcity disappears. It retires also because use value becomes observable and the reason for keeping a proxy is gone. The first is a problem of energy, and a problem of decades. The second is a problem of measurement and of trust, and it is probably the harder of the two.

## 7.8 Projects that form and dissolve replace the firm

Once prediction is nearly free, the bits side stops needing the firm as a unit. What appears instead is co-creation raised for the occasion: a vision is set, individuals holding the skills it needs gather, they build for a few months, and they disperse.

A firm is a boundary drawn where searching for people, contracting with them and monitoring them costs less inside than it does through the market. Searching, matching, contracting and monitoring are all problems of prediction. In the language of Chapter 2, the further C_pred falls, the thinner the reason to draw the boundary at all. The boundary dissolves, though, only where the resources to be integrated are information. C_config stays bound to physics, so reactors, fabs and grids keep the shape of a firm.

What remains is how a contribution is recorded. Equity, as a residual claim, cannot form around a group that dissolves in a few months. An enterprise cannot create value on its own in any case. What it can do is offer a value proposition; value arises when the beneficiary integrates it with resources of their own.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> The claim attaches not to the organization but to the use value of the thing made. If the fact that a beneficiary felt a benefit can itself be recorded, so can who gave how much of it. Investing becomes the purchase of a claim on use value that has not yet arrived, rather than the purchase of a share in a company. So this form does not arrive until measurement is cheap.

## 7.9 Diligence begins outside compulsion

Step down from nine to five, and from being made to work for a wage, and what a human being does is not rest but the creation of value for someone.

Grow up inside task-based distribution and automation can only be spoken of as having one's work taken away. If distribution is guaranteed by another arrangement (Chapter 6), what automation hands over is not leisure but the state of being able to choose: what to make, for whom, and how far to take it.

In 1930 Keynes predicted that within a hundred years a fifteen-hour week would be enough.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-18">18</a></sup> The prophecy about wealth came true and the one about hours did not. Redesign distribution and it still does not come to fifteen hours. That automation does not reduce what people do has two centuries of precedent. Lusch and Vargo set out how much of innovation has consisted of moving acts people used to perform for themselves onto appliances: knowing when to change gear moved to the automatic transmission, knowing how a word is spelled moved to the spelling checker, judging how much water a load needs moved to a sensor in the washing machine.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup> Each time something moved, what people do did not shrink. The further AI and physical AI run construction on their own, the more value creation passing through human hands is called for around them. What changes is not the quantity of activity but whether the activity is compelled, and whether it appears in the ledger.

People then move on mission and on curiosity. Each holds a question of their own, as a founder does, and pushes it to the scale of an undertaking. What arrives is not idleness but diligence, standing for the first time outside compulsion. SDL's authors propose the same inversion in their final chapter: entrepreneurial activity is the basic form of value creation, and management inside an established market is the special case.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup>

## 7.10 There is a second gate on births

This thesis has treated the fall in births as a price phenomenon: so long as the expected cost per child keeps rising, no birth-rate policy works. But that is only one of the two gates. The second gate is what human time is being used for.

Having a child now competes with the side of a career that gets monetized. What competes is not only time. The measure of who one is has itself been placed inside the line. On that ruler, having a child is always a deduction. When someone decides to work through the weekend and gives up a trip with a grandchild, there is no way to convert what was given up into a sum of money. Outside the line there is no unit to begin with.

Retire the ruler and the deduction disappears. Not because prices fall but because what is being measured changes. Distribution opens the gate on the income side; the redefinition of value opens the gate on the side of meaning. What starts the equation of Chapter 4 running backwards is not disposable income alone.

## Objections and Limits
### Four reservations about the argument that use value becomes measurable (7.5 to 7.7)

First, service-dominant logic does not support this conclusion. The original work has a side to it that defends money. Money is a device for learning relative exchange values quickly on a common scale, and without exchange an actor has little information on exchange value at all, as chapter 4 of the book states. Whether direct measurement by data can replace that learning function depends on whether measurement is cheap and, just as much, on whether it is trusted.

Second, the current state of the technology is a long way off. Invasive BMIs are at the clinical-trial stage, and what has reached practical use non-invasively is at the level of reading fragments of intention from muscle potentials. Smart contact lenses are at the prototype stage. And the quantification of subjective experience such as pleasure and satisfaction still depends, for now, on proxies of its own: dopamine response, electrodermal response.

Third, a problem of principle remains there. If we depend on proxies, we may simply have replaced one proxy, price, with another, biometric data. And what the fourth axiom says is that an external party cannot assess the beneficiary's value. Brain activity measured from outside is precisely an assessment from outside. This argument holds only where measurement stops standing proxy and records the person's own phenomenological determination itself. Including the inverted qualia problem, this is a question that remains on the side of philosophy rather than engineering.

Fourth, what can be measured gets manufactured. An institution that pays for measured pleasure simultaneously creates the incentive to manufacture measured pleasure. Price was a coarse measure, but it was hard to fake at scale. There is no guarantee that biometric data has the same property. Being that much easier to handle, it may be that much easier to manipulate.

### Where SDL ends and this thesis begins

This chapter uses SDL's axioms and vocabulary as a foundation. That the line is drawn by who performs an act rather than by the act (7.3), that the producer-consumer division has no explanatory power, that money is a claim on future competence: all of this is SDL's claim and not a contribution of this thesis.

There are three things SDL does not say. First, it does not support the retirement of money (the first reservation above). Second, it does not claim that the weight of the uncounted side will rise. SDL's claim is that the classification is wrong, not a forecast that the outside of the classification grows in size. The argument about weight in 7.1 and 7.4 belongs to this thesis. Third, SDL does not treat the lifting of the constraint, the collapse in energy prices, or universal capital ownership. Every step from the axioms to the conclusions of this thesis is this thesis's own. Blur that distinction and an established theory looks as though it were guaranteeing an unestablished claim.

### The limits of GDP are not in themselves a new point (7.4)

The observation about the uncounted economy was formally set out by the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission in 2009,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> and the satellite accounts for household production have a series running back to 1965.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> Go further back and the critique of the classification itself was written by Bastiat in 1848.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> What is new here is not the limits of measurement themselves but the direction: that those limits may be lifted by technology. The first half is an introduction to existing knowledge, not a contribution of this thesis.
