## 7.1 Smith's fork

There was never a line between economic activity and everything else.

To create value is to apply one's own competence for the benefit of another.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup> Under that definition, serving coffee in a cafe and a mother loving her child are the same act. The only difference is that one of them carried a price. The side that carried one was called the economy, sorted into industries, counted in statistics and added up into GDP. The side that did not was called nothing at all.

So treat the coffee as a production unit and pay seven dollars for it: that reading is the one to drop. What is happening is that I receive a benefit from what the person behind the counter does, and that person, with the wage they are paid, receives a benefit in the same way from someone else's work in their own hours. Each is putting their competence at the other's disposal, and money is only the vehicle. The story in which a production unit gets replaced by AI comes from mistaking the vehicle for the substance.

What AI accelerates is the part of value creation a machine can take over: making the coffee, moving the freight, drawing the plans, writing the code. The range of human action, that part included, is far wider. So what happens is that the counted part shrinks against the whole and the weight of the part that was never counted rises. Human beings are not switching to something else. What changes is what gets counted.

Adam Smith drew the line. The distinction itself, though, was not his invention. Aristotle separated use-value from exchange-value in the fourth century BCE, and the medieval Schoolmen explicitly held that use-value came first, on the grounds that exchange is motivated by human need.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup> What Smith did was not to distinguish but to choose.

In Book I, Chapter IV of *The Wealth of Nations*, Smith notes the two senses of the word value.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup> Use-value, which expresses the utility of some object, and exchange-value, which expresses the power of purchasing other goods that possession of it conveys. Then he wrote the famous paradox. Nothing is more useful than water, and almost nothing can be bought with it. A diamond has scarcely any use-value, and it will exchange for a vast quantity of other goods. Immediately after setting the paradox down, Smith makes a quiet and decisive choice. He confines everything that follows to exchange-value.

The reason was practical, not metaphysical. His concern was not economics in general but how England, at the start of the industrial revolution, could grow wealthy through international trade. For that purpose exchange-value was the easier of the two to understand and it gave a standardized measure. And it was use-value that he called real value. The real value of a thing is the toil and trouble of acquiring it, because the necessities, conveniences and amusements of life can only be drawn from the labor of others, and the only thing anyone has to offer in return is their own labor. Having defined real value that way, Smith moved to the measurable one, nominal value, the price paid in the market. He did so knowing that value is in fact settled inside the experience of the person who receives it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

His distinction between productive and unproductive is misquoted almost as often. Smith did not say that services have no value. The criterion was whether the result could be carried forward and exchanged again. A barber shaves and cuts a person's hair. However valuable that act, the person who received it cannot put it into another exchange. Receive a tangible good and it can be sent on into the next exchange. So the view grew up that a good comes with transportable, transferable value embedded in it.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup> Smith called productive those activities that contribute to producing and distributing exportable tangible goods, and for the purpose of explaining how to increase national wealth it is the right line. The line was not wrong. Later generations took a distinction drawn for that purpose and began using it as the measure of what people are getting. The paradox of water and diamonds was not solved but shelved, and then the shelving was forgotten.

## 7.2 Undoing a 250-year detour

There is a body of theory that undoes that detour of two hundred and fifty years: Vargo and Lusch's service-dominant logic (SDL), whose axiomatization comes down to five statements.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

Axiom 1: applying competence for the benefit of another is the fundamental basis of exchange. Axiom 2: value is always co-created by multiple actors, including the beneficiary. Axiom 3: all social and economic actors are resource integrators, combining what they have on hand to bring value into being. Axiom 4: value is always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the beneficiary. Axiom 5: value co-creation is coordinated through actor-generated institutions and institutional arrangements.

Goods are a mechanism for delivering competence to the other party, a vehicle for value, and nothing more. Money and organizations stand between the buyer and the seller, and that is what hides the fact that the two are putting competence at each other's disposal. What a human being needs is not a good but value. We have never, from Day 1, been buying coffee. We feel, and pay for, the value that comes through the coffee, which is to say the use-value that arises in context. Every economy was an experience economy from the start.

So SDL has no division into producers and consumers either. The picture in which one party creates value and the other uses it up renders the economy as a cycle of making stuff, selling stuff and destroying stuff. But is education consumed? Are books? Is entertainment? An entrepreneur, a salesperson, a customer, the head of a household, a parent driving the carpool, a grocery shopper and a restaurant patron are not different kinds of actor. Quite often they are, literally, the same person.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup>

And the assessment of value is heterogeneous all the way down. One person finds value in flying from Dubai to New Haven for a dense intellectual conversation. Another finds it in going to a music festival. The moment you grant that value is settled inside the experience of the person receiving it (Axiom 4), both are equally rational. And then Axiom 5. What gathers those heterogeneous assessments and coordinates them is not the market price alone. It is language, custom, contract and standards, the arrangements actors make for themselves.

To take each person's own assessment as the input and assemble from it the experience that carries value for them, which is to say who they meet, where they go, what they read, when they rest: that is the role of AI in the world to come. AI becomes a delivery system for value.

## 7.3 The measurable side won

Smith's line was disputed from the moment it was drawn, long before SDL. It survived because only tangible goods, the ones that passed through a market and carried a price, could be counted.

Jean-Baptiste Say held that production is the creation of utility rather than a change in the form of matter.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> John Stuart Mill went further. No human being can produce one particle of matter, so what labor produces is not objects but utilities. Why then, Mill asked, should all labor that produces utility not be accounted productive<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>? The one who went furthest was Frédéric Bastiat.

> The great economic law is this: services are exchanged for services. It is trivial, very commonplace; it is nonetheless the beginning, the middle, and the end of economic science. Once this axiom is clearly understood, what becomes of such subtle distinctions as use-value and exchange-value, material products and immaterial products, productive classes and unproductive classes?<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>

That was written in 1848. Even so, Say and Mill both took issue with Smith's classification and then built their own arguments on top of it. Only what could be measured came to be called the economy. What is new is not the claim but that the means of measuring use-value directly is coming into reach for the first time.

The line is still drawn the same way. What decides whether an activity counts as goods production or as services is not what the activity consists of but who performs it, as the Economic Classification Policy Committee of the US Bureau of Economic Analysis says in its own words:

> One and the same activity, such as painting, may be classified as goods or service production depending purely on the organization of the overall process of production. If the painting is done by employees within the producer unit that makes the good, it will be treated as part of the goods production, whereas if it is done by an outside painting company, it will be classified as an intermediate input of services. Thus, when a service previously performed in a manufacturing establishment is contracted out to a specialized services firm, data will show an increase in services production in the economy even though the total activity of painting may be unchanged.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup>

The statistics produce, by the way they classify, the very facts they are supposed to be observing. The boundary between the acts the economy counts and the acts it does not is settled in the same place: not by what the act consists of, but by whether it can be measured.

## 7.4 The size of the blind spot

What GDP measures is not value but the monetized part of value. Of the value a person creates in a day, only a part of it carries a price. Full-time work is booked as economic activity in GDP. A conversation with a friend, worth fifty dollars to each of them, is not booked at all. Incorporate, though, and bill the very same conversation as consulting, and it enters GDP.

That reading is borne out by what has actually been measured. Three sets of figures, in order. First, household production. In the satellite accounts of the US Bureau of Economic Analysis, unpaid household production such as cooking, cleaning, childcare and elder care accounts for twenty to twenty-five percent of output once the accounts are extended to take it in. Restated as an addition to published GDP, around thirty percent.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> Value placed outside the national accounts for no reason other than that it does not pass through a market. Second, time. Averaging the OECD time use surveys over ages 25 to 64, paid work is only four and a half hours a day, against four hours of unpaid work and just under five hours of leisure.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup> More than half of human waking time is outside price from the start. Third, free digital goods. In the large-scale choice experiments of Brynjolfsson and colleagues, the median user's compensation for giving up Facebook for a month was about forty-eight dollars.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup> For a good with a price of zero and near-zero contribution to GDP. Their proposed GDP-B is an attempt to measure the benefit users get over and above what they pay, which is to say the consumer surplus that never gets counted. All three ways of measuring point the same direction. The value we create through the tasks we are employed to do is the minority of the value we create in a day.

This is not a bug in GDP. It is the specification, Smith's proxy institutionalized over two hundred and fifty years. As the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission formally acknowledged,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> we have measured what was easy to measure and called what we measured value.

## 7.5 Price's two jobs

Price does two jobs: it rations scarcity and it measures value. If the monetary institution is to retire, both have to go.

Money is not a natural object but an institution. Simmel drew it as a pure form of social relation, Graeber as a technology of credit and accounting that precedes coinage, Harari as a shared intersubjective fiction.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> SDL's vocabulary is blunter: money is a claim on the future, a right to have some other actor apply their competence on your behalf one day.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup> A balance is not value itself but an inventory of those rights. An institution that loses its function is retired. With two jobs, retirement takes two conditions.

The first condition has already been observed in information goods. Where the cost of making one more unit, the marginal cost, is close to zero, competition pushes price down to it, which is to say to zero, and price stops working as a signal for allocating resources.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup> As Chapter 5 showed, the collapse in the cost of energy and automation extends the same mechanics into the material world. When the marginal cost of goods and services goes asymptotically to zero, price as a rationing device loses the scarcity it was rationing.

The second condition is not settled by energy. As section 7.1 showed, Smith granted that use-value is the substance of value and then confined his inquiry to exchange-value. Price as an institution stands on one fact and no other, which is that at the time there was no way to observe use-value. So something observable was put in its place. SDL's fourth axiom states the same fact from the other side. Value is always uniquely and phenomenologically determined by the beneficiary. The original goes on: firms and government agencies may attempt to assess value, but they assess it from their own goals and their own context, and their assessment always diverges from the beneficiary's. Proxies such as economic efficiency and productivity can be measured, but actual value cannot be assessed.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup>

That premise, that it cannot be observed, is the one now breaking. Chapter 2 traced the history of rising fidelity in the reproduction of symbols. Language, writing, printing, the telegraph, the telephone, the internet. That sequence has a second effect that has never been counted. Each time fidelity rose, the range of economic activity that could be observed widened. The ledger made transactions observable, the telegraph made distant prices observable, and the smartphone made countless acts that had never carried a price into objects of measurement and billing. Riding in someone else's car, staying in a spare room, five minutes of advice: all of it used to sit outside monetization. What technology changed is not human desire but the resolution of observation.

And this rise has not yet reached its endpoint. As Ochiai has it, the twenty-first-century computer does not stay inside the screen: it rewrites reality itself and dissolves into the environment until it can no longer be seen.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-15">15</a></sup> The place measurement is taken moves closer to the body as this proceeds. From the screen to the wristwatch, from the wristwatch to the smart contact lens, and then to the brain-machine interface. The point of observation moves from the transaction to the body, and from the body to the nervous system.

## 7.6 The retirement of money

If the very fact that a beneficiary felt a benefit becomes recordable, the reason for keeping a proxy disappears.

Suppose I talk with someone and get something deep out of it. Under the present institution that value vanishes between the two of us. But if heart rate, facial expression, and in time brain activity, record that I received a benefit, a claim can arise on the side that gave the value. Call it the right to three visits to a café. Without passing through money, the claim goes from the person who received the value to the person who gave it. The exchange SDL set out from the beginning, competence put at one another's disposal, gets implemented that way for the first time. Take measurement as far as dissolving into the environment and the handover disappears from view as well. With AI assembling what is offered, the receiver's own body recording it, and the claim moving on the spot, there is nowhere left for either person to notice that anything was paid. Put a price on pleasure, though, and someone will manufacture pleasure. What that would actually look like, I cannot yet picture. The same measurement technology runs toward politics as well. In *Democracy for the 22nd Century*, Yusuke Narita set out aggregating preferences from unconscious data, from words and expressions down to the level of hormone secretion, in place of the vote.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-16">16</a></sup> Democracy replacing the explicit vote with implicit data, and the economy replacing the explicit price with implicit value data, are the same substitution.

And the two costs set out in Chapter 2 both go to zero here. As the convergence cost of arranging matter goes to zero, price loses the thing it rations. As the convergence cost of predictive models goes to zero, price loses its reason to stand proxy. That is the sense in which the retirement of money is not a metaphor. The two do not arrive at the same speed, though. The rationing job goes for reasons of energy, on a scale of decades. The proxy job goes for reasons of measurement and of trust, and that is probably the harder of the two.

The place where money first gives way is not the side that measures value in data but the side of energy. The token that counts AI inference, and the carbon emission allowance. Both were designed as units of account, and both are converging on currency. For an AI company constrained by energy, the token is a unit of revenue and at the same time a unit of the scarce resource that has to be rationed. As Chapter 5 showed, the supply of compute is held down by connection to the power grid for a decade. Through that decade the scarcity is real. Anything issued, exchanged and stored as a unit of a scarce resource is performing the function of currency at that point. A world in which the quantity of energy allowed per head is the unit arrives before a world that measures value in data.

## 7.7 Machines work, people create

The further mechanization goes, the more conscious people become of their own value creation, and the more willing. Count a day by what was of use to someone rather than by what carried a price, and the size of what you make comes into view. It runs far past the frame of a full-time job, and far past the frame of GDP.

What has kept it out of view is the way employment counts. Hold a full-time post and sell your hours, and only the hours you sold are recorded as value you created. That shape is optimized for an institution that measures exchange-value alone. So is the firm. A firm is a boundary drawn where searching for people, contracting with them and monitoring them costs less inside than it does through the market. Searching, matching, contracting and monitoring are all problems of prediction. In the language of Chapter 2, the further the convergence cost of predictive models, C_pred, falls, the thinner the reason to draw the boundary. It thins, though, only where the resources to be integrated are information. The convergence cost of arranging matter, C_config, stays bound to physics, so reactors, fabs and grids keep the shape of a firm.

What appears on the information side instead is co-creation raised for the occasion: a vision is set, the people holding the skills it needs gather, they build for a few months, and they disperse. Equity cannot form around a group that dissolves in a few months, because a share is a right to keep receiving whatever is left over for as long as the business runs. An enterprise cannot create value on its own in any case. What it can do is offer a value proposition; value arises when the beneficiary integrates it with resources of their own.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup> The claim attaches not to the organization but to the use-value of the thing made. Investing becomes the purchase of a claim on use-value that has not yet arrived, rather than the purchase of a share in a company. So this form does not arrive until measurement is cheap.

What automation takes away is not the work but the compulsion. Grow up inside an arrangement where income is set by the quantity of tasks you get through, and automation can only be spoken of as having one's work taken away. If distribution is guaranteed by a stake in capital rather than by tasks, what automation hands over is not leisure but the state of being able to choose: what to make, for whom, and how far to take it. Step down from nine to five, and from being made to work for a wage, and what a human being does is not rest but the creation of value for someone.

And automation does not reduce what people do. There are two centuries of precedent for that. In 1930 Keynes predicted that within a hundred years a fifteen-hour week would be enough.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-18">18</a></sup> The prophecy about wealth came true and the one about hours did not. Redesign distribution and it still does not come to fifteen hours. Lusch and Vargo set out how much of innovation has consisted of moving acts people used to perform for themselves onto appliances: knowing when to change gear moved to the automatic transmission, knowing how a word is spelled moved to the spelling checker, judging how much water a load needs moved to a sensor in the washing machine.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup> Each time something moved, what people do did not shrink. The further AI and physical AI run construction on their own, the more work accumulates around them deciding what to build, who to build it for, and whether what got built was of any use. What changes is not the quantity of activity but whether the activity is compelled, and whether it appears in the ledger.

People then move on mission and on curiosity. Each holds a question of their own, as a founder does, and pushes it to the scale of an undertaking. SDL's authors propose the same inversion in their final chapter: entrepreneurial activity is the basic form of value creation, and management inside an established market is the special case.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-17">17</a></sup>

The ratio of section 7.4, monetized value against unmonetized, turns over here. The side that was counted shrinks against the whole, and the side that was never counted gets counted for the first time, as something the person made. What arrives is not idleness but diligence, standing for the first time outside compulsion.

## Objections and Limits
### Four reservations about the argument that use-value becomes measurable (7.5 and 7.6)

First, service-dominant logic does not support this conclusion. The original work has a side to it that defends money. Money is a device for learning relative exchange-values quickly on a common scale, and without exchange an actor has little information on exchange-value at all, as chapter 4 of the book states. Whether direct measurement by data can replace that learning function depends on whether measurement is cheap and, just as much, on whether it is trusted.

Second, the current state of the technology is a long way off. Invasive brain-machine interfaces are at the clinical-trial stage, and what has reached practical use non-invasively is at the level of reading fragments of intention from muscle potentials. Smart contact lenses are at the prototype stage. And the quantification of subjective experience such as pleasure and satisfaction still depends, for now, on proxies of its own: dopamine response, electrodermal response.

Third, a problem of principle remains there. If we depend on proxies, we may simply have replaced one proxy, price, with another, biometric data. And what the fourth axiom says is that an external party cannot assess the beneficiary's value. Brain activity measured from outside is precisely an assessment from outside. This argument holds only where measurement stops standing proxy and records the determination that happens inside the person's own experience. Including the inverted qualia problem, the possibility that the same stimulus is felt differently by different people, this is a question that remains on the side of philosophy rather than engineering.

Fourth, what can be measured gets manufactured. An institution that pays for measured pleasure simultaneously creates the incentive to manufacture measured pleasure. Price was a coarse measure, but it was hard to fake at scale. There is no guarantee that biometric data has the same property. Being that much easier to handle, it may be that much easier to manipulate. Just how measured pleasure would be manufactured, though, I cannot yet picture. If anyone can, I would like to hear it.

### Where SDL ends and this thesis begins

This chapter uses SDL's axioms and vocabulary as a foundation. That the line is drawn by who performs an act rather than by what the act consists of (7.3), that the producer-consumer division has no explanatory power, that money is a claim on future competence: all of this is SDL's claim and not a contribution of this thesis.

There are three things SDL does not say. First, it does not support the retirement of money (the first reservation above). Second, it does not claim that the weight of the uncounted side will rise. SDL's claim is that the classification is wrong, not a forecast that the outside of the classification grows in size. The argument about weight in 7.1 and 7.4 belongs to this thesis. Third, SDL does not treat the lifting of the constraint, the collapse in energy prices, or universal capital ownership. Every step from the axioms to the conclusions of this thesis is this thesis's own. Blur that distinction and an established theory looks as though it were guaranteeing an unestablished claim.

### The limits of GDP are not in themselves a new point (7.4)

The observation about the uncounted economy was formally set out by the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission in 2009,<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup> and the satellite accounts for household production have a series running back to 1965.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup> Go further back and the critique of the classification itself was written by Bastiat in 1848.<sup class="noteref"><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup> What is new here is not the limits of measurement themselves but the direction: that those limits may be lifted by technology. The first half is an introduction to existing knowledge, not a contribution of this thesis.
